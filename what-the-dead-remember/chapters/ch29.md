## Chapter 29: Interlude: The Recording

The Works did not sleep, exactly; it had merely stopped breathing some decades ago and nobody had come to certify it. Sidious liked that about the place. He stood in the long room above the dead foundry with his hood up and his hands folded in his sleeves and listened to the building tick as the night cooled it, and waited for his apprentice to finish saying what he had come to say.

"Fett delivered it to me in person," Dooku said. "On Serenno. He would not send it."

"A careful man."

"A man who wishes to be paid in person." Dooku set the holoprojector on the table between them and did not sit. He had been standing since he arrived, which was a tell; the Count sat when he was certain of a room. "There is a message attached. I have not edited it."

"I would know if you had."

"Yes," Dooku said. "You would."

Sidious let the small silence that followed go on for exactly as long as it pleased him, and then inclined his head, and Dooku touched the projector.

Jango Fett resolved above the table at a quarter of his height, helmet on, standing in what might have been a ship's hold. He did not introduce himself. He said that the job had been done as specified: the Jedi isolated, taken, held, the droids run as the client's droids were meant to be run, the hostages kept alive. He said that the Jedi had been shot in the tower's defence and that he had not fired the shot, which was in the recording, and that if the client had wanted the man dead the client should have said so. He said that a second Jedi had arrived without appearing in the contract and that the price was therefore revised, and he named a figure, flatly, the way a man names the weather. Then he paused, and Sidious, who had listened to a great many men decide whether to say a thing, watched him decide.

"I have fought Jedi," Fett said. "I have killed one. I know what they do and how long it takes them to do it." The helmet did not move. "That was not one. You want to know what it was, you watch it yourself. I'm not getting any closer to it than I did."

The image cut. There was nothing about a son. There was nothing about the Mandalorian's own retreat, which Sidious already knew was in the file, because Dooku's silence about it had been so very complete.

"The revised figure," Sidious said.

"I have paid it."

"Good. Play the rest."

* * *

The helmet saw what the man saw, and the man had been looking at the door.

The tower room on Oba Diah was round and poorly lit, with drains in the floor and a gantry two storeys up where the Pykes kept their cargo, and the recording held it at the height of a tall man's eyes, swinging a little as Fett moved. Sidious watched the Jedi fall from the gantry, which Dooku had already told him of; watched the Pykes go to the body; watched the Mandalorian step back against the wall, pistol low, the way a man steps back from a thing that is not yet finished. Fett's breathing was audible in the helmet. It was slow. He had been bored.

Then the door.

Sidious had seen the boy perhaps forty times in six years, across a desk, in a garden, once in the undercity with his twelve-year-old heart in his hands. He knew the way the boy came into a room looking for the exit, which was the slave in him, and stood too close to his master, which was the orphan. He had made a study of it.

The thing that came through the door on Oba Diah did not look for the exit. It did not look at the Pykes, or the droids, or the Mandalorian with the pistol. It looked at the floor where the Jedi lay, once, and then it did not look at anything at all, and the first droid was in two pieces before the second had finished turning.

Sidious leant forward very slightly, which he did not often do.

The stroke was overhand. One hand. The blade came down the way a tree comes down, without flourish, without the little rising preparation every Jedi in the galaxy had been taught at nine years old; it simply arrived, and was through, and was somewhere else. A second droid. A third. The boy's off hand did not touch the hilt at any point. He walked through them as though the room were a corridor and they were a narrowing of it. Fett's breath had stopped being slow.

A Pyke fired and the shot went back into him and he folded. The boy did not break stride. He did not look at the man.

He did not look at any of them. That was the thing. Jedi killed with their faces full of regret, Mandalorians with their faces full of arithmetic, his own kind with pleasure, which was honest. The boy killed the way one closes a door behind oneself. The dead were behind him before they had finished falling. He was only ever looking at the next thing.

Then the second Pyke, the tall one, who had got to the Jedi's body with a blade and had the sense to want a hostage.

The boy stopped. He raised his left hand — the hand without the saber, open, the fingers not quite closed — and the Pyke went up.

Up. Off the floor. By the throat, with four metres of nothing between the boy's fingers and the Pyke's neck, and his boots kicking at the air, and the blade clattering down out of a hand that had forgotten it. The boy held him there. He did not squeeze theatrically; there was no fist. He simply held him, with the thumb and two fingers a little apart, the way a man holds a cup, and the Pyke's face went from grey to a darker grey and his heels stopped kicking, and the boy opened his hand and let the body drop and had already turned away before it landed.

Sidious said, "Stop."

Dooku stopped it. The image hung above the table: a boy of fifteen with a Padawan braid behind his right ear and a face still soft at the jaw, standing with one arm out in a dead man's tower, and the Pyke's body not yet on the floor.

Sidious looked at the hand.

He knew what that was. Not in the way the Jedi would know it, if they ever saw this — a Force choke, dark side, a tutting in the Council Chamber. He knew it the way a man knows his own handwriting. The open hand instead of the fist. The thumb apart. The holding, not the crushing, because a crushed throat ends the lesson and a held one teaches it. He had done it himself, in a corridor on Naboo to a Neimoidian who had needed teaching, and a thousand times in the mind, where it was most useful. It was his. The grammar of it was his. He had taught it to no one. Not to the one who had come before, who had been a blade and had no patience for the slow hand; not to the man standing beside him now, who found it vulgar and preferred the saber for the same reason he preferred the harpsichord.

"Go on," he said.

The boy went to the Jedi. He knelt. The helmet lost him for a moment behind the gantry's stanchion and then found him again, lifting the man — the one-armed lift, the weight across the shoulders, the boy's knees shaking under it because he was fifteen and the man was not small — and carrying him toward the door with the saber still lit in his free hand and his back to the Mandalorian. His back. To a Mandalorian with a pistol. He did not look round once. He had not spared Fett so much as a glance in the whole of it, and Sidious understood, watching the helmet's view drop as Fett lowered his own head, that the man had understood that perfectly well, and that it was why he had let him go.

The recording ran on through an empty room and a lift and a landing pad and a courier's engines, and Sidious let it, because Dooku was standing very still beside him and he wished to see how long the Count could stand still.

* * *

"Well," Sidious said at last, "that was not a Jedi."

"No."

"Nor a boy."

"He is fifteen," Dooku said. "The braid is real. Kenobi's apprentice. I made certain of it at the reception. I touched the mind, as you know, and I told you what I found."

"You told me a wall. You told me Sith-built."

"I told you what it was."

"You did." Sidious turned, and looked at him properly for the first time since the recording had begun, and let him feel it. "And now you have told me what he does with his hands. Which is also Sith-built, my friend, and not by me."

Dooku did not look away. That was to his credit; the Count had many things to his credit, which was why he was standing in this room and not lying in the foundry below it. He was proud and cultured and believed in his own reasons, and a man who believes in his own reasons can be led by them further than a man who merely obeys. Sidious believed him. Mostly. He believed that Dooku had never met the boy before the Dome Gallery, because Dooku had been angry about it afterward in a way that did not lie, and that the boy's hatred had unsettled him so deeply he had failed to hide it in a crowded room. He believed that Dooku had not taught a fifteen-year-old to hold a man's throat open-handed. He believed it to perhaps nine parts in ten.

The tenth part he kept, the way one keeps a knife in a drawer one never opens. It was pleasant to have it there. He had not had a tenth part about anyone in years.

"There are two of us," he said. "There have been two of us for a thousand years, and there are two of us now, and one of them is you. Someone has made this boy an instrument. Not a Jedi's instrument; they do not teach that stroke, and if that child used it in front of Drallig the old man would still be sitting down. A Sith's instrument. He has been taught by someone who learned what I learned."

"I did not—"

"I know you did not." Gently. A hand on the shoulder, without the hand. "I am thinking aloud, Tyranus. Indulge me."

Dooku inclined his head, and Sidious watched him do it, and went on.

"The first possibility is you. I have set it aside. The second is that the boy has taught himself, from a holocron, from a book, from some scrap the Order forgot to burn; and that is a thing I would like to believe, because it would mean he is mine already and does not know it. But one does not teach oneself to hold a throat *like that*. One is shown. The third possibility—"

He stopped.

He did not stop because he was uncertain. He stopped because the third possibility had a shape, and the shape was a shaft on Naboo with a boy of twenty-five standing at the top of it, and a red blade going out, and a thing falling in two pieces into the dark, and the dark not being, after all, very deep. He had felt it the way one feels a tooth go, from the Senate Rotunda, and he had not gone to look, because the Chancellorship was being handed to him across a floor full of applause, and because one does not go to look. He had not thought about the shaft in a long time. He found he did not wish to finish thinking about it now.

"The third possibility," he said, "I shall keep for myself."

Dooku said nothing. He was wise.

"What matters," Sidious said, "is not who. It is when. I had allowed ten years." He walked to the window, which looked out on the black backs of factories and the single orange light of a maintenance drone going about a job nobody had told it to stop. "Ten years was a sound plan. The boy was to be knighted in a war. The war was to be mine from both ends. His master was to die in it, well, somewhere I could be seen to grieve. The boy was to come to me grieving. One builds a thing like that slowly, Tyranus, the way one builds a Senate majority, a vote at a time, and I have been very happy building it." He turned. "And someone has walked into my garden and done in a season what I had allowed a decade for, and done it *better*, and left me the account."

"Then the plan—"

"The plan is now."

He said it quietly. He had never raised his voice in his life and did not propose to begin over a child.

"The boy is to be taken this year. Or broken this year; I shall have him one way or the other, and I confess I am no longer certain which I would prefer. The master is to be removed in a manner that does not make a martyr of him; you will think about that, and you will think about the Mandalorian, who has seen the boy's back and knows what it cost him not to shoot it, and who will therefore want more money or a long way away, and may have both. The crisis I had meant for the end of my term is to be brought forward. All of it. The Rim, the Federation, the army, the vote. I have been patient for six years. I find I have an appetite."

* * *

Dawn came up over the Senate District the colour of weak tea, and the Chancellor's office was already lit, because the Chancellor was already in it.

He had the recording on the private display set into his desk, the image frozen at the hand, and he looked at it between items of business the way another man might look at a view.

The business was considerable and he did not hurry it. Sly Moore stood at his shoulder with her pad and her pale, patient face and took down what he gave her in the order he gave it; he liked to let the day arrange itself and see what it chose to put first.

The Rim first, then. Dooku's Serenno printers had done good work all season: pamphlets in four scripts on the Rim's tariff burden and the Core's indifference, circulated through planetary councils that had never before been told they were owed something. Three worlds had passed resolutions. One had recalled its senator. It had been meant to take two more years to ripen. It would have to ripen faster. He told Moore to have the Trade Federation's Senate liaison to lunch — the new one, the Neimoidian with the grievance — and to let it be understood that the Chancellor's office would not stand in the way of a revised tariff measure on Rim bulk freight, provided it was proposed by someone else. Verrane of Tyrrhus would do. Verrane had asked for Kenobi by name and found it exhilarating, and a man who has been useful once will usually be useful twice to see whether the feeling lasts.

The committee next. Senator Amidala's Rim franchise bill — disclosure of Syndicate holdings in Rim concessions and a tribunal to hear them — was in its second reading in committee, and the committee had, after Oba Diah, grown unaccountably fond of it. Eleven surveyors had been freed by a Jedi and the committee wished to take evidence on Syndicate conduct and had written to the Temple to ask after Padawan Skywalker's health. He had read the letter. He had enjoyed it. He told Moore that the Chancellor was gravely concerned that the tribunal clause overreached the Senate's jurisdiction and would be grateful to see it referred for study, and that the disclosure provisions might be narrowed, in the interest of their passage, to holdings above a threshold that no Syndicate had ever been foolish enough to exceed. Organa would object, in a voice like a good bell, and be listened to, and lose; it was a pattern worth a Chancellor's protecting. Mothma would second him and count the votes and be right about the count, which was the dangerous thing in her, and could wait.

Amidala he thought about a moment longer. He had a list. It was not written anywhere and it was not, as yet, a list of anything but nuisances, the way a gardener keeps a list of what has seeded itself in the wrong bed. Her name had been on it since she was fourteen and had stood up in his Rotunda and called a vote of no confidence against the man she had been sent to beg from, which had been, he still thought, the most useful afternoon of his life. She wrote to the boy. He knew that; he knew that Kenobi had kept one letter back. He moved her name, in his mind, a little higher up the list, not across the line into the other list, because that was a different instrument and one did not reach for it over a tribunal clause. Not yet. The line was a thing one crossed once.

The term. He did the arithmetic as he did every morning, and it came out as it always did: the Senate's calendar gave him something under two years; his own reckoning gave him as long as the Republic was frightened; the gap between those figures was the whole of his work. He would close it this year. One needed a war for that, and an army for the war, and the army was six years old and looked twelve and would be ready, the Kaminoans said, in four. They would have to be hurried. Everything would. He found he did not mind.

"One more," he said.

Moore waited.

"An invitation. On the residence card, not the office. To Padawan Anakin Skywalker, care of the Jedi Temple." He looked at the display. The boy's hand. The thumb apart. "The Chancellor would be honoured if Padawan Skywalker would join him for a private supper at 500 Republica on the evening of the twelfth, in recognition of his conduct on Oba Diah, of which the Chancellor has heard a great deal and wishes to hear more. The invitation extends to the Padawan alone, as his master is understood to be convalescent. Send a copy to the Council as a courtesy; address it to Master Windu, who will dislike it, and copy Master Yoda, who will say nothing. Make the hour late enough that he will be hungry."

"Chancellor," Moore said, which was how she said yes.

She went out. The door closed behind her with the soft, expensive sound doors made on this level, and the morning went on arriving across the window, and the Temple sat in the middle of it on the far side of the city with its five spires taking the light.

He looked at the frozen image for a while longer. Fifteen. Soft at the jaw. A Padawan's braid, a little crooked; nobody had fixed it for him that morning. One arm out. A man dying in the air at the end of it, and the boy already looking at the next thing, the way one does, the way he himself had looked, at that age, at a corridor with a dead man in it and the door at the far end.

He had never seen that style in his life. He knew every line of it.

"Who taught you that, my boy?" he said, almost fondly, to the hand.

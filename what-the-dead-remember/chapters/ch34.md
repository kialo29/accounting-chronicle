## Chapter 34: What the Dead Remember

Ahsoka Tano sat on the eastern parapet with her boots hanging over four hundred storeys of nothing and the convor an arm's length to her left, and watched the sky decide what colour it was going to be.

It had not decided. Over the Senate dome it was the grey of old flimsi, and over the Works, where the light came from, it was going the yellow-brown it went every morning before the lanes filled, and between the two the air held still the way it did only at this hour, when the Temple was asleep under her and the city not yet awake. She had the roof to herself. She had had it to herself most mornings for seven years, which was a long time to keep a secret from a building, and the building had never once given her away.

The convor had not moved since she climbed up. It sat on the stone with its feathers tucked and its round yellow eyes half shut, looking at nothing, or at the same nothing she was looking at. She did not reach for it. She had learned on Shili, at three, in a body that could barely carry her to the edge of the village, that it did not like to be reached for, and that if she sat still enough for long enough it would come within reach on its own, and that this was the whole of what it offered and had to be enough.

It was enough. She was ten. She had been ten before and she had been thirty-three, and this morning she was both at once with no seam between them, which had happened only twice in seven years and both times on a roof. She could feel the one inside the other: the small strong body with its montrals barely past her brow and its lekku not yet to her collarbones, that had to look up at every adult in the Temple and most of the children; and inside it the woman who had stood in a temple with the floor going out from under her and a black mask looking down, and had said the thing she had said. The body was cold. She had not brought a cloak. The woman did not mind the cold, because the last time she had been warm it had meant fire.

The saber lay across her knees, unlit. It was a child's first saber, the hilt too fat for her fingers because Huyang built for the hand you would have and not the one you had, and she had wound the grip with cord from the *Crucible*'s stores to make it hold. Inside it the crystal was green. She had known it would be green before she went into the cave; she had stood in the dark on Ilum with her hand out and felt it answer and had not been surprised, and when it came up out of the ice into her palm it had been the colour of the convor's wing and she had closed her hand on it and said nothing. She had said nothing since. The others knew it was green because they had eyes. They did not know she had wanted it to be, that she had gone into the cave half afraid it would come up blue, that when it did not she had sat down on the ice with her forehead on her knees until Kessa Ruun found her and thought she was crying from the cold. It was a small thing. It was hers. There were not many of those, and somewhere in the fourth year she had decided to keep one.

She had decided other things since. Most of them on this roof, most of them before the light. She had decided, the night she felt him arrive like a struck bell, that she would find out, and she had. She had decided on Ilum, with his hand on the back of her neck and both of them shaking, that she would not cut the thread. She had decided on the waterfall ledge, with the three of them bent over a datapad and forty-one names and then more, that she would be the one who knew where the chips were, because the other two had never had a hand inside a clone's skull and she had. And she had decided, somewhere between then and now, what she was going to be this time, which was not what she had been. Not the one who left. Not the one who came back too late with two white blades and a plan. She would be the one who was there. In the room. Early. Small enough to be overlooked and old enough to use it. If that meant four more years in a sand-coloured tunic letting Tam Dorreth win at sabers, she would let him win. She had let worse things happen for worse reasons.

Rex was six. She thought it the way she thought it every morning, as a fact to be checked and put back. Six years old on a world of rain, in a body that looked twelve, in a line of boys with his face, with a thing behind his eye that had not been asked for and would be used. She had carried him out of a crashed Venator and buried the others under their own helmets and she had been thirty-three then, and she was ten, and he was six, and there was time. That was the whole of what the morning offered. There was time.

Plo would be awake by now. He rose before the clan did and sat in the small room off the Hawkbat dormitory with his mask off and the air of Dorin hissing into it, and if she came to the door he would put the mask on before he answered, so that she would not have to see him breathe. He had known for seven years that she was more than she showed and had never once asked her to show it. He would have to be told. Not today. Soon. She owed him that, and she owed him the thing she had said to him in the sling-weeks, that one day the Council would have to trust a child, and he had said he would remember she said it. She intended that he should.

The light came over the Works and found the dome and ran along it, and the convor opened its eyes.

She did not look at it. She looked at the hangar mouth, far down on the eastern face, where the courier shuttle would be out on the apron by now with a boy under it who was not a boy, and she thought the name she had for him and did not say it, not even here, not even to a bird. She had said it last on a floor that was falling. She would say it again when it was earned.

The convor turned its head, the whole of it, the way they did, and looked at her with both eyes. Then it settled its feathers and closed them again, within reach, and she let it be there, and did not reach.

Obi-Wan Kenobi drank his tea standing, which he did not usually do, at the window of the common room, which he did, and watched the morning take the city one tower at a time.

The cup was the chipped one. He had two and this was the one he preferred, for reasons he would not have been able to give the Council. The tea was the Alderaanian that Dex kept under the counter and overcharged him for. His left arm ached where the sling had been, in the dull, honest way of a thing healing properly, and he had stopped noticing it except at this hour, when he noticed everything. The Senate dome was gold. The Works beyond it were brown. Four hundred storeys down, the lanes were beginning to fill with the first of the day's traffic, the long slow freight barges that ran before the speeders, and he watched one of them go by at the level of the Temple's knees and thought, as he thought every morning and would not have admitted to, that it was a strange thing to have seen all this burn and then to see it, every morning, not burning.

The message had come in overnight on the Senate-liaison channel, which meant the Council's clerks had already read it, which meant it had been written to be read. It was from the office of the Senator for Naboo. It announced, in the Senator's own hand and the Senate's own tense, the formation of a select committee to take evidence on labour practices in the Outer Rim territories, with particular reference to contract indenture, the conduct of licensed syndicates, and the recent detention of a Republic survey team on Oba Diah; it noted that the Jedi Order had been party to the recovery of that team; and it asked, in the courteous way that left no room to decline without saying so, whether the Order might see fit to provide an adviser to sit with the committee during its evidence sessions. Then it said, below the seal, in a different voice: *I hope Padawan Skywalker is fully recovered. I have had no reply to my earlier enquiry and I do not take silence as an answer. P. A.*

He had read it three times. The third time he had smiled, which he had not meant to.

He put the cup down on the table, beside the datapad, and took up his own and began to answer. He answered as her channel, because that was what he was and because the clerks would read this one too. The Order was honoured to be asked. The request would be laid before the Council at its next sitting, and he was confident of its reception; the Council had, he wrote, an interest in the conduct of licensed syndicates that was more than academic, and he thought she would understand him. If an adviser were appointed he would see to it that the appointment did not depend on the convenience of any one office, which she would also understand. He did not say he would sit with the committee himself. He did not know whether he would be allowed to, and he had learned in four years to promise only what he could do alone.

Then he looked at the postscript for a while and wrote, under his own name and not the channel's: *The Padawan is in excellent health and has no account to share, which I take to be the ordinary condition of a boy of fifteen and not a comment on his correspondents. He is, this morning, inside a courier shuttle. I am assured this is a repair.* And after a moment, because she had been twenty once and he had not known her then, and because the thing he knew about her end was not hers to carry yet: *You were right to ask twice. Most people do not.*

He read it over. It was dry and it was careful and it said nothing that could be quoted in a committee or a Chancellor's office, and under it, where only she would look, it was warm. He sent it.

Anakin's datapad lay by the cup where the boy had left it last night, dark, the stylus across the flimsi notebook beside it, both face up. Inside the datapad was one file. He had watched it made. Forty-one names in his own hand and then more in two others, the three lists become one thing in plain Basic, no cipher, because the boy had said that if it was going to be found it should at least be read, and the girl had said nothing and added a name and then underlined it, alive. He held the key to the cipher. He did not need it. He did not open the file. He had read those names every night for four years, face down by day, and last night he had read them once more in company and found that in company they were lighter, not because there were fewer but because there were more hands. He would not read them this morning. This morning he would let the datapad be dark and the notebook be face up and the names be what they were, which was a list of work to be done, and not what they had been, which was a confession.

He drank the last of the tea cold.

He had tried again, in the night, when the boy was finally asleep in the next room and the bond had gone the thin even grey that meant no dreams. He had sat on the floor with his back to the wall and his hands open on his knees and reached, the way he had been taught in a desert by a voice that had not come until the third year, and he had reached further than he had in a long time, and there had been nothing. No voice. No sense of a presence withheld. Only the Temple's own quiet and the sound of a boy breathing through a wall. He had sat with it a while. Four years ago it had been the worst of all the silences; worse than the Council's, worse than the Chancellor's. This morning, standing at the window with the city not burning, he found that he did not mind it. If Qui-Gon was out there he would come when he came. If he was not, then the thing that had sent three of them back had done it without him, and that was its business. Obi-Wan had spent twenty years waiting to be told what to do by someone wiser. He had been told, eventually, and he had done it, and he had watched what it cost. He thought he might manage without, for a while. He had a Padawan and a committee and a girl on a roof and forty-one names in two other hands, and that was enough to be going on with.

The Chancellor's next audience was in four days. He had counted it the way he counted the tenth days, from the one before. He would walk the boy to the door of the red room and wait in the outer office under the eye of the Umbaran woman, and when the boy came out he would look at him once, as he always did, and the boy would say *fine*, as he always did, and they would go down in the lift together and say nothing in it, and it would have been one more audience that he had not managed to end and had not left the boy alone for. Forty-one times now. Forty-two. He would be there for the forty-second. It was not a plan. It was a habit, and he had learned on a ridge above a farm that a habit kept long enough became the thing itself.

He rinsed the cup and set it upside down to dry, and left the datapad where it was.

* * *

Anakin Skywalker had both hands inside the compensator housing of a courier shuttle up to the wrists, and the housing was cold, and his hands were not.

That was the thing he noticed. He noticed it last. He had noticed the breath first, the night he woke, and the cold second, and the water in the refresher third, and then, over seven months, in no order, everything else: the way a stair felt in the knees, the way a tunic hem caught on a belt, the way sweat ran, the way a flush came up the neck when Drallig looked at him, the way sleep took him under like a hand and let him go in the morning without asking leave. He had been startled by all of it. He was not startled now. He was a boy of fifteen lying on his back on a hangar floor with the shuttle's belly a hand's width above his face and grease on his forearms and the smell of coolant in his nose and his hands, his two hands, working a bolt loose by feel in the dark, and nothing hurt. Nothing in him hurt. That was all. He noted it the way he would have noted a reading on a gauge: nominal. Then he went back to the bolt.

The compensator had been drifting for a month. Knight Thane had logged it as a drift and done nothing about it because the courier had nowhere to be, and then the courier had had somewhere to be, which was Tatooine in ten days, and a drifting compensator over the Jundland would put the pilot's teeth through his tongue. So Anakin had asked. He had asked Thane at the hangar office with his braid behind his ear and his hands behind his back, the way a Padawan asked, and Thane had looked at him for a long moment with the face of a man who had written *repulsor test, on the log already* in his own hand over a theft he had not seen and could not now unsee, and had said, "Bay four. Don't touch anything else." Thane was in the office now with the door open and his back to the bay and had not turned round once. He was pretending, with the whole of his body, not to know anything. He was good at it. Anakin had known men who were worse.

"Number three," he said, to the dark under the shuttle.

A hand came in under the lip of the hull and put a number three driver into his palm. It was a small hand. It was already holding the number three before he spoke, because its owner had been watching him strip the housing for ten minutes and knew what came next, and was ten years old and could not possibly know what came next, and had arranged to be in bay four at this hour by telling the Hawkbat Clan master she was observing a mechanics practical for an assignment, which was true in the way that most things she said were true. She sat on an upturned crate by the landing strut in a sand-coloured tunic with her boots not reaching the floor and her lekku not reaching her collarbones and the youngling's blankness on her face that she could put on like a hood, and she handed him tools, and pretended not to be anyone.

"You're going to strip it," she said.

"I'm not going to strip it."

"You're turning it the wrong way."

"It's a left-hand thread."

"On a Kuati housing? They don't put left-hand threads on a Kuati housing. Anybody knows that."

"Anybody," Anakin said, and turned it the other way, and it came.

There was a pause under the shuttle, in which a boy's hands worked and a girl's heels knocked gently against a crate.

"Who taught you about compensators?" he said.

"Nobody. I'm ten."

"Right."

"I read a lot."

"Right." He got the housing plate off and slid it out and she took it from him without being asked and set it on the floor on a rag so the mating face stayed clean, which was what you did, and nobody taught a youngling that either. "Hand me the—"

The coupling tool was already in his palm.

He lay still a moment with it in his hand. Not because anything was wrong. Because it was so exactly the thing it was. There had been a war, once, with a fleet in it and a woman beside him with two blades who knew what he wanted before he did and told him when he was turning it the wrong way, and the war was not here and the fleet was not built and the woman was sitting on a crate with her feet not touching the floor, and the thing between them was here anyway, in a hangar, at the size of two children, with no name on it. It did not need one. He put the coupling tool to the drift bearing and felt it seat, and heard her hear it seat, and neither of them said anything, which was also a thing they had used to do.

Out at the hangar mouth, on the lip where the apron met the air, something green sat in the new sun. He saw it over the edge of the hull when he turned his head. It did not fly. It did not look at him. He looked at it for the length of a breath and went back to the bearing, and when he looked again it was still there, and he let it be.

What he was for, now. He had asked it on the first night with his face in a pillow and had not got an answer and had stopped asking around the third month, because the asking was itself the old thing, the wanting to be told. He was lying under a shuttle. He was fixing it so a letter could go to a woman on a farm who kept his hydrospanner on a hook so that he would come back for it. He was keeping, in the next week and the one after, a boy in the Hawkbat dormitory from waking with a stair and a gate and a circle of chairs in his head. He was walking into a red room in four days with a surface on his mind and a wall behind it, and walking out again. He was a Padawan with a braid and a master with a notebook and a youngling on a crate who was going to be in the room early this time, and none of that was a reason, and he had stopped needing one. He did not believe he deserved to be here. He had stopped needing to believe that too. He was here. The air went in and out of him on its own. He turned the bearing a quarter and it bit.

"Try it," he said.

She slid off the crate and went up the ramp with the boots of a ten-year-old girl loud on the metal and he heard the compensator spin up, whine, catch, and hold.

"Nominal," she said from inside, in a child's voice, and then, lower, where only the hull could hear it, "told you."

He lay on the floor with grease to the elbows and his two hands open on his chest, and breathed, and did not resolve anything, and was here.

In the Works, in a room with no window, Dooku stood before the boy's face and waited to be told what it meant.

It was very large. The projector threw it a metre high against the bare ferrocrete, blue-white, and the Mandalorian's helmet had caught it at the one instant that mattered: the gantry, the grey light, a Padawan of fifteen with his lightsaber lowered in his right hand and his left hand open and raised, and his face. It was the face that had stopped the recording. His master had run it through once at speed and once slow and then had stopped it here, on the boy, and had been looking at it for some time without speaking, and Dooku, who had seen that face across a reception hall and felt its hatred find him like a hand on the back of the neck, had been looking at the floor.

The face was not a boy's. That was all that could be said of it, and his master had not said it. It was not angry. There was no strain in it, no effort, none of the bared-teeth concentration of a child doing a thing beyond him. It was calm. It was the calm of a man who had done this before and had done it so often that there was no longer anything in it to feel. The eyes were a Padawan's eyes, blue, young; the thing behind them was not. Dooku had killed men. He had stood in rooms with his master while his master did things that he did not watch. He had not seen that calm on any face but one, and that one was standing beside him in the dark with its hands folded in its sleeves, and did not have it now.

What it had, Dooku could not read. He had never been able to. He had thought, six years ago, that it was contempt, and had been glad to serve it because contempt was at least a kind of attention. He knew better now. His master was looking at the boy's open hand the way a man looks at his own signature on a document he does not remember signing.

The room was cold. The Works were always cold; the heat had gone out of them a century ago with the foundries, and what was left was ferrocrete and rust and the long sound of wind in ducts. The old man did not feel it. Dooku did, in the joints of his hands, and did not move.

"Find out what happened to Maul," Sidious said.

Dooku did not look up at once. He let the words stand in the cold a moment, because he was a precise man and the words were not precise, and he had learned that when his master was imprecise it was never an accident. Maul. The Zabrak. The thing his master had made before him and lost on Naboo, cut in half by a Padawan of twenty-five on a reactor catwalk, fourteen years ago; a name his master had not spoken in his hearing in six years of service, and had spoken now, in front of this face, in the tone of a man asking for a ledger to be checked.

He was aware of being tested and did not know on what. He answered as he always answered, in the voice he used for the Senate and for his master, which were the same voice.

"Maul is dead, Master."

Sidious did not turn his head. He went on looking at the boy's hand, open, with the grey light of Oba Diah in the palm of it and three droids gone to pieces at the edge of the frame. His own hands stayed in his sleeves. When he spoke it was gently, almost kindly, the voice of a man who has waited a long time for something to be interesting and has at last been obliged.

"Then find out what else is."

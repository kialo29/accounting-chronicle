## Chapter 7: The Dream the Temple Heard

Anakin went in, in the end, because the corridor lamps had gone down to their night level and a Padawan sitting on a ledge in the dark was a thing somebody would eventually ask about.

The common room had been tidied after all. The supper things were gone from the table, a cup stood rinsed and upside down on the drainer, the reader lay face-down on the arm of the chair where Obi-Wan had put it when he gave up waiting, and the light under his door was out. Anakin stood in the middle of the room with the datapad flat against his ribs under his tunic and listened to the quarters the way he had once listened to the bridge of a ship, for the sound under the sound, and heard nothing but the air system and, a long way off, a transport going over.

He did not take the list out again. He went into his own room and shut the door and sat on the bed to get his boots off, and lay down on top of the blanket in his tunic, because getting under it would have been a decision to sleep and he had not made that decision.

He had not slept properly in ten days. He had learned in the first week how much sleep a fifteen-year-old body believed it was owed, and how loudly it complained when it was not paid, and he had been paying it in instalments: an hour here, two there, on his back with the shield held level in both hands like a tray of water, going down only as far as he could go and still hold the tray. He had done this before. In the suit, sleep had been a medical event, a thing that happened to the body on a schedule while the mind walked its corridors with the lights off, and he had got good at letting the body go and keeping the rest. He was good at it. He had also been forty-five and sealed inside a machine that did his breathing for him. Now he was fifteen and growing, and his shins ached at night, actually ached, the long bones complaining from the inside the way they had the first time he had been this age, and the body wanted the whole night and did not care what he was holding.

He lay and held it. The bond, thinned to a boy's sulk, a crack of light under a door. The surface, the fifteen-year-old's surface, speeders and grievances and Mirren Dask's hair. Under that the wall. Under the wall, himself.

He thought about the line on the list going uphill. He thought about *Kamino* in his own ugly cipher and *KENOBI, O.* beneath it and the two-year-old date, and the eleventh niche in the Archives with its bust, and the man asleep through the wall who had knocked on this door at supper and gone away. He thought about Monument Plaza. He thought, with great clarity, *I will not sleep. I will rest the body and I will not sleep,* and he felt his hands, which were a boy's hands and tired, and the tray in them, and the water in the tray, perfectly still.

The body, which had been promised ten days' arrears, took the whole night.

* * *

*The Processional Way was wet. It had rained in the afternoon, the thin engineered rain the city made for itself and switched off, and the great stair held it in the shallow places where a thousand years of feet had walked the stone hollow, and the boots went through it.*

*He could hear the boots. That was what he would keep, after: not the sight of them, which was only white, rank on rank behind him with the blue of the 501st gone grey in the dusk, but the sound. The single, many-footed sound of men who had been bred to step together and had never in their lives stepped any other way. Behind him. Not beside him. He had not told them to come beside him and they would not have thought of it.*

*He walked up the centre of the stair as he had walked up it ten thousand times, and it took exactly as long as it had always taken, and he knew the count of the steps, and the hilt in his hand was warm from his hand and nothing else. He was not angry. He had been angry on the landing platform and in the gunship, and the anger had gone somewhere below him on the way over, like a thing dropped from a height that you do not hear land, and what it left was clear. He could see very well. He could see the lamps coming on along the façade, and the two Temple Guard on the upper landing turning towards the sound, the masks catching the light and the pikes not yet lit, and he could see that the one on the left was going to be the first to understand and the one on the right was going to be the first to die, because the one on the right was going to hesitate. He was right about both.*

*Jurokk met him at the gate. The Gate Master had come out onto the top step as he came out for every returning company, and he had a datapad in one hand because there was always a manifest, always somebody to sign in, and he looked past Anakin at the white ranks coming up through the wet, and then at Anakin, and his face did the thing faces did, that he had watched faces do all that afternoon: it went to the person it knew and looked for him there.*

*"Skywalker," Jurokk said. "What's happened? Is it the Chancellor? Is he—"*

*He did not answer. There was nothing to say that would have been true, and he had stopped saying things that were not true some hours before, on his knees, in a red room. The blade came on. It was blue. It was the same blue it had always been, and nobody had told him that, that it would still be blue. Jurokk had time to look down at it.*

*Then the gate, and the boots on the stone of the entrance hall, loud now, under a roof, and blaster fire behind him at something in a side passage, three shots and done, and ahead and to the left the sound of a lightsaber coming on and then the sound of it stopping. He gave orders. He gave them in the voice he had used at Christophsis and Umbara and Ringo Vinda and the men took them the way they had always taken them, and that was the part of it the dreaming part of him could not bear and the dreamed part did not notice at all: that nothing was different. Fives had been right. He had stood over Fives on the floor of a warehouse with the man's blood on the duracrete and said nothing, and here were the men, the same men, good men, doing it.*

*The Room of a Thousand Fountains was dark because somebody had cut the lights, and full of the sound of water, which did not stop for anything. He went in alone. He had sent the company round by the galleries because the room was Drallig's, and he knew where Drallig would be, and he was there, by the long pool under the central fall, with a dozen of the older initiates behind him and two Padawans and a Knight he did not know, and the green blade already lit and held low, in the guard he had taught every one of them in that room.*

*"Anakin," Drallig said. Not Skywalker. He had the right to the name; he had given the boy most of what he knew. "Whatever this is. Stop here. Stop here and we will—"*

*He did not stop. He never stopped; that was what Drallig had said of him at eleven and at fourteen and at nineteen, with a different weight each time: the boy does not stop. Drallig was the best blade in the Temple and old and had never in his life fought anyone who did not, at some level, want to live, and he had only one hand on his hilt by the third exchange and one working arm by the fifth. The fall came down behind them. The spray was on his face and he could taste it. He killed the Knight who came in on his flank without looking at him and then he finished it, and Drallig went down backwards into the shallow end of the pool and the water took him and moved him a little, and the initiates behind him did not run, because he had taught them not to.*

*He was wet to the knees, after. He noticed that on the stairs. The Council tower stairs, the long spiral he had climbed to be told no, and no, and not yet, and wait, and he was climbing them now, and the boots were not behind him, because he had left the men at the foot of the tower and had not said why. He did not know why. The door at the top had been shut from inside and he put his hand to it and it opened, because every door in the Temple opened to him. That had never been the problem.*

*The lights in the Council chamber were on. He saw the twelve chairs and the windows black behind them and the city beyond doing what the city did at night, and then he saw that the chairs were not empty, that the room was not empty, that in the circle of floor between the chairs, where the petitioner stood, where he had stood at nine years old while they told his master no, there were children. Eight of them. Nine. Bear Clan, by the size, by the tunic hems that had been let down and let down again and were still not long enough. They had hidden. Somebody had told them go up, go to the top, the Council will be there, and the Council had not been there, and they had hidden behind the chairs and under them, and the first of them, a boy, came out from behind Master Windu's chair with his face white and glad.*

*Glad. That was the thing the dream kept, every time. The boy was glad to see him.*

*The boy said his name. The old name, the one with Master in front of it.*

*He heard his own blade come on, in the quiet, and the small faces turned to the sound, all of them at once, the way the fountains room had turned, and the light on them was blue.*

* * *

He woke because the Temple was screaming.

Not the Temple. He understood that in the first second, lying rigid on top of the blanket with his hands full of nothing: stone could not scream. It was the thing that lived in the stone, the Force as it ran through five thousand sleeping Jedi, and it was ringing like a struck pan, and the ring was made of children.

He could not have said how many. Dozens. The dormitory levels were below him and to the east and to the west and they were all awake at once, in the way a hive is awake, and the awakeness was crying: not one child, a weather of children, high thin voices he could hear through the Force and one he could hear through the actual floor, a girl, close, in the quarters below. He felt the Hawkbat dormitory, which was below and east, the way you feel a room full of people through a wall. He felt Bear Clan, the little ones, who had not understood what they dreamed and were the loudest. He felt clan-masters coming awake and lights coming on and somewhere a Knight on night duty running, and he felt, high up and far off in the dark, a small old attention turn towards him like a lamp lit in a window on the other side of a city.

Yoda had sat up.

He did not know how he knew it was sitting up. He knew. Eight hundred years of stillness and then that: a small body in a dark room pushing itself upright, and the attention coming out of it and across the Temple, not fast, not searching yet, only open, the way an old man opens a door at night to see what the noise was.

Anakin put the shield up. It was like throwing a blanket on a fire that had already got into the roof. He put it up anyway, hard, all of it, the wall and the bond with it, slammed, and the crying did not stop because the crying was not his any more; it belonged to the children now, they would be crying for an hour, and he lay there and listened to it and understood, in a clean cold way that had nothing of the dream's coldness in it, that he had done this.

Twice. The first night, ten days ago, waking with the hole in him where the Emperor had been, he had done it without knowing; the healers had been asked, Obi-Wan had told him the four clans had woken. He had put that down to the arrival, to the violence of it, to whatever had brought him. It had not been the arrival. It was him. He had come back forty years and carried this in with him, in his body, in the place where a man keeps the worst thing he has done, and tonight he had taken his hands off it for six hours and it had gone through the Temple like gas through a ship.

He had killed those children once. Tonight he had shown them.

The door opened.

He had not heard the knock; perhaps there had not been one. Obi-Wan stood in the doorway in a sleep tunic, hair flat on one side, the common-room light behind him, and did not come in and did not say anything, and looked at him. Anakin lay where he was. He had not moved since he woke. He realised his hands were in fists on the blanket and opened them.

"The younglings are awake," Obi-Wan said. His voice was quiet and very level. "All of them, I think."

"I heard."

"Did you dream?"

"No." It came out in the right voice, the boy's voice, rough with sleep. He did not know how; something in him had done it before the rest of him was in the room. "Something woke me. I don't know what. Is it— what is it?"

Obi-Wan did not answer. The bond was shut, Anakin knew, shut as hard as the first night, and the man in the door could feel it shut, could feel the exact shape of a door slammed, and said, "You have closed it again," in the same quiet voice, not a question.

"Sorry. I— it was loud. I didn't think."

"No."

Then Obi-Wan did a thing that the Obi-Wan he remembered would not have done. He came in. He came across the small room and sat on the end of the bed, with the mattress tipping under his weight, and put one hand on Anakin's shin through the blanket, the way one would with a child who had been sick, and left it there. He did not look at Anakin's face. He looked at the wall.

"They will ask in the morning," he said.

"Who will?"

"Everyone." He did not take his hand away. The girl in the quarters below had stopped crying; another, further off, had not. "Go back to sleep if you can, my friend. If you can't, don't lie here. Come and sit up with me. I'll make tea."

He got up and went out, and left the door open, and Anakin heard him in the common room, heard the kettle, heard the reader picked up and put down again. The man he remembered would have stood in the doorway. He would have said *Try to sleep* and gone back to bed, because he had been a good master and a good master did not sit on a Padawan's bed at the third hour with his hand on the boy's leg. This one sat. This one had been sitting, in one way or another, for ten days, and Anakin lay and looked at the open door and the slice of light, and could not have said whether the thing in his chest was love or alarm, and did not go out, and did not sleep.

* * *

The summons came at the first hour, by the quarters' panel, in the flat voice the Temple's systems used for everything from fire to laundry. *Padawan Skywalker to the Halls of Healing at the second hour. Master Kenobi is informed.*

Master Kenobi was informed. Master Kenobi had been up since the third hour of the night with the lights on, and had made tea twice and drunk one of them, and when Anakin came out of his room dressed with his braid retied he was already in his boots.

"You don't have to come."

"No," Obi-Wan agreed, and came.

The Halls of Healing were on the north side, three storeys down, and the corridors on the way were wrong. Not loud; the Temple was never loud. But there were people in them who should not have been, at that hour: a clan-master with a sleeping four-year-old over her shoulder, going somewhere; two Knights talking at a junction who stopped when Obi-Wan and Anakin passed and started again after; a pair of Hawkbat initiates in sand-coloured tunics, a Rodian girl and the loud human boy, Tam, being walked somewhere by an older Padawan, the boy not loud at all. The Rodian girl looked at Anakin as she passed and then down. He had taught her a Shii-Cho guard ten days ago and corrected her elbow. He did not know what she had seen.

The Halls were pale and warm and smelled of bacta and something green. There was a bench outside the chief healer's room and a Padawan on it, a Zabrak girl of twelve or so with a blanket round her shoulders, who was taken in as they arrived and brought out again ten minutes later, red-eyed and steadier, by a young human healer who put her hand on the girl's back and sent her off down the corridor. Then the healer looked at the panel, and at them, and said, "Padawan Skywalker. Master Che will see you now. Master Kenobi, she asked that you come in as well."

Vokara Che's room was small and had a window. She was standing at it when they came in, and turned: a Twi'lek, blue-skinned, her lekku wrapped in the pale cloth the healers wore, older than Obi-Wan by twenty years at least and looking, this morning, every one of them. She had been up all night. It was in the set of her shoulders and the way she held her hands, loosely, in front of her, the way people did who had been touching other people's pain for hours and had not yet had time to put it down. Her eyes were kind. That was the first thing and the worst thing. Anakin had expected a Council face and got a healer's.

"Sit down, Padawan," she said. "Master Kenobi. Thank you for coming. Sit, please. I'll be as quick as I can; there are others."

They sat. She did not sit. She leaned against the edge of the table with the window behind her and looked at Anakin, not hard, not for long, the way she would have looked at a sprain.

"I'll tell you what I know and then I'll ask you what you know, and I'd like it to go in that order. Last night, a little before the third hour, nearly every youngling in this Temple under the age of twelve woke from the same dream. Not a similar dream. The same one. I have spoken to thirty-one of them so far and the clan-masters have spoken to the rest, and they describe it in the words they have, which are children's words, but it is one dream." She counted on her fingers, lightly, the way she might have counted symptoms. "A stair, going up, wet. Boots; a great many boots. A gate. Water falling in the dark. A room with chairs in a circle. And a blue light, at the end, in the faces of children. Several of them say someone they knew was in it. None of them can tell me who. Children's dreams are like that; a face in a dream is whoever you were thinking of when you woke." She paused. "Four of the older initiates say that they were the ones holding the blade. They are very frightened by that. I have had to be careful with them."

Anakin said nothing. There was nothing a boy would have said. Beside him Obi-Wan was perfectly still, the stillness of a man holding a cup very full.

"It is the second time in ten days," Vokara Che said. "The first was not so clear, or we were not listening. This time we were. I was awake, and so was Master Yoda, and so were half the healers on this floor, and a dream that goes through a building has a centre, Padawan, as a sound does. You can stand in a corridor and tell which way the bell is." She looked at him. "It was on your level. It was not far from your quarters. I am not here to accuse you of anything. I don't think there is anything to accuse a boy of in a dream; I have had my own. But I would like to ask you: did you dream last night?"

He had decided, on the way down, what the boy would say. "Yes."

"A bad one?"

"Yes, Master."

"Can you tell me what it was?"

"I—" He let it catch. He let his hands do the thing a boy's hands did, in his lap, and looked at them. "It was Tatooine. It's always Tatooine. I'm sorry. I didn't know it— I didn't know it would go anywhere."

"No one does," she said. "That's not a thing anyone is taught, because it is not a thing that is supposed to happen." She was quiet a moment. "I'd like to walk it with you. Do you know what I mean by that?"

"Yes."

"Tell me, so that I'm sure you do."

"You come in," he said. "With my leave. I show you the dream. You look at what I show you. You don't go anywhere I don't take you. I can stop it whenever I want." He had been taught this at twelve by a healer in this room and had never once consented to it, and he knew the words, and he knew, with the part of him that had been a Sith Lord, what they were worth. Not nothing. That was the thing. The practice was gentle and the practice was honest; she would do exactly what she said. A good person was going to come into his head and keep her word, and he was going to lie to her with it.

"That's it exactly," said Vokara Che. "Master Kenobi may stay or go, as you like. I'd ask him to stay."

"Stay," Anakin said, and did not look at Obi-Wan, and Obi-Wan did not move.

"Then when you're ready."

He had perhaps four seconds. He had known since the summons that he would have them and had not wasted them on the walk down, because a thing like this could not be made in a corridor; it had to be made in the room, out of what was in the room, with the real dream still hot behind it so that the seams would not show. He shut his eyes, which was permitted, which a boy would do, and built it.

The rule was the one he had learned from the best liar in the galaxy, who had been his master for twenty-three years, and it was this: do not lie from nothing. Take a true thing and turn it. So. Tatooine, which was true. The heat, which was true, the specific heat of the slave quarter at Mos Espa at the fourth hour with the second sun not yet down, that pressed on the back of the neck like a hand. Sand, which was true, in under the door no matter what you stuffed in the crack, so that the floor of his mother's room had a soft grey drift along one wall that she swept every evening and that was there every morning. He put those down first, floor and air, because a dream needed a floor to stand on and a healer would check the floor.

Then the things the children had dreamed, because she would be looking for them, and if she found their shadows here she would stop looking. A stair: the stair down into the quarter from the street, cut in the rock, that his feet had known in the dark. Wet: the vaporator at the end of the row that leaked, that the whole row had queued at with cups because the leak was free water and Watto's water was not. Boots: not troopers, a Gamorrean's, Gardulla's overseer's, the sound of them on the rock stair when he was three and had not been Watto's yet. Water in the dark; a gate; a blue light: he gave her the blue of a pod engine lit at night in the Boonta pit, and let it fall on faces, and made the faces the faces of the other slave children watching from the wall.

And then, because a lie needed a wound to stop at, or the walker kept walking, he put his mother's hands in it.

He did not have to make those. They were there, they had always been there, under everything, under the suit. The cracked knuckles. The way she pushed his hair off his forehead with the back of her wrist because her fingers were dirty from the shop. He put them in the dream, in the heat, with the sand, and he put, at the edge of the dream where a child would not look straight at it, a slaver's whip: not on him, that would have been too much, that would have been melodrama, and Obi-Wan had read his file; on someone else, down the row, the sound of it, the two-part sound, and his mother's hands going still on his hair while they both listened.

It was a good dream. It was the worst dream a boy his age could have had and it was all true, every piece of it, and none of it was the thing that had rung through the Temple, and it took him four seconds, and when he was done he laid it down over the Processional Way like a cloth over a table and opened his eyes and said, "I'm ready."

"Thank you," said Vokara Che, and came in.

He had been walked by Sidious a hundred times, and had learned what that was: a hand that took the chin and turned the face to the light. This was not that. This was a hand on a railing. She came in the way a healer came into a sickroom, with the door, slowly, giving him time to see her, warm and blue and tired and very careful, and she stood at the edge of what he had made and waited for him to show her where to step.

He showed her the floor. She stood on it. She felt the heat, he felt her feel it, and the sand under the door, and she went where he led, down the rock stair, past the vaporator with its wet dark patch, into the room. She did not hurry. She touched nothing he did not hold out to her. When she came to the whip she stopped, and he felt her stop, felt the real grief in it, a grown woman's grief for a child three years old on a stair in the dark, and he hated himself cleanly and completely and held the dream steady. When she came to his mother's hands she stopped for a long time.

*I'm sorry,* she said, in the dream, not in words. *I'm sorry. This is enough. You've shown me enough.*

And then, because she was good at her work, and because the practice was honest and a healer who had sat up all night with crying children did not stop at the first wound she found, she looked down.

He had known she would. It was why he had built the floor. She looked down through the floor of the dream as a healer looked through skin, and under it there was the other thing: not the Processional Way, which he had put away; not the dream at all. The wall. He felt her find it. He felt her put her hand on it, flat, the way a person puts a hand on a wall in the dark to know what the room is, and he felt the hand go still.

She did not push. She did not try the door. She stood with her palm on it for the space of two of his breaths, and then she took her hand away, and came back up through the floor, and out through the door she had come in by, and was standing against the edge of her table with the window behind her and her hands loose in front of her and her face not quite the same.

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Thank you, Padawan," she said. She said it to him. Then she turned to Obi-Wan, and she did not lower her voice or move away or ask Anakin to wait outside, because she was an honest woman and he was in the room, and she said it in front of him.

"There is a dream. It is a bad one and an old one, and it is a child's; Tatooine. I would not have asked anyone to show it to me and I am sorry I had to ask him." She stopped. "And beneath it there is a wall, old and scarred, as if built over years. I have not seen one like it in a Padawan. I have not seen one like it in many Knights. I did not try it, and I would not, and I don't think I could."

Obi-Wan said, in his courteous voice, "He has had reasons to build one. You'll know some of them, Master. He was a slave. He has spent an afternoon every ten days for six years with a man who—" He stopped himself, visibly, the way he stopped himself about the Chancellor in company. "He has had reasons."

"Perhaps," said Vokara Che. She looked at Anakin again, and he made himself a boy under it, thin and tired and ashamed. "Who taught you to shield, Padawan?"

"Master Kenobi."

"No," she said, quite gently. "He didn't." And to Obi-Wan: "I will have to tell Master Yoda what I found. You understand."

"Of course."

"Go and eat something, Skywalker. You look as though you haven't." She was already moving to the door, already, in her mind, with the next child. "And if it comes again, come here. Come at the third hour, come at any hour. Do you understand me? Don't lie in the dark with it. Nobody should."

He said that he understood.

* * *

Windu was in the corridor.

He had not been there when they went in. He was there now, standing at the junction with his hands behind his back as though he had been passing and had stopped to look out of a window, which was not a thing Mace Windu did. He was in his Council robes. Anakin felt Obi-Wan check beside him, half a step, and recover.

"Master Kenobi," Windu said. "Master Che will want you. She said so, as I came by." It was true; the healer's door had opened again behind them and the young human healer was looking out. "I'll walk your Padawan to the refectory. Unless you object."

There was nothing to object to. Obi-Wan looked at Anakin, one look, and went back.

Windu did not walk anywhere. He waited until the door had shut and then he looked at Anakin, down, because he was a tall man and Anakin was not yet, and his face was the face it had always been, that gave nothing and took nothing and had been right about him, in the end, and had never once liked him.

"Something has come into this Temple, Padawan," Windu said. "If you know what it is, now is the time."

Clipped, direct, not unkind. He had never been cruel. Anakin had told himself for forty years that he had been, and he had not; he had been a man who looked at a boy and saw a danger and said so, to the boy's face, which was more than most of them had done.

*The window. The red room, and the glass gone out of it into the night, and the old man on his back on the sill with the lightning climbing his own arms, and Windu's blade above him, and Windu's face turned to Anakin saying* he's too dangerous to be left alive, *and his own blade coming up, and the hand. The purple blade spinning away. Windu's hand going with it, into the dark, and the rest of him after, the robes opening in the wind like a thing let go, and then nothing, the city, the wind.*

"I don't know what it is, Master," Anakin said. "I had a nightmare. I'm sorry it woke them. I didn't know it could."

Windu looked at him for a long moment. Anakin looked back and let his eyes go hot and wet, because he was fifteen and had been up all night and it was easy, it was easier than anything, and he felt the old thing come up in him under the boy's face: the grudge, the specific and unjust grudge, forty years old, that said *you never once gave me the benefit of a doubt,* and that knew, even as it said it, that the man had been right not to. He had been right. Anakin had thrown him out of a window. He would, if he closed his eyes and let himself, throw him again. He hated him for it and it was not the man's fault and he hated him anyway, standing in a pale corridor in a boy's body with his eyes wet, and he lied to his face, and the lie went in, and Windu had no way to know.

"Then that is what I will tell the Council," Windu said. He said it the way he would have signed a form. "Go and eat, Skywalker. Your master will find you."

He went. His boots were very quiet on the Temple floor and the sound of them went away down the corridor, and Anakin stood where he was until it had.

Obi-Wan came out five minutes later. He did not say what Vokara Che had wanted him for. They walked to the refectory, which was a long way, three storeys up and across half the Temple, and the corridors were still wrong, still full of the night: a clan-master, two Knights, a Bear Clan boy of six being carried, asleep, past a window with the morning in it. Obi-Wan did not say anything for the whole of the first corridor, or the second. At the stair he put his hand briefly on Anakin's shoulder, as he had in the plaza, and took it off.

He had been asked three questions that morning, by three people, and had lied in answer to all of them. The one who had the most right to ask was walking beside him. He had been there in the room when the healer said *a wall, old and scarred, as if built over years,* and he had heard her say *he didn't,* and he had not looked at his Padawan when she said it, and he had not, in two corridors and a stair, asked what the wall was.

Anakin waited for it all the way to the refectory, and it did not come, and he did not know, sitting down across from the man with a tray he could not eat from, which of them that was a mercy for.

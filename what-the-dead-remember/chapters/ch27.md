## Chapter 27: The Council Must Not Know

The eastern spire had four hundred and twelve steps, and Obi-Wan Kenobi had counted them once, at twenty-seven, on a morning when he had needed something to count.

He counted them again now because the alternative was to think about his left side, which had a hole in it that the bacta had closed and his body had not yet agreed to. The sling held the arm. Nothing held the rest. He had been out of the tank two days, and Vokara Che had given him a list of things he was not to do, and climbing the eastern spire to be questioned by a creature nine hundred years old had been left off it only because Che had not thought anyone would be so stupid.

Yoda had sent for him. Not the Council. Yoda.

He stopped on the hundred-and-fortieth step, where the stair turned and there was a slit of window, and let his breath come back. Below, the Temple roofs went away in grey terraces, and beyond them the city, and beyond the city, under the haze, the dome of the Senate. He had stood in this exact place four years ago. He had been counting then because the corridor was still in his hands.

It had been in his hands for days. The weight of the hilt; the thumb on the switch; the old body's ache in the shoulder from holding the blade up and level, the way the eyes behind the mask had been a dark he could not see into, the small, private smile he had allowed himself because he had understood, at the end, what he was for. And then the sweep of red, and nothing, and a ceiling.

The ceiling had been this one. The quarters below. The small room off the common room, a cot, a shelf, the window he had looked out of for thirteen years without seeing it. He had lain and looked at the ceiling and then he had looked at his hands, and they had been a young man's hands, no spots, no thickening at the knuckles, the little finger of the left one straight that had been crooked thirty years, and the hilt had still been in them. He could feel it. He could feel the hilt of a lightsaber he had let fall on a floor that did not yet exist.

He had got up. He had gone to the other door.

The boy had been asleep on his stomach with one arm hanging off the cot, the way he always slept. Eleven. The braid, two years old and still thin as a bootlace, lay across the pillow. Sandy hair cut short and badly, because he would not sit still for it. A child's hand on the floor, palm up, the fingers curled. A child's shoulder blades under the sleep-tunic. A child's breathing, open-mouthed, untroubled, with no machine in it.

Obi-Wan had gone into the refresher and shut the door and sat down on the floor with his back against it, and he had stayed there an hour, and he had not made a sound, because the boy would wake.

He began to climb again, and the other thing climbed with him, the thing he had been carrying up the stair since the first step. *Can you still look at me?* Two nights ago, the night Che had let him out of the tank for good, the boy had asked it in the dark of the common room, in a voice with no armour on it at all, and Obi-Wan had looked. That was all he had done, and all he had been able to do: he had looked at Anakin through the whole of that night's vigil, at the face that had been Vader's under the mask and was fifteen and wary and wanted an answer, and he had kept looking until the window greyed, so that the boy would have it in the only coin he had that did not need a lie. He had not slept since. He had lain on the cot with his eyes shut and the question going round, and had got up before the bell and dressed one-handed, and now he was climbing to a creature who saw further into a man than any scanner, with no sleep in him to hide behind.

* * *

He had decided on the stairs, that first morning, not to tell Yoda. He had been deciding it every day since, and the reasons had not changed, which was either the mark of a sound decision or of a man who had stopped listening.

The first reason was simple and he could have said it to the Council's face. He had watched them. He had sat in that circular room for three years and watched the twelve wisest people in the Order be wrong at every turn: wrong about the Sith, wrong about Dooku, wrong about the Chancellor, wrong about the army, wrong about the war, wrong about the boy, wrong about him. Not stupid. Never stupid. Wrong in the particular way of people who had been right for a thousand years and could not conceive of the shape of a thing they had never seen. He had been one of them. He had been wrong with them. He would not, this time, hand the one thing he knew to a room that would do with it what it did with everything, which was to deliberate, and watch, and wait for certainty, and arrive too late.

The second reason was the boy. A Council that knew would watch him. They would do it kindly; Yoda would do it with love. They would watch him the way one watches a charge that has been set and not yet gone off, and the boy would feel it, because the boy felt everything, and a watched boy becomes the bomb. That was how it had happened the first time. Not Palpatine alone. Palpatine, and a Council that had looked at a frightened child and seen a danger, and a child who had looked back and seen that he was not trusted, and had gone to find someone who would.

The third reason he had never said aloud, not even to himself, not until the ledge behind the third waterfall nine nights ago, with the lists on the stone and the boy's face turned towards him and the Tano child's small boots not reaching the floor.

They would have told him to give the boy up. For the boy's own sake; for the Order's. And he could not have done it. He had been told once to let go of everything he feared to lose and he had tried, by the gods he had tried, and what he had let go of had got up out of the lava and gone and killed everyone he had ever known.

He reached the top of the stair. His side was a hot wire. He stood a moment, so as not to arrive breathing like a man who had climbed four hundred and twelve steps with a hole in him, and then he went in.

* * *

Yoda's own room was small, and round, and had nothing in it but a cushion and a window and the old one on the cushion, and it was always warmer than the rest of the spire, as if nine hundred years of sitting had got into the stone.

"Sit, you will," said Yoda, without opening his eyes. "Before fall down, you do."

"I had been going to stand, Master. On principle."

"Hm. Principle. Heavy, principle is. Sit."

Obi-Wan sat. The floor was warm. He arranged the sling and did not let his face do anything while he did it. Yoda opened his eyes and looked at him, and it was the look that Obi-Wan had been looked at with since he was a boy of four in a sand-coloured tunic, and it had never once got easier.

They did not speak for a while. That was Yoda's way; he let a silence sit until it had said what it had come to say. Obi-Wan, who had learned a longer patience on a ridge above a farm, let it.

"Five months and a little," Yoda said. "A shadow, the Council has hunted. In the Temple. Since the night the younglings woke crying."

"Yes."

"Found it, we have not."

"No."

"Know what it is, you do."

It was not a question, and Obi-Wan did not answer it as one. He had shaped what he would say on the stairs, between the two-hundredth step and the three-hundredth, and he had shaped it the way one shapes a blade: to be true along its whole edge and to cut only where he chose.

"The boy had a vision, Master," he said. "Of the end of the Order. Of everything that is in this Temple, ended, and by whose hand. I believe it. I believe it is true. I will not say more than that, and I am asking you not to make me."

Yoda looked at him. The ears had gone down a fraction, which was the only tell the old one had ever had, and Obi-Wan had learned it at nine and had never told anyone.

Behind the eyes was a thing Obi-Wan had seen there once before, on the *Crucible*'s return, when Yoda had said four words about the Tano child to Plo Koon in his hearing, and had looked, saying them, as though he were looking at a stone dropped into water a very long way off and counting the rings. He was looking at Obi-Wan like that now.

"Believe him, you do," Yoda said at last.

"Yes."

"Lie to me, you do also."

"By what I do not say. Yes."

"Hm." The old one shifted on his cushion. "Nine hundred years, I have taught. Thousands. Asked me many things, they have. Asked me to be blind, none has." A pause. "Blind you ask me to be, Obi-Wan."

"For a while."

"For a while." Yoda's voice was very quiet. "To the Council, this I will not bring. Not yet. Hunt the shadow they will, and find it they will not, and say nothing I will, and when Master Windu looks at me, look away I will. Cost me, this does."

"I know."

"Know, you do not. Lied to the Council, I have never. Not in nine hundred years. Begin now, I do, for you. And cost you more, it does. Your oldest teacher, you have lied to. Let you, I have. Carry it, we both will."

Obi-Wan found that he could not speak for a moment.

"When the while ends," Yoda said, "know you will. Before I do. Tell me then, you will, or tell me not, and the Council I will tell either way. Agreed, this is?"

"Agreed."

"Hm." The ears came up. "Go now. Four hundred steps, down. Fall, do not. Explain to the healers, I would have to, and like that, I would not."

Obi-Wan stood. It took him longer than it should have. At the door he turned, because there was one more thing, and he did not know until he said it that he was going to.

"Master. The boy is not the shadow."

Yoda had closed his eyes again.

"Know that, I do," he said. "Afraid of what he has seen, he is. Afraid of him, you are not. Afraid for him. Different, that is. Different enough, perhaps."

Obi-Wan went down.

* * *

He did not go back to the quarters. He went to the Archives, because they were on the way, and because he was tired enough to want the one room in the Temple where no one looked at him.

He had come here in the second year, when the boy was thirteen and in Drallig's hall for the afternoon, and had sat at terminal four and typed *Kamino* and waited forty minutes for an answer that did not come. He had known it would not. He had typed it anyway, to see the gap with his own eyes, because if the gap had not been there then nothing he remembered could be trusted. It had been there. He had felt what he had come to call, privately, the relief of the damned.

Four days later, *Sifo-Dyas*. Eleven minutes. A Council seat, a death on Oba Diah, a lightsaber unrecovered. He had read it and gone to Dex's.

Dex had put a mug in front of him with one hand, wiped the counter with another, and said, before Obi-Wan had asked anything, "You look like a man who's about to ask me about something a long way off." And Obi-Wan had asked, and Dex had told him: tall, white, polite, cloners, the best there were, out past the Rishi Maze on a world that was on no chart in the Core because they paid to keep it off, and it would cost a courier and a season to get there and he'd want to go armed. "What's on Kamino that's got you drinking my coffee at eleven in the morning, Obi-Wan?"

"Nothing I can prove," he had said, which was true.

He had not gone. He had sat in this Archive the next week with the Padawan's training schedule on one side of the terminal and a list of courier costs on the other, and had understood that he could go to Kamino, alone, for a season, and leave the boy in the Temple with the Chancellor's audiences falling every tenth day, or he could stay. He had stayed. That was the choice, made in this room, that had made all the others. He had chosen the boy over the army.

The list of the dead he did not read here. He did not read it anywhere but at night, alone, at the table in the common room with the boy asleep. By day it lay face down in the drawer under his night-pad, and he did not open the drawer.

It had begun in the first week. He had lain awake on the third night with a name in his head that would not go, a name he had seen on a security recording in a place he would not name, and he had got up and written it on a flimsi so that it could be somewhere other than inside him. Then another, because the first had been lonely. Then he had understood what he was doing and had done it properly: a name, a place, what he knew of how. He had got to forty-one. He had stopped when the knowing stopped, because he would not guess at the dead. The Tano child's name was not on it. He had not known.

There was a place at the bottom of the list he had never written in. He had never written the boy's name. He had had the stylus on the flimsi, more than once.

* * *

The message had come that morning, before the spire. It was on the table in the common room when he got back, where he had left it, and the boy was standing over it, not touching it, with his hands behind his back as though it might go off.

"The seal's the Senate liaison office," Anakin said. "It's addressed to you."

"It is. Sit down. You are looming, and it does not suit a person who still has to grow into his feet."

The boy sat. Obi-Wan sat, slowly, across from him, and picked the flimsi up and read it aloud, because there was no way to tell him that was better than another.

"'The Office of the Senator for Naboo presents its compliments to Master Kenobi of the Jedi Order, and enquires after the health of Padawan Skywalker following the incident on Oba Diah, of which the HoloNet reports have been various and alarming. The Senator's committee on Outer Rim franchise disclosure will take evidence in the coming session on Syndicate conduct in the Oba Diah sector, and the Senator would be grateful for any account the Temple is in a position to share. With respect and regards, by the hand of the Senator's secretary.'" He put it down. "There is a stamp. It is a very good stamp."

He watched it land.

The boy's face did nothing for the length of a breath. Then it did several things, fast, and put them away, and what was left was a fifteen-year-old sitting very still with his hands flat on the table, and Obi-Wan, who had seen this face at every age it had had, including an age it had not had a face at all, saw the man under it go and stand at a window a long way off, and not turn round.

"She wrote to you," Anakin said.

"She wrote to the Temple. The Temple wrote to me. There is a department for it. I believe there is a droid." He let that sit, and then he said, more gently, "She has written before. I have the second letter. I have had it four years. I did not give it to you, because I did not know what it would do to you, and then I knew, and that was worse."

"I know you have it."

"Of course you do."

The boy looked at the flimsi. He did not pick it up either.

"What are you going to tell her?"

"That Padawan Skywalker is in excellent health, having lately been in the company of a master who is not, and that the Temple has no account to share with the committee at this time. Which is true, and which is what the Council will want said, and which I shall say in better prose than the liaison office is used to." He paused. "Unless you want to answer her yourself."

"No."

"All right."

"Not—" Anakin stopped. His jaw worked. He was fifteen, and he flushed easily, and he was flushing now, up the neck, the way the boy had in the first year when anyone spoke of Tatooine. "Not yet."

"All right," Obi-Wan said again, and left it, because he had learned when to, and he reached across the table with the hand that worked and turned the flimsi face down, the way he turned the other thing face down, and the boy watched him do it and understood, and neither of them said so.

* * *

Qui-Gon had never answered.

He had tried on the first anniversary of Naboo after he woke, in the Room of a Thousand Fountains, at the hour of the morning when the mist came off the lower pools and the light came through it sideways and the whole place looked like somewhere a dead man might consent to visit. He had sat, and opened himself, and asked. He had asked with courtesy, because his master had liked courtesy when he could get it, and then with grief, and then, in the second year, with the particular dry irritation that had got more out of Qui-Gon Jinn alive than any amount of reverence ever had. *You spoke to me on Tatooine. I was sixty and alone and you spoke to me. I am younger now and I have better questions.* Nothing. Water on stone. The third year, the fourth. He had gone on going. He had sat, this last time, with the boy fourteen and the Tano child a shape in the back of every room, and said nothing at all, and waited to see whether silence would do what words had not, and the silence had been answered with silence, which was at least consistent.

He had, at any rate, been alone with a boy he was afraid of for four years and not once had anyone wiser told him what to do. That was going to have to be enough.

* * *

The Room of a Thousand Fountains at night was a different place, and the third waterfall from the east door was louder than it looked, and the ledge behind it was dry and narrow and smelled of moss and cold stone, and had been, Obi-Wan now knew, a dead-drop for two dead people for the better part of four months before a third dead person had been let in on it.

He got down on to the ledge with some difficulty and a certain amount of commentary, none of which either of the other two acknowledged, and sat with his back against the rock and the sling against his chest and the water a sheet of black and silver a hand's breadth from his boots.

Anakin sat on his left. Ahsoka sat on his right, on the end of the ledge, where it narrowed, with her knees drawn up and her small boots not reaching the stone below, and the white of her face-markings catching what light there was. Ten. A girl in a sand-coloured tunic with lekku barely past her shoulders and a woman's eyes in her face, and he had understood at last, nine nights ago, why she had frightened him for seven years, and the understanding had not made it stop.

They had been quiet a while. It was the boy who broke it, and he broke it flatly, as a man puts a tool on a table.

"I can kill him."

Nobody asked who.

"I've done it once. I know what it takes. I know where he sleeps, I know where he works, I know what the guards are and what they aren't, and I know what he can do, because I watched him do it for twenty-three years, and I know what I can do, because I did it." He was looking at the water, not at either of them. "He's at 500 Republica eight nights in ten. He's in the Works the other two. I know which two. He doesn't know I know. He thinks I'm a fifteen-year-old with a crush on Mirren Dask and a grudge against Master Windu. I could walk in on the next tenth-day with my saber on my belt and come out with the Republic saved and nobody the wiser, and the only thing stopping me is the two of you."

"Yes," said Obi-Wan. "That is the only thing. I am glad we have established it."

"Master—"

"Not yet."

"Why?"

Obi-Wan looked at the water for a while himself. He was very tired, and his side hurt, and he had lied to Yoda that morning and been allowed to, and all of that was in his voice when he spoke, and he did not try to take it out.

"Because I have sat in the Senate," he said. "You have not. You were a boy there and a weapon after; I have sat through a hundred sessions. If the Chancellor dies tonight, in his bed, in the sanctum of 500 Republica, with a lightsaber wound through him, what the Senate will see is not a Sith Lord. They do not know the word. What they will see is a kindly old man from Naboo, six years in office, who stood up for the Rim and was butchered in his sleep by the religious order he had been so patient with. They will make a martyr of him before the body is cold. And the Jedi who did it—"

"Nobody would know it was—"

"The Jedi who did it," Obi-Wan said, "are the Jedi who are purged. Not in twelve years. In twelve days. You know who is waiting. Dooku is two years from his speech and he will give it two years early with a dead Chancellor to point at. The Trade Federation have their droids on the shelves; we saw the hull-codes. The army on Kamino is not ready, so they will not need the army. They will need a vote. And a Senate that has just watched its Chancellor murdered by a Jedi will give them any vote they like, and the first one will be about us." He stopped. "And he is not alone. You know that better than I do. There is Dooku. There is whoever Dooku has. There was a creature on Naboo that I cut in half, and it got up, and I did not know it for twelve years. If you kill Sidious tonight you kill one Sith, and you do not know how many are left, and neither do I, and the one thing I am sure of is that there is always one more."

Anakin's hands had closed. "So we wait. We wait, and he goes on. He has a chair in the Senate and a boy in his office every tenth day and a war in a drawer. I know what waiting costs. I've paid it. I've *been* it."

"I know."

"You don't—"

"I know," Obi-Wan said. "I sat on a ridge for nineteen years. I am not asking you to wait because I like it. I am asking you to wait because I have seen the alternative, and it was you."

That landed. He had meant it to and he was sorry, which was a thing he could be both of at once now, at fifty-seven, in a body that was thirty-one and bleeding.

Ahsoka had said nothing. She said it now, quietly, over the water.

"You're both wrong about where to start."

They looked at her. She did not look back; she was looking at her own boots.

"It's not him. It's not the Senate. It's the chips." Her voice was a child's, and the words were not. "There are a million of them growing on Kamino, right now, tonight, in tubes, and every one of them has a thing in its head that Sidious put there, and they're *six*. They're six years old. They look twelve. Rex looks twelve. Cody looks twelve." She said the names the way one lays flowers. "You kill Palpatine, Skyguy, and the chips are still there, and the next one who knows the words says them, and it's Dooku, and it happens anyway, only now it happens to an Order that's just murdered the Chancellor. You wait, Master Kenobi, and the chips are still there, and every day you wait they're a day older, and they're still children, they're children like *that* one—" a small sharp nod at the tunnel to the main hall, where somewhere a hundred clans were asleep— "and nobody in this Temple knows they exist. Chips first. Kamino first. Find the thing in their heads and get it out, and then there's no army to use on us, and then you can argue about the Senate all you like."

"Kamino is a season away on a courier," Obi-Wan said. "I have the costings. I have had them four years."

"I know. I'm not saying it's easy. I'm saying it's first."

"And I'm saying," said Anakin, "that while we're pulling chips out of six-year-olds one at a time on a planet nobody can find, he's in that office, and he's *looking* for us. He's got the recording. Dooku's got the recording. He knows what I am. He doesn't know what you are, Snips, and he doesn't know what you are, Master, and that's the only edge we've got, and it's going."

"Then it is going," said Obi-Wan. "It will go whatever we do. The question is what we have bought with it before it does."

They had come round to the beginning. He could feel it; they all could. He had been in enough councils to know the sound of an argument that had found its circle and would run it until someone was too tired to go on, and he was the one who was too tired, and he was not going to say so.

Nobody said anything for a long time. The water went on.

"I'm not agreeing," Anakin said eventually.

"No."

"I'm not agreeing either," said Ahsoka.

"I would be alarmed if you did. It would be the first time in two lives." He let his head go back against the rock. "I am not agreeing with myself, for what it is worth. I am simply the one who has to say *not yet*, because somebody must, and I have the beard for it."

The boy made a sound that was not quite a laugh. On his other side the girl's shoulder, small and warm through the tunic, came to rest against the sling, carefully, on the side that did not hurt.

None of them moved to go.

He had thought, on the refresher floor four years ago, that he would do this alone. He had thought it on the ridge for nineteen years and in the corridor at the end, and he had been wrong about that, at least, and he sat with the two of them in the dark behind the water and did not agree with either of them and did not get up, and neither did they.

## Chapter 28: The Daughter's Bird

Ahsoka had stopped listening somewhere while Obi-Wan kept answering *not yet*, and had started counting instead, which was what she did in rooms where the adults were talking.

The water went on. The ledge was cold through her tunic. On her left Obi-Wan's good side was warm and his sling smelled of bacta and the healers' soap, and on her right, a metre off, Anakin sat with his knees up and his long boy's hands hanging between them, and the two of them talked across the top of her montrals as if she were a low table. They did not mean to. She knew they did not mean to. That was almost the worst of it.

*Kamino is a season away on a courier.* *He's in that office and he's looking for us.* *The question is what we have bought.* She had said the chips were first and she had kept saying it and each time it had gone into the argument the way a stone goes into that pool: a sound, a ring, and then the surface closed. They heard her. They did not hear her. There was a difference and she had spent seven years learning its exact width.

She was ten. She had been ten for most of a year now, and nine before that, and eight, and the body had got used to her long before she had got used to it. Her feet did not reach the floor of the ledge where it dropped; she had tucked them under. Her hands in her lap were a child's hands, the nails bitten, which was not a thing she had ever done in the other life and which she had let herself do here because children bit their nails and it was one less thing to perform. She had commanded a battalion at sixteen. She had run a network for ten years under a name that was not a name, and sat in rooms with men who would have killed her for a tenth of what she knew, and said nothing in the right order until they left. And here she sat between the two people in the galaxy who knew all that, and they talked over her head, with love, with a combined century of grief in their voices, and she hated it with a clean small hatred that surprised her by being so childish.

She let her shoulder rest against the sling, on the side that did not hurt. Obi-Wan did not move away. That was something.

* * *

Anakin walked her back as far as the fountain stair, because it was past the hour when a youngling could be in the corridors alone without a story, and a Padawan escorting a youngling to the dormitories was a story that explained itself.

"You didn't hear me," she said, on the stair.

"I heard you. Chips first."

"You heard the words."

He looked down at her. He had to; the top of her montrals came to his shoulder. She had been taller than this once, nearly his height, and she had been able to hit him in the shoulder without reaching, and she missed that more than she had expected to miss anything about a body.

"Snips, I'm not—"

"You're both doing it. You don't know you're doing it. Every time I say something you both go quiet for a second and then you carry on from where you were, and the second is you deciding whether a ten-year-old gets a vote." She kept her voice low. There was a Guard on the landing above, and the Guard's mask turned, and she thought of another mask, grey and marked, and did not let the thought finish. "I'm not asking you to like it. I'm telling you I've noticed."

"All right."

"That's not hearing it either."

"I know." He rubbed the back of his neck, the boy's gesture, the one that was real. "I'm trying. It's — you're small. I look at you and you're small."

"I was small on the *Venator* too," she said. "I was small when I cut it out of him."

He did not answer that. They had gone over it at Ilum and again on the ledge and he had been there for the end of Fives, he knew the shape of it, but he had not been in the surgery bay with the lights going red and the droid saying *the scan shows nothing* while she held her hand over the place on Rex's skull and said *look again*. Obi-Wan had been told. Anakin had been told. Neither of them had seen Rex's face when he came out of it, the look of a man who has been informed that he is a loaded weapon and has agreed to be carried anyway. That was hers. It was not a thing she could hand across a ledge in a sentence.

There were others. She kept a count of them, the things that were only hers, the way she kept the count of the stairs. Maul in the throne room with the beams coming down, and what he had offered her, and what he had said about Anakin, and how nearly she had believed him; Obi-Wan knew that Maul had lived and that he had killed him at the end, and did not know that the last person Maul had asked for help was a girl of seventeen standing in a dead city, and that she had said no. The faces under the Inquisitors' helmets, the ones she had seen without them. The man Caleb Dume became, and his eyes. She had not told Anakin all of that. Some of it she would never tell him, because it would not help, and because he carried enough.

"Goodnight, Skyguy," she said.

"Night, Snips."

He went up and she went down. On the lower landing the same Pau'an Guard stood at his post behind the black-marked mask, and she passed within a pace of him. She knew what he would become in another life: the Grand Inquisitor, who would hunt her friends across the Rim with a double-bladed saber and a patience she had learned to dread. Once her stomach would have turned and her feet stopped; now she kept walking and did not flinch. He was only a Guard on a stair, a person who had not yet chosen anything, and she decided, quietly, that she would let him be that for as long as he could.

* * *

Master Plo kept his quarters on the walkway side of the Tranquillity Spire, where the Council members who liked the sky had theirs, and she had never once gone to them unasked in seven years. She went the next afternoon, between astrogation and the meal, with the permission she had asked Master Sen for and Sen's *I am surprised at you* still in her ears from the other week. She knocked. She was small enough that she knocked at a height most visitors did not, and she heard him notice it on the other side of the door before it opened.

"Little 'Soka."

"Master Plo. Can I come in?"

He stood back. The room was spare and dim and smelled of the filtered air of his mask, and there was a low table and two cushions and a window that looked out over the whole of the Temple's eastern roof, and she sat on the cushion with her boots not touching the floor and he sat opposite, folding down in that slow way of his, and waited. He was good at waiting. He had waited seven years.

She had rehearsed it on the stair and in the dormitory and in the astrogation class with the instructor's voice going on about Rishi vectors, and all the versions had been wrong, because they had been too clever. Obi-Wan had said: *tell him you are frightened for a boy.* Obi-Wan had told Yoda he believed in a vision. It was a good shape. She took it, and cut it down to a child's size.

"I have dreams," she said. "About what's going to happen. Not — not like other people's dreams. They're true. I know they're true because some of them have already come true and I didn't tell anyone, I just waited and watched them happen." She looked at her hands. That part she did not have to perform. "I asked you once if a dream could be a memory. You remember."

"I remember."

"It wasn't a question. I'm sorry. I didn't know how to ask the real one."

"And what is the real one?"

"Whether you'll believe me. Not now. Later." She made herself look up. The mask gave back nothing, the way it always had, and she had learned long ago to read the stillness instead, the angle of the head. "Some of what I dream is bad, Master Plo. Some of it's — it's for the whole Order. And when it starts to happen, I'm going to come to the Council and tell them, and they're going to look at me the way everyone looks at me, and they're going to decide a child can't know. And they'll be wrong. One day the Council will have to trust a child. I need one of them to remember that I said it first."

The room was very quiet. Below the window the roof of the eastern hall went out in long grey tiles and a line of birds that were not convors sat along the ridge of it, and she watched them because she could not watch him.

"Then I will remember that you said it," Plo Koon said.

That was all. He did not ask what she had dreamed. He did not ask which dreams had come true, or what was going to happen to the Order, or why a child of ten would speak of the Council as though she had sat in rooms with it. He did not ask about the hangar, or the Guard he had never sent, or the leave he had never signed and had said *I have no question* about. He looked at her, and the look went on a long time, longer than a look at a child goes on, and somewhere in the middle of it she understood that it had stopped being a question. It had been a question for seven years. She had felt it on the back of her neck in dojos and refectories and on the *Crucible*'s deck. Now it was something else. It was the look of a man who has decided to know something and not to say it, and to carry the weight of not saying it himself so that a child will not have to.

She loved him. She had loved him since she was three the first time and three the second, and there was nothing to be done about it, and it was terrible, because she knew where it ended. She knew the sky over Cato Neimoidia. She had read the report in a cold room on a ship that no longer existed. She sat on his cushion with her boots off the floor and she could not tell him, and he had just told her, in nine words, that he would not make her.

"Thank you, Master," she said, and her voice was a child's and shook like one, and he let it.

"Eat something before the meal," he said. "You are too thin for your clan. Sen tells me so and I am inclined to believe her."

"Yes, Master Plo."

At the door he put his hand on her head, between the montrals, the way he had on Shili, and she went out under it and down the stair and did not stop walking until she was somewhere nobody could see her, and then she stopped.

* * *

Barriss Offee was twelve and a clan above her and left things behind.

It was a small thing to know about a person. Ahsoka had collected it over seven years without meaning to, the way she had collected the sound of Master Sen's sleeves: Barriss, who was quiet and earnest and whose handwriting on a flimsi was like a diagram, and who forgot her towel on the rail at the end of the dojo hour, every time, and came back for it with her face hot while the next clan was filing in.

Hawkbat had the dojo after Barriss's clan on the fourth day. Ahsoka had learned not to look at her. She had learned it the first year, when looking had made her stomach turn and a healer had asked whether she was eating. So she filed in with Kessa and Wix and Tam Dorreth being loud, and kept her eyes on the floor, and saw the towel.

It was blue-grey and folded and had *OFFEE* stitched in the corner in the healers' neat thread. The rail was at Ahsoka's eye-height. She stood and looked at it and the clan went past her and Drallig's assistant called the first form and she thought: *leave it.* Let it be found by someone else. Let the girl come back in with her face hot and take it in front of twenty ten-year-olds. It was nothing. It was a towel.

In six years that girl would stand in a courtroom and tell the truth about the Order in a voice that would not shake, and it would be the truest thing anyone said that year, and she would have killed people to earn the right to say it, and she would have let Ahsoka hang for it first. Ahsoka knew the exact weight of the cuffs. She knew the sound of the Senate guards' boots, and the shape of the tunnel under the Temple where she had run from Anakin because she could not trust him and had been right not to trust anyone, and the look on Barriss's face at the end when Anakin pulled her saber from her hand and it was green, Ahsoka's own green, and the girl had said — Ahsoka did not let herself remember what the girl had said.

She took the towel off the rail.

It was easy. It weighed nothing. She stood at the dojo door with it and the clan ahead was still in the corridor, a dozen twelve-year-olds in their sand-coloured tunics, and she had to look up to find the one she wanted, and she held it out.

"You left this."

Barriss turned. She had blue eyes and the Mirialan marks across her nose and she was already going red. "Oh — thank you. I always — thank you." She took it. She looked at Ahsoka properly, which she had never done, because there was no reason for a girl of twelve to look properly at a girl of ten. "Tano, isn't it? Hawkbat?"

"Yes."

"Thank you, Tano."

She smiled. It was a shy, real smile, the smile of someone who has not yet done anything, and Ahsoka smiled back with the child's face she had built, and went in to the first form, and held her training saber the right way round for the whole hour because she did not trust her hands.

That night she did not sleep. She lay beside Kessa with her palm flat on her chest and the thin bond faint under it, and did not reach down it, and watched the dormitory ceiling until the light changed. Kindness was cheap. Everyone said so. She had just found out what it cost, and it was a night, and she paid it, and in the morning her eyes hurt and Sen said nothing, which was Sen's way of saying a great deal.

* * *

Caleb Dume found her in the refectory, as he always did, by the method of walking the length of the Bear Clan table and then the length of the Hawkbat table until he reached her, and then sitting down.

He was seven. His hair stuck up at the back. His tunic hem was turned up twice because they had not yet found one that fitted him, and he had a bowl of something he had already half spilled, and he put it down beside hers and said, "They moved the stairs."

"Which stairs?"

"The ones by the Archives. The ones that go down. There's a Guard on them now and you can't go down."

"They didn't move them. They just put a Guard on them."

"That's the same," said Caleb, with the certainty of a boy who has never in his life been contradicted by the facts, and started eating.

She watched him. She could not help it. She had told herself a hundred times that she would stop, that it was unfair to a seven-year-old to be looked at by someone who knew what was coming for him, and she had not stopped, because he kept coming and sitting down.

"Ahsoka."

"What."

"When I'm big I'm going to be—" He stopped and frowned at his spoon. "I forgot."

"That's all right."

"I was going to say something good."

"You were."

"Yeah." He ate. "I'll remember later."

She did not say *you will.* She put her hand briefly on the top of his head, the way Plo had put his on hers, and took it away before he noticed, and finished her meal, and when she got up he got up too and followed her as far as the door and then went to find Bear Clan, because that was as far as he ever followed her, and she had never found out why.

* * *

The roof at dawn was the coldest place in the Temple, and she went up because of that.

She had climbed to it the first year, when she was four and nobody watched a four-year-old at the fifth hour, and had found the place where the eastern hall's roof met the base of the spire and made a lee from the wind. She had come up most mornings since. The convor was always there, somewhere: on the ridge, on the stair-head, on the gutter at the far end. It never flew. It had never, in seven years, come within four metres of her. She had stopped trying to make it, around the time she stopped trying to make a lot of things.

This morning the sky over the Senate District was the colour of the inside of a shell and the air hurt to breathe and she sat with her back to the spire's stone and her knees pulled up inside the tunic, which was a thing you could do at ten and not at thirty-three, and watched the speeder lanes come up out of the dark.

She heard it before she saw it. The small scrape of claws on tile.

It came along the ridge of the roof, hopping, the way it walked, because it did not fly. Green. The owl-face. The feathered crest that went up and down as it turned its head. She did not move. She had learned that much. It came along the ridge and then it came down off the ridge onto the flat where she sat, and crossed the flat, and stopped.

Within reach. For the first time in seven years, if she put out her hand, she could have touched it.

She did not put out her hand.

It looked at her. The eyes were gold and round and knew her. They had known her on Shili, in the grass, before Plo came. They had known her on a ship with no name over a planet where a man with a lightsaber had told her she would die. They had known her at every stage in between, and she had never asked why, because asking was a thing you did when you expected an answer, and she had not expected one.

*A hand on her chest. Light going in. The Daughter's face, which had been so much like a girl's.*

The flashback cut across the morning and was gone. She sat with it.

She had never let herself think it through. She had thought *Mortis* and then stopped, because Mortis was the one place in either life where the rules had not held, and she had wanted so badly, these seven years, for there to be rules. But sit with it. Sit with it on a cold roof with the bird a hand's breadth away.

The Daughter had died on Mortis. The Son had killed her, and in dying she had given what was left of herself to a girl who was also dead, because Anakin had asked and the Father had allowed it, and the girl had got up. Ahsoka had got up. She had carried something of the Daughter out of that place in her body and had carried it for seventeen years without ever quite looking at it, through the war and the Siege and the long dark and Malachor, and on Malachor the temple had come down on her and she had stood under the falling stone and — and then Shili. Grass. Three years old.

The light that had carried her back from her own death at the Son's hand had been the Daughter's. It had still been in her when she stood under the stone. It had been in her at the end.

And the bird had been there on Shili, waiting, before she had even known she was alive again.

"It's you," she said aloud. Her breath went out white. "It was you. She did this."

The convor turned its head the full way round and back, the way it did, and settled its feathers, and did not go away.

It was not an answer. It was the nearest thing to one she had been given in seven years and she took it, and held it, and found that her hands were shaking, which they did not do, which she had trained out of them before she was twenty. The Daughter's life was in her. The Daughter had put it there to save her once and it had saved her twice, and the second time it had not saved her where she stood but had put her somewhere else, somewhere before, and the Daughter's creature had come to sit by her on the grass and had not said a word about it for seven years because it could not, or because she had not asked.

And perhaps not only her. The thought came up cold and clear as the air. If the Daughter's light had done this for Ahsoka, then it had been in the room when Anakin died, wherever he had died, because the Daughter had been Anakin's too, in her way; it had been Anakin who carried her body across the floor to Ahsoka's. And Obi-Wan —

She did not know about Obi-Wan. She sat with that too, honestly. Obi-Wan had been on Mortis for the end. He had stood at the altar with the dagger in his hand while the Daughter's light went out of her and into Ahsoka, and he had never once spoken of it, and nothing of that light had gone into him. She had no thread that ran from the Daughter to him. She would not make one up. She had been Fulcrum for ten years and the first rule of Fulcrum had been that you did not report a thing you wanted to be true.

"I don't know about him," she said to the bird. "I'm not going to pretend I do."

The bird closed its eyes, slowly, and opened them.

The sun came over the edge of the district and the roof went gold, and she was very cold, and small, and it was the first morning in seven years that she had not been alone up there.

* * *

She wrote nothing at the drop. She went to it at the hour they had agreed for the day after a night like the last one, when the Room was emptiest, and counted the doors from the east and the waterfalls from the door, and went in behind the third, and he was already there.

He was sitting with his back to the rock where Obi-Wan had sat. He looked tired in the way the body looked tired, the fifteen-year-old's way, grey under the eyes and the braid coming loose, and he looked up when she came through the water and started to say something easy, and stopped, because of her face.

"What."

"The bird came and sat with me," she said. "This morning. Close enough to touch."

He waited.

"It's her," said Ahsoka.

She watched him take it. She watched him go back through the years to the room with the dais and the Father and the Son, and she saw him find the body on the floor and himself lifting it and the light going from one girl into the other, and she saw the moment he understood, because his face did a thing she had seen it do under a helmet and had not thought she would ever see it do again, and it was not a thing she had words for.

"Then why me?" he said. His voice had cracked, the boy's way, and he did not seem to notice. "I was there when she died."

"Because you were there."

The water went on. He put the heel of his hand against one eye and held it there.

"I don't know how," she said. "I don't know what it is or what it wants or if it wants anything. I don't know why now and not some other year. I don't know if it's her or just — what she left. I'm not going to tell you I know more than I do." She came and sat down on the stone, an arm's length from him, with her feet not touching the drop. "And I don't know about Obi-Wan. Obi-Wan had been in that room, and had never once spoken of it. I can't make it reach him and I'm not going to try. I'm telling you that so you don't ask."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"You were going to think it."

"Yeah." He took his hand down. His eyes were red and he was fifteen and he was forty-five and he looked at her the way he had looked at her across a hangar once, when she had come back for him and he had not deserved it. "She said — the Daughter. She didn't say anything. She never said anything. She just put her hand out."

"I know. I was the one she put it on."

He laughed, once, not well. "Snips."

"I'm not saying it makes anything better."

"No."

"I'm saying it's the first thing that's been *ours*. Not his. Not the Chancellor's. Not the Council's. Something came for us on its own and it's sitting on the roof and it won't fly." She looked at the water, grey and gold where the morning came through it. "I thought you should know before he does. He can have it tonight. I wanted one thing that was just this."

He did not say anything to that for a while. Then he reached across the arm's length and put his hand on her head, between the montrals, the way she had done to Caleb and Plo had done to her, and left it there, and his hand was too big for a boy's and shook very slightly, and she let it, because she was small, and he was there, and she had been there when the Daughter died, and so had he.

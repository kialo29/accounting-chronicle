## Chapter 26: What Remains

The chair had been carried in from the common room and set beside the bed, and Obi-Wan was in it, and Anakin lay on his back under a blanket he was too warm for and looked at the ceiling, where the traffic lanes of Coruscant drew their slow lines of light across the plaster, and did not take the wall down. It was the night Vokara Che had let Obi-Wan out of the tank for good, a week after the Council had finished with them both.

"You should be in the tank," he said.

"I have been in the tank. The tank and I have an understanding. It let me out for a few hours at a time and I came back grey and apologised. Tonight it has let me go for good, and I intend to be insufferable about it." Obi-Wan's left arm was in the harness sling still, and the dressing over his side made a square under the tunic; he sat the way a man sits who has been told not to sit, upright and careful and a little pleased about it. He had been out of the tank six hours. He should not have been awake. "Go to sleep, Anakin."

"You don't know what's in there."

"I have some idea." A pause, dry. "I have had, for four years, a little room in my head with a door that would not open, and every night I have sat outside it. Tonight I would rather sit inside." He shifted the sling. "Whatever comes through the bond comes to me. It stops here. There will be no younglings crying on the levels below. I have done this before, for a Padawan with nightmares, when he was a boy, and he was never the wiser."

"That was a boy."

"Yes," Obi-Wan said. "It was."

Anakin's hands lay on the blanket, two of them, both flesh, narrow and too big for the wrists, the hands of fifteen. He had not slept unshielded since the night thirty-one children across the Temple had dreamed his stair and his boots and his circle of chairs, and the healers had come, and the Council had begun to hunt a shadow. Every night since, he had gone to sleep inside the wall, the old scarred thing that Vokara Che had read like a scar on a hand, and he had woken each morning with his jaw aching from holding it, and gone on. A man can live like that. He had lived like that for twenty-three years inside a worse thing than a wall.

The bond was open between them. It had been open since the ledge behind the third waterfall, when there had been nothing left to hide it for, and the openness was its own strangeness: Obi-Wan's presence at the back of his mind was not the thin, patient, worried thing it had been for five months, nor the vast closed grief it had been on the other side of a cracked helmet. It was warm. It was tired. It was waiting, as a man waits in a chair.

"If you look away," Anakin said, "I'll know."

"I shan't look away."

He let the wall down.

It was not like taking off armour. He knew what that was like, and it was nothing; the armour never came off. It was like the catch of the mask, the one at the back, the one only the droids and the tank-room could reach, giving under a hand that was not his own. The first thing that came through was the room: the chair, the light, the dressing, the tea gone cold on the shelf, a man's breath careful around a lung. The second thing was Obi-Wan, closer than a voice. And then he was fifteen and growing and had been awake for nineteen hours and the body, which was a boy's body and did what a boy's body does, took him down into the dark like a hand on the back of the neck.

* * *

*The table was cold through what was left of his back.*

*They had not put him under. He understood later that this had been decided, and by whom, and why; at the time he understood only that there was a ceiling very far up, and lights in it, and that the droids were working at the ends of him where the ends were, and that the sound coming out of his own throat was not a scream because there was not enough of his throat for a scream. The legs went on first. He felt them go on: the sockets seated over the bone, the pins driven, the nerves found and taken, each one lighting like a fuse. Then the arm. Then the thing that went down into his chest, the two thick tubes that found what the fire had left of his lungs and fixed themselves there, and from that moment he did not breathe. He was breathed.*

*He heard it before he understood it. A sound from inside his own chest, a mechanical draw, a long rising hiss and a drop, and then again, the same, and again, the same, and it was in him and it was not his, and it would never be his, and it would be the last sound in every room he ever stood in for the rest of his life. The droids lifted the mask. He saw the inside of it come down: black, moulded, the two lenses, the grille, a thing like the inside of a helmet made for a face that no longer existed. It came down over him and seated with a sound like a door, and the lenses lit, and the world went red.*

*A voice at the edge of the table said his new name. He asked for his wife. The voice told him what he had done to her.*

*He took the room apart. The droids went into the walls; the table bent under him; the ceiling lights went one after another in a line, and the voice stood in the middle of it, in the one still place, and watched him with something that was not pity and was not fear, that was, he understood later, possession. When he was done he hung in the straps and the sound in his chest went on, draw and drop, draw and drop, regular as a clock, and nothing he had done to the room had touched it.*

* * *

The chair did not move. Through the open bond, as clear as the lights on the ceiling, came the fact of a man sitting very still in a chair in the dark, with his good hand closed on the arm of it, and his eyes open, and his eyes on the bed.

* * *

*The tank at the castle was the only place he was ever out of the suit.*

*He had had it built over the river. Not near it: over it, the fortress rooted into the black rock of the bank with the lava a long way below, so that from the tank-room's single tall window, if he turned his head in the bacta, he could see the bend where the crust had a particular shape, where the slope ran down from the landing platform to the water, where a man had once lain and burned. He had had the window placed. He had stood with the architect and the plans and said: there. The architect had not asked why.*

*The bacta was warm and the breathing-tube ran down his throat and the mask hung on its arm beside the tank with its lenses dark, and he floated, and his skin, what was left of it, hurt. It never stopped hurting. The burns had never healed and would never heal; they had been sealed, which was different, and under the armour every day the sealed places itched and pulled and wept, and he could not reach them, and no one could, and the tank was the only hour in which anything touched him that was not metal. He did not use it for that. He used it for the window.*

*He would turn his head in the fluid and look down the slope and find the place and lie there and look at it. The lava went by, slow and red. He did not look away. He fed it. He went down there with his eyes every day and lay on the bank again and felt the heat come up through the ground and the fire reach his hair, and the man above him on the slope with his face wet, and he took that and put it into the thing he kept in his chest beside the machine, and the thing grew, and he got out of the tank and let the droids put the mask back on.*

* * *

Obi-Wan did not move. Something came along the bond, from the chair, and it was not pity, which Anakin would have known how to hate; it was a man watching a window be placed and understanding, with twenty years' grief, exactly where.

* * *

*The Grand Inquisitor had worn yellow once. He had stood at the Council tower doors with a pike and a mask, and said nothing, and let the Masters go by; the man who told Vader this told it as if it should matter to him. The Pau'an was tall and grey and had filed teeth and red lines on his face and a way of speaking that was almost courtly, almost Dooku's, and he hated the Jedi with the thoroughness of a man who had been one of their furniture. Vader disliked him and used him. He used all of them. They were a pack of damaged children with red blades, and when they failed he broke their arms in the training room and let the droids set them.*

*The moon had rain. He remembered the rain on the lenses, and the way it ran, and the sound it made on the armour. The Jedi was a woman of about forty, a Knight who had been a Knight for six years when the Order ended, and she had been there eleven years as a well-digger and a healer of animals, and a family had taken her in, and when the shuttle came down in their field she came out of the house first, so that they would not. He respected that. He remembered respecting it, in the half-second it was given room.*

*She had a saber. She lit it. She was good; she had kept up her forms in a barn, alone, for eleven years, and it showed in her feet. It took him eleven seconds. One overhand stroke, which she took, and which drove her a step back into the mud; a second, which she took; the third she could not take, because she had been trained to parry a Jedi and he was not one, and the blade went through her guard and through her and she sat down in the rain. The Grand Inquisitor, at his shoulder, made a small sound of appreciation, the sound a man makes at a clean cut of meat. The family stood in the doorway. He let them stand. He had no orders about them and no interest, and that, too, was efficiency.*

*That was what it felt like. Not rage. Rage was for the table. This was clean. This was a thing done fast and well with nothing wasted, and in the cleanness of it there was a kind of rest, because for eleven seconds there was only the work, and no bank, and no river, and no woman's throat under his hand. He went back to the shuttle in the rain. He slept, afterward, if what the suit allowed was sleep, and did not dream.*

* * *

In the chair, the man who had taught him that third stroke's parry, once, in a dojo when he was eleven and his wrists were too thin, sat with his eyes open and watched a woman sit down in the mud. Nothing came along the bond that Anakin could have named. He was asleep. He knew, the way the sleeping know, that Obi-Wan had not looked away.

* * *

*The Archives were lit blue.*

*They were always lit blue: the holobooks in their stacks, tier on tier, a cold undersea light that had been the colour of every evening of his boyhood that he had spent in here failing to be a scholar. He came in through the main doors with the 501st at his back, their armour marked in his blue, and the light fell on the white of them and on the black of him, and the archivist droids turned from their shelves and asked, in their mild voices, whether he required assistance.*

*The row of busts ran down the centre aisle. The Lost Twenty. Bronze faces of Jedi who had left the Order of their own will, which was the Order's way of saying it forgave them a little. Dooku's was at the end. He had killed Dooku days before, on his knees, with two blades, at the suggestion of a voice at his shoulder; he walked past the bronze of the old man's face and did not slow and felt nothing, and noted that he felt nothing, and was pleased.*

*A Padawan came down the stairs between the stacks. A Twi'lek boy, sixteen perhaps, green-skinned, lekku bound back for archive duty, with a datapad still in one hand and a Knight's saber in the other, a saber too heavy for him, that he had taken off a body somewhere in the lower halls on his way here, because the Temple was burning and someone had to hold the Archives. He saw the blue markings. He saw the black figure at the head of them. He had not been told. He came down three more steps with his face opening, and the saber lowering, because he knew that armour and he knew those markings and he thought help had come.*

*He said a name. Not the one the voice had given at the table; the other one. He said it the way a youngling says a Knight's name, with the title in front of it, and relief in it, and he was four steps from the bottom.*

*He was not relieved for long. It was one stroke, from above, with the whole weight of the arm, and the boy and the datapad and the borrowed saber went down the four steps together and lay at the bottom in the blue light, and the clone sergeant at his side said, Sir, and he said, Secure the vault, and went on, and the archivist droid behind him said, in its mild voice, that the stairs would need to be cleaned.*

* * *

The chair did not move. Anakin, asleep, felt the man in it hold himself in it, felt it in the back of his own mind like a hand gripping the arm of a chair until the knuckles show; and felt him stay. The light on the ceiling went on drawing its lines. Below the quarters, on all the levels of the Temple, children slept, and nothing reached them.

* * *

*The hunter said the name standing up.*

*He had come aboard with his report and his price, a man in green Mandalorian armour that was a dead man's armour, the son of the man who had worn it before him, who had worn it once on a tower on Oba Diah with a recorder running in the helmet; this son had been six years old then. He was not six now. He said that he had found the pilot. He said that the pilot had got away from him on Tatooine, which was a thing Vader did not forgive and did not, this once, hear, because the hunter went on, and said the name.*

*Skywalker.*

*He had not heard it said aloud in nineteen years. He had not said it. The voice at the table had taken it from him and he had let it go into the fire with everything else, and here it was in a hunter's mouth, attached to a boy who flew.*

*The room was a Star Destroyer's briefing bay with a viewport. He did not take the room apart; he was past that. He stood very still, and the sound in his chest went on, draw and drop, and inside him something he had kept twenty years in the dark put its hand on a wall that was not there any more. Because it meant she had lived. It meant she had lived long enough. It meant there had been a child, and someone had taken the child, and had hidden the child on the one world in the galaxy he would never go back to, and had known he would never go back to it, and had known exactly why. There was one man who could have done that. He saw the slope. He saw the wet face. He had thought the hate was finished with that man and found it was only beginning.*

*And beneath it, far down, where he did not go: a boy. Alive. Flying his ship. With his name.*

* * *

Something came along the bond from the chair. It was not a word; the bond had never carried words. It was the shape of a man on a ridge above a farm in the desert, watching a child walk between the vaporators, year after year, and never going down; and it was the shape of that man understanding, now, in a chair on Coruscant, what the name had done when it was said. He did not move. He did not look away. Anakin, asleep, felt him not look away.

* * *

*Bespin was orange. The freezing chamber, the steam, the pit where the carbonite came up; he had built it for the boy and tested it on the smuggler, and the smuggler had gone down into it with his eyes open. He remembered that. He had not expected to.*

*Then the boy, with the blue blade. His blade; the one Obi-Wan had picked up off the bank and carried for nineteen years, in the boy's hand now, and the boy using it badly, with heart, the way a boy uses a thing that is too heavy for him. He let it go on. He wanted to see. He drove the boy back through the chamber and out along the gantry and into the dark where the wind came up the shaft, and the boy fought, and the boy was fast, and the boy was not enough, and had never been going to be enough. He had known that since the first exchange. He drew it out because he could not stop looking.*

*Then he was tired of looking, and the blade went through the boy's wrist.*

*It was not a hard stroke. It did not need to be. The hand went away with the saber in it into the wind and the dark and the boy made the sound a boy makes and went back along the gantry to the end of it, to where there was nothing but a rail and the shaft, and held his wrist to his chest, and looked at him.*

*He told him. He had not planned to tell him. He had planned to bring him in whole and let the voice decide, and instead he stood on the gantry with the wind in the cloak and told the boy who his father was, in the voice the machine gave him, and watched the boy's face. Luke's face. He had not known the name until that year. Luke's face took it in, and he watched it go through the boy the way the voice at the table had watched the room come apart, and the boy said no, and said it again, and the boy's eyes were hers.*

*He held out his hand. The good one; the black one. He said what he could offer. The boy looked at the hand, and looked at him, and let go of the rail.*

*He went to the edge and looked down. The boy fell a long way. He was very small by the time the wind took him. Vader stood at the rail of the gantry with the cloak going out behind him and the sound in his chest, draw and drop, draw and drop, and looked down into the shaft where his son had chosen to go rather than be touched by him, and did not move, and did not move, and did not move.*

* * *

Obi-Wan put his hand on the bed.

It was the good hand, the right; it came down on the blanket near Anakin's shin, not on him, on the blanket, and it stayed there. It was the only time all night that the chair moved. It came through the bond as plainly as the ceiling, a man who had hidden that child, who had sat on a ridge above him for nineteen years and had died to get him off that world, watching him let go of a rail, and reaching out, with the one hand that worked, for the nearest thing he could reach.

* * *

*What the hate was for.*

*It was for the table. It was so that when the droids seated the legs he had something to be instead of what was being done to him. It was for the itch of the sealed skin under the armour that no hand could reach, the lungs that did not draw, the lenses that made every colour red, so that the body was a thing he hated rather than a thing he was. It was for the bank: for the slope and the heat coming up through the ground and the wet face above him and the words said down the slope, so that he could lie on the bank every day in the tank and not die of it. It was for her throat. It was for the sound her breath had made under his hand on the landing platform, and her eyes, and her falling; it was so that he never had to stand in that again, because the hate stood there for him, and told him it was Obi-Wan's doing, and he let it. It was for the stair, and the blue light, and the four steps, and the Council chamber, and the faces at the height of his belt. It was a floor. It was a floor laid over the lava, and he walked on it for twenty-three years and never once looked down, because looking down was the fire.*

*And what it bought.*

*Nothing. He laid it out, in the dark where the sleeping lay things out, and it was nothing. It had not brought her back. It had not brought the children back. It had not made the lungs draw. It had killed Jedi who would have died anyway and some who would not, and it had killed a boy on the stairs who thought help had come, and it had served a voice that had never once, in twenty-three years, said a true thing to him, and it had put him on a gantry over a shaft watching the only clean thing he had ever made choose the fall. It had bought the Emperor an instrument. It had bought the instrument a tank with a window. Everything else it had cost.*

*And at the end, in a corridor on a thing the size of a moon, with the lightning in him and the boy on the floor, it had not been the hate that moved. He had looked for it and it had not been there. Something else had been there, something very old, that had been under the floor the whole time, the whole twenty-three years, in the fire, not burned. He had picked the voice up. He had carried it to the edge. The boy had taken the mask off him and he had seen with his own eyes, and the eyes had been hers, and the hate had bought him nothing, and the nothing had been enough.*

* * *

The light on the ceiling had gone from the slow lanes of night to the thick grey wash of early morning, and Anakin lay under it, awake, and did not know for a moment that he was; and then he knew, because his jaw did not ache. He had slept. He had slept nine hours without the wall and the Temple below him had not cried out, and he was in his own narrow bed in a body that was fifteen and growing and had a cramp in one calf, and both his hands were on the blanket, and one of them was a finger's breadth from where another hand had been all night and was still.

He turned his head.

Obi-Wan was in the chair. He had not moved from it. The sling was where it had been; the good hand was on the blanket. He had not slept; it was in the grey of him, in the way the lines at the eyes had gone deep and stayed, in the mouth. He looked, in that light, like a man of sixty. He looked ten years older than the man who had sat down in the dark, and the man who had sat down had already been carrying four years and a tower on Oba Diah and a list of the dead. He looked like the man on the ridge. He looked like a man who had sat all night in a room with the door open and had not once gone out.

"I saw," Obi-Wan said.

His voice had very little in it. It was not the dry voice. It was not the voice that said *my friend*. It was a voice that had been used to hold a thing up all night and had nothing left over for shape.

"I know," said Anakin.

The traffic began outside, the first lanes of the day, and the light moved on the ceiling, and neither of them moved. Anakin lay and looked at his master's face, and his master's face did not do anything. It did not close. It did not arrange itself. He had seen that face arrange itself a thousand times in two lifetimes, for Councils and Chancellors and Senators and him, and it was not doing it now; it was only grey, and tired, and there, with its eyes open.

He had wanted two things, for five months, since he had woken in this bed. He had wanted to keep anyone from becoming what he had been. And he had wanted never again to be looked at the way he had been looked at from the top of the slope.

He had not meant to ask. He had meant to say something about tea, or the tank, or the hour. What came out came out in a voice he had not used since the braid had been new behind his ear, a boy's voice, fifteen and unsteady and thin at the top, and he heard it and could not take it back.

"Can you still look at me?"

Obi-Wan looked at him.

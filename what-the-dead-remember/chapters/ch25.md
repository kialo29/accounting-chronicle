## Chapter 25: Three

The hangar officer was waiting at the foot of the ramp with a stretcher, two healers, and the face Obi-Wan had promised, and Anakin Skywalker came down behind the stretcher with his master's lightsaber still clipped beside his own and found that of everything he had braced for in the last nine minutes, the thing he had not braced for was being fifteen in front of a man he had robbed.

Sabo Thane said nothing. He looked at the scorch along the courier's port fairing, then at the boy, then at Obi-Wan going past on the stretcher with the dressing on his side gone the colour of weak tea, and put away whatever he had been going to say. Men like Thane kept things.

The Halls of Healing smelled of bacta and warm stone. Vokara Che met them at the inner door with her lekku bound back and her sleeves already rolled, and she did not greet anyone; she put two fingers on Obi-Wan's throat and two on the dressing and said, to the room, "Tank. Now. Full immersion."

"After the Council," said Obi-Wan from the stretcher.

"The Council can come here."

"The Council does not come anywhere, Vokara, that is rather the point of them." He had his eyes shut. His face had gone the grey of old mortar under the beard, and he was speaking in the voice he used for customs officials and the Chancellor's aides, light and courteous and immovable. "A patch. Something for the pain that will leave me able to count. Three hours. Then you may pickle me for as long as you like."

Che looked at him for a long moment, and then she looked at Anakin, standing by the door with his hands at his sides and his master's saber on his hip, and Anakin felt the look go over him the way her hands had gone through his dream months ago, slow and professional, finding the shape of a thing without needing its name.

"Days," she said. "You understand me. The bolt took part of the lung and the shoulder is held together with my goodwill. You will be in that tank for days, Master Kenobi, and you will be weak for a month after, and if you stand in front of the Council for three hours this morning you are doing it on stubbornness and a stim and I will note that in your file."

"Note it," said Obi-Wan. "I have a very full file."

* * *

The High Council Chamber had not changed. Anakin had known that it would not, and he had still walked in through the tall doors expecting something: a mark on the floor, a scorch on the sill. There was nothing. Round room, twelve chairs, the sky all the way around through the tall windows, Coruscant in its morning haze, and the light lying across the floor in the same long bars it had lain in when he had come through those doors with the 501st at his back and found eight children behind the chairs.

He did not look at the chairs. He stood at the centre with his hands behind him and his feet together and his shoulders in the shape a Padawan's shoulders were supposed to make, and he let his Force presence down into the floor as far as it would go, and he waited to be asked questions.

Not all the chairs were filled. Yoda was there, small in the big chair, his hands folded over the head of the stick. Windu was there. Plo Koon was there with his mask catching the light. Ki-Adi-Mundi, Shaak Ti, two others by hologram, flickering. Obi-Wan had been given a seat, which was unheard of; Yoda had said, "Sit, you will," in a tone that was not a kindness, and Obi-Wan had sat, upright, arm strapped across his chest, and had somehow made it look like a courtesy he was extending to the Council.

"Four matters," said Windu. He did not stand. He had a datapad on his knee and did not look at it. "A courier taken from the north bay without authorisation. A Padawan absent from the Temple on a mission this Council did not assign. A Mandalorian in full armour present at what was reported to us as a routine escort. Eleven Republic surveyors, now on a Pyke vessel bound for Eriadu, telling anyone with a comm that a boy walked through a droid garrison like a door." He let that sit. "I'd like to take them in order."

"Then I will save the Council time," said Obi-Wan, "by taking them all at once. The order was mine."

It was very quiet in the chamber. Anakin kept his eyes on a point on the floor a metre in front of Master Windu's boots and listened to his master lie.

He had never heard it before. In two lives he had heard Obi-Wan deflect, evade, understate, and tell the truth in ways that left the listener believing the opposite; he had never heard him simply say a false thing to the Council's face and go on saying it. The voice did not change. The grey face and the strapped arm did the rest.

"The negotiation turned on the second day. Lom Pyke had decided that the surveyors were worth more as a lever than as a fee, and that I was worth more as a corpse than as an envoy, and the droids on the gantry were not his, Masters, they were Federation landing-craft stock with the codes filed off, which I will come to. I had the courier pilot under a six-hour order and the six hours were nearly gone. I opened a private channel to my Padawan."

"On what equipment," said Windu.

"My own. A comm I keep for the purpose, which I will surrender to the Council if it wishes to see it. I told him where I was and what I expected to happen, and I told him to take whatever ship he could get and to come, and that I would answer for the ship. He did as he was told. It is a thing he does more often than his reputation suggests." The ghost of the eyebrow. "I was shot before he arrived. I have a fairly clear memory of the floor, and a less clear one of the boy, and I am told I owe him my life. The Mandalorian was the Pykes' observer, there as a courtesy to their client, and he left when the odds turned. The surveyors are alive because my Padawan freed them and told them to take the Pyke ship, which was sensible. If the Council has an objection to the order I gave, the objection is to me."

"A Padawan on a comm order from a wounded master," said Windu, "does not, in my experience, clear forty droids."

"Then your experience of my Padawan is incomplete, Master Windu," said Obi-Wan, "which I would say is the general condition. The droids were old. Their codes were a decade stale, their targeting was poor, and he had the Force with him and a good deal of adolescent outrage. I have seen him do worse to a training remote."

Somebody — Mundi, Anakin thought — made a sound that was nearly a laugh and was not permitted to be. Windu did not turn his head.

"The surveyors say he used a stroke they'd never seen."

"The surveyors are mineral assessors, Master Windu. I would not take their word on lightsaber forms. I would take it on ore."

It went on. Windu asked about the Federation hull-codes and Obi-Wan gave him the codes from memory, which was true and which bought three minutes of real concern around the circle, because a Federation garrison on loan to the Pykes was a thing worth being concerned about and was not a boy. Windu asked who had requested Master Kenobi by name for a routine escort and Obi-Wan said, "Senator Verrane of Tyrrhus, through the Chancellor's office," and Anakin filed the name where it would stay. Windu asked about the six hours and Obi-Wan said the pilot had obeyed his order, as he should have, and would the Council like to reprimand a civilian for punctuality.

And through all of it Yoda looked at Anakin.

Not at Obi-Wan. At the boy. The old eyes rested on him the way they had rested on him on Ilum across the ice, patient, unblinking, interested, as if the boy were a stone in a stream and Yoda were watching to see which way the water went round him. Anakin kept his presence in the floor. He kept his face in the shape of a Padawan who has done a brave stupid thing and is now being talked about in the third person. He kept his hands still. He thought about speeders. He thought about Mirren Dask, who did not exist, and about his mother's handwriting, and about the sting of a training saber on the wrist, and he laid those things over the top of himself like a cloth over a cage, and Yoda looked at the cloth, and said nothing at all.

"The hangar log," said Plo Koon.

Anakin's stomach went cold and he did not let it reach his face.

Plo's voice came filtered through the mask, deep and unhurried. "It records a repulsor test on the courier under a crew chief's authorisation, entered some forty minutes before the ship left the bay. A test on a vessel which then made a hyperspace jump to Oba Diah." A pause, in which the mask turned very slightly, not toward Anakin, toward the windows. "I note it. I have no question."

Nobody else had a question either. Windu looked at Plo for exactly one second, and Anakin saw him decide not to ask the Kel Dor why not, and saw him file it, the way Thane had filed the scorch on the fairing.

"Lucky, you were," said Yoda, at last, to Obi-Wan, and then to the boy, "Luck, a poor teacher is. Hm."

"Yes, Master."

"Reprimand noted," said Windu. "Both of you. The Padawan is confined to the Temple until Master Kenobi is out of the tank, and the courier's cost comes out of Master Kenobi's stipend for the next five years, which I understand is the first expense he's incurred in ten." He stood. The others began to stand. He looked at Anakin, directly, for the first time, and said in the clipped voice, "I was wrong about you once this year, Skywalker. I'd like to know which way."

*A window. A window and purple light, and an old man's face turned up with its ruin already in it, and his own hand on his own hilt as he chose.*

"Master," said Anakin, and bowed.

He had hated Mace Windu for thirty years. He had carried it in the suit like a stone in a boot: the distrust, the appraising look in this very room, the way Windu had said *the boy* as if the word were a verdict. He had told himself it was unjust. He had told himself that a man who was trusted does not fall, and that Windu's doubt had been the first stone out of the wall.

And Windu was right. Windu had been right then, for the wrong reasons, and he was right now for reasons he could not name, and he was standing in a room where eight children had died behind the chairs looking at the thing that had killed them, and he knew it was wrong and could not prove it and would not stop trying. Anakin thought that if he were in Windu's place he would have had the boy in a cell by now. He found, bowing, that he could not hate him. It was a strange thing to lose in the Council chamber, and he had no time to look at it.

* * *

Vokara Che put Obi-Wan back in the tank the moment the Council rose, and Anakin and Ahsoka waited two days, which was exactly as long as she would allow. On the second night, at the eleventh hour, she took him out again, swearing in Ryl, because he had asked her to and because, she said, a man who could argue through a healing trance could not be healed by it and she would rather lose a few hours of bacta than the whole week's.

"You have until the third hour. Then he is back in and I am sealing the lid."

"Vokara."

"Walk slowly. Do not bend. Do not lift. If you fall, Padawan Skywalker, you will carry him back here and you will explain to me in person what you were doing in the dark with a man who has no business being out of a tank."

"Yes, Master Che."

They walked slowly. Obi-Wan was in a healer's grey robe with the arm strapped under it and he walked the way Anakin remembered walking in the first year of the suit, like a man carrying himself in a box, every step planned a step ahead. The corridors were empty. The Temple at night had its own breathing, the air-handlers and the far-off fountains and the hush of a thousand children asleep, and Anakin kept his presence low and thin and felt, faint as a thread, the other presence that had been waiting for this for two days with her hand on her chest.

"You have not told me who," said Obi-Wan, at the east door of the Room of a Thousand Fountains.

"No."

"You are going to make me find out by walking."

"You'll know before we get there."

The Room was dark and green and full of water. They went in by the east door and counted waterfalls, one, two, the path turning between ferns taller than the boy, the air wet on the face, and at the third the path went behind the water into the dripping space where the ledge was, where the moss was, where a waterproof sample box had lived under the moss for four months with two people's handwriting in it. Obi-Wan stopped before the fall. He stood very still with his good hand on the wet rock and his head slightly turned, the way he had stood in the hangar months ago when he had said there was something on that ship he was afraid of.

"Of course," he said, very quietly. "Of course it is you."

He went through the water. Anakin went after him.

She was sitting on her heels at the far end of the ledge with the spray on her montrals and her hands on her knees. Sand-coloured tunic. Brown belt. Lekku to her shoulders and no further, white markings bright in the dark, blue eyes. Small. Smaller than the box of moss beside her made her look. She had been crying at some point in the last hour and had done what she always did about it, which was to finish and wash her face. She got up when she saw him. She had to tip her head back.

"Master Kenobi."

"Ahsoka."

For a moment nobody said anything else, because there was nothing to be said that was the right size. Anakin stood with the water at his back and looked at the two of them. He had known both of them longer than anyone alive, and he had never seen them look at each other like this, because the last time they had looked at each other at all, she had been a woman of nineteen leaving the Temple by the front steps with the Council watching from the top and Obi-Wan among them, and the time after that she had been a blue flicker on a holoprojector in a Mandalorian hangar and he had been the same, a foot tall, telling her that Anakin was with the Chancellor and could not come.

A fifty-seven-year-old man in a thirty-one-year-old body, with a bolt through his lung and a beard that was not yet grey, looking down at a thirty-three-year-old woman in the body of a ten-year-old girl, who was looking up.

"You're shorter," said Ahsoka.

"I am precisely the height I was. You are smaller. You will forgive me if I do not bend; I have been expressly forbidden." His voice was not steady. He made it steady. "You will have to do the looking up, I am afraid."

"I'm ten. I look up at the furniture."

"And your beard's wrong," she said, after a moment, and her voice was not steady either. "I remembered it longer. Greyer."

"It is eleven years younger than you remember. So am I. So, for that matter, are you." He looked at her, and the look went on too long, the way his looks at the Tano child had always gone on too long, except that now he knew why, and she knew that he knew, and something in his face that had been held for four years let go. "I have been afraid of you since the first day I saw you in the initiates' corridor, child, and I never once knew what I was afraid of. I thought I was going mad. I made a note of it."

"I know," she said. "You looked at me like I'd taken something of yours."

"You had. I did not know what it was yet." He put his good hand flat on the rock, and lowered himself, slowly, badly, with his jaw tight, until he was sitting against the wall of the ledge with his legs out in front of him, and when Anakin moved to help he was waved off without a glance. "The last time I saw you, you were walking down the Temple steps. I was at the top. I did not go after you. I have been meaning to say that for some years."

"The last time I saw you, you were blue, and you told me he was busy."

"He was."

"I know what he was busy with."

"Yes," said Obi-Wan. "I rather think we all do."

Anakin had meant to say something at this point. He had thought about it in the tank's anteroom for two days, what the right thing would be, and he had decided on something short, and when he opened his mouth to say it, what came out was: "She was my Padawan," and on the last word his voice went up and cracked, the whole fifteen-year-old register of it, a boy's voice breaking on the one word he had wanted to say like a man.

Ahsoka put her face in her hands. Obi-Wan closed his eyes.

"I'm aware," he said. "I was there."

"I'm going to kill you both."

"You have already killed me, my friend, and I did not care for it. Sit down. We have very little time and Master Che has threatened to seal the lid."

* * *

The three lists came out of three pockets, and lay on the wet stone between them, and for a moment nobody touched them.

Anakin's was the datapad, scratched, its screen showing a column of characters that were not Aurebesh and were not any cipher the Archives held, because the key to them was the serial number of a respirator unit that would not be built for seven years. Ahsoka's was the grey flimsi, folded to the softness of cloth, eight lines of Togruti and the lines under them, and the mark beside the sixth that Anakin had never asked about. Obi-Wan's was a single sheet, folded twice, and he laid it face down on the rock and kept his hand on it.

"Not yet," he said. "You know what it is. Forty-one names and where each of them falls. I will show it to you when there is something to be done about it. Tonight there is not."

"Rex isn't on it," said Ahsoka.

"Rex is a clone, my dear. The list is of Jedi."

"Rex is alive," said Anakin. "On Kamino. He's six. He looks twelve."

Obi-Wan looked at him. Then he looked at the girl, and something in the look sharpened, because he had spent four years watching this child hold a training saber backwards and had never once thought to ask himself what she had seen that had taught her to hold her tongue.

"Tell me," he said, "what you know that I do not. Quickly. Start with the thing that will hurt most; it saves time."

"What do you think happened on Utapau," said Ahsoka.

"I know what happened on Utapau. The order came. They obeyed it. Cody handed me my lightsaber, which I had dropped, and said something about the next time, and ten minutes later his gunner put a shell into the ledge I was standing on." He said it in the customs-officer voice. "I have had nineteen years to think about it. They were soldiers; soldiers obey. I do not hold it against him. I find I cannot quite forgive him either, which is a failing in me and not in him."

"He didn't choose."

"Ahsoka—"

"He didn't *choose*." She had got up onto her knees to say it, which brought her face level with his. "They had chips. Here." She touched her own temple, under the montral. "Organic inhibitor chips, implanted at the embryo stage. The Kaminoans were told they were a safeguard against rogue Jedi, in case any of us turned, and they put one in every clone they grew. One order, one code, and the chip takes the man. Fives found it. A trooper of the 501st, in the third year of the war; he found it in his own head and he got as far as Coruscant to tell someone, and they shot him in a warehouse with Anakin standing over him. I had Rex's cut out of his skull on a Venator with a medical droid while the rest of his brothers were trying to kill us both. I watched him come back. He cried, Master Kenobi. He was crying when he came round, and he kept saying the names of the men who'd just tried to kill him, and he did not know for a moment whether he had done it or they had. Cody didn't choose. None of them chose. It was done to them before they were born."

Obi-Wan did not say anything.

He was already sitting; there was nowhere further down to go. But Anakin watched him go there anyway. He watched the straight back come off the rock wall and bow, slowly, around the strapped arm, as if something that had held his spine up for nineteen years had been quietly taken out of it, and watched the good hand come off the list on the stone and go to his face, and stay there.

"He handed me my lightsaber," said Obi-Wan, through his hand, after a long time.

"I know."

"I thought— he looked at me. Before. He looked at me and I have thought, every day since, that there was a thing in his face. A decision." The hand came down. The eyes were wet and the voice was very dry. "There was not. There was a man with a knife in his head."

"Yes."

"Thank you," said Obi-Wan, "for the order of business," and laughed, which hurt him, and stopped.

Then it was his turn. Kamino: the planet that was not in the Archives because Dooku had taken it out with a dead man's codes; Sifo-Dyas, who had ordered the army in the year Qui-Gon died and been killed for it the same year on Oba Diah, by the Pykes, for Dooku; the Pykes keeping the lightsaber as a trophy; and the tower room where Obi-Wan had sat across from Lom Pyke and felt, with the part of him that had spent twenty years learning to feel such things, that something had been taken out of that room very recently, something that had sat in it a long time. "I would wager the saber. I would wager it went to Dooku. I would wager Dooku did not want it for sentiment."

"And Verrane," said Anakin.

"Caius Verrane of Tyrrhus asked for me by name for a routine escort. Senators do not ask for Jedi by name unless someone has given them the name. It came through the Chancellor's office." He looked at Anakin. "You were to be alone with him on the fourth day I was gone."

"I was."

"Tell me about the wall."

Anakin told him. Not all of it; there was not time for all of it. He told him that the Chancellor had walked in his head once every ten days since he was nine, and that the night he had woken in this body the garden had closed and a wall had come down behind it, old and scarred, built over twenty-three years by a man who had learned shielding from the Sith themselves, and that Vokara Che had put her hands through his fake dream and found it and said so, and that the Chancellor had found it too, twenty-two days before he said a word, and had pressed nothing and smiled and said the boy looked tired. "He thinks somebody's had me. Someone who knows the discipline. He thinks it's Dooku. He's been watching Dooku to see if Dooku knows."

"He does not consider that you came back," said Obi-Wan.

"Nobody considers it," said Ahsoka. "We didn't."

"At the reception," said Anakin, "Dooku touched me. He went through the surface like it wasn't there, straight to the wall, and he put his hand on it, and I hated him. For one second. All of it, Geonosis and the arm and the shuttle and the kneeling, the whole thing, aimed at him, and he felt it, and he couldn't understand where it had come from, because he's never met me. And the Chancellor was ten metres away watching him feel it."

"I saw his face change," said Obi-Wan slowly. "Across the gallery. I thought it was the wine."

"And now the helmet."

Nobody needed that one told. They had all three seen the shape of it. A Mandalorian hunter on a gantry forty levels up, in a helmet that recorded everything, watching a fifteen-year-old Padawan walk through a garrison of droids with a single-handed overhand stroke that had never been taught in this Temple, and close his fist, and lift a Pyke off the floor by the throat without touching him. That recording had left Oba Diah before the courier had. It had been with Dooku for two days now, and everything Dooku had went to his master.

"So," said Ahsoka. She was sitting back on her heels, small, with the list in her lap. "They know. Not what. But they know he's wrong. Before tonight they thought somebody had got to him. Now they've seen him fight."

"And the Council," said Obi-Wan, "is hunting a shadow in the Temple. Thirty-one children woke screaming on the same night and the centre of it was his level, and Master Yoda has felt it, and Master Windu is looking for a stain, and the stain was standing in the middle of the chamber two days ago with its hands behind its back, and he looked straight at it and reprimanded it for stealing a ship."

"He's right not to trust me."

"He is entirely right. He is right about everything except what it is. Which is the Council's condition, my friend, and always was; I sat on it." Obi-Wan looked at the two lists on the stone and at his own, face down under his hand. "So. The Sith know the boy is wrong and are closing from one side. The Council knows something is wrong and is closing from the other. There are three of us who know what, and one of us is a child the Council will not hear, and one is a Padawan the Council will not trust, and one is a Master who has just lied to it, fluently, and been believed."

"With reservations."

"With reservations. Windu does not believe a word. He also cannot break it, and he knows that, and it will keep him up at night, which is good for him." He drew a breath, carefully, around the lung. "Plo Koon saw the hangar log and chose not to see it."

"I know," said Ahsoka, very quietly. "I'll have to tell him something."

"Tell him you were frightened for a boy. It is what Master Yoda told the Council about Ilum. It has the merit of being true."

Above them the fall went on falling, and the spray beaded on the datapad's screen, on the grey flimsi, on the back of Obi-Wan's hand where it lay over his dead. Beyond the water a Temple Guard's boots went by on the main path, and the three of them went still until they had gone, the way children go still.

Anakin looked at them. His master, grey and bowed against the rock, holding a list of the dead he would not yet show. His Padawan, ten years old, face washed, eyes very steady on him, as they had been through a cracked black mask the last time she had looked up at him from this near. The two people he had loved most and failed worst, who had both seen only the monster, and were both here, on a wet ledge behind a waterfall, lying to everyone they knew.

"So we're all lying to everyone," he said.

Obi-Wan opened his eyes.

"Yes," he said. "Try to do it well."

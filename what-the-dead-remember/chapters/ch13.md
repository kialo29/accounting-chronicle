## Chapter 13: Ilum

The *Crucible* sat on the hangar floor like something the Temple had forgotten it owned, and Anakin Skywalker stood under its wing at the fifth hour with six children shouting around his knees and tried to remember whether it had looked this old the first time.

It had. He had been ten and it had been the oldest thing he had ever seen that still flew, and he had wanted to take the engines apart before they had cleared the atmosphere. Huyang had not let him. Huyang stood now at the foot of the ramp with his hands folded in front of his copper belly, the way he had stood at the foot of the ramp for a thousand years, and looked at the six younglings of Hawkbat Clan with the patient dismay of a droid who had seen ten thousand of them and expected every one to be sick on the way.

"Boots," Huyang said. "All of you. Show me."

Six pairs of boots were shown. Tam Dorreth's were on the wrong feet, which was a thing an eleven-year-old boy could do while discussing, at the top of his voice, whether the crystals were alive. Brannick Sel had his head-tails bundled inside a scarf because someone had told him Ilum would freeze them off; the Nautolan boy's eyes were enormous with the injustice of weather. Wix, the Ithorian, said something through both sides of his mouth that Huyang answered in Ithorese without looking round. Ilyo, the Rodian girl, had already got her kit bag open on the floor and was counting what was in it with a green finger. Kessa Ruun stood with her hands in her sleeves the way Master Sen stood, because Kessa did everything the way someone else did it, and beside her, a head shorter, the Tano child stood with her hands at her sides and looked at the ship.

Not at him. At the ship. Anakin had been counting, since the night of the bell, how often the girl looked at him, and he had noticed that she looked at him most when she had decided he was not looking at her. He let her have the ship.

Knight Sabo Thane came across the floor with a datapad and the expression of a man who had wanted this launch gone by the fourth hour. "Padawan Skywalker. Master Yoda's aboard. Master Kenobi's at the stair."

Anakin turned. Obi-Wan was at the top of the hangar stair with his hands behind his back, in the brown robe, as if he had come down for something else and happened to be there. He came down slowly. He looked at the children, each one, the way he looked at a room before he went into it, and then he looked at Anakin, and the bond, thin as Anakin had kept it, carried a thing across that Anakin could not have put a word to and did not try.

"They let you off the penance, then," Obi-Wan said.

"Master Drallig said he'd seen enough of the stroke to last him."

"I imagine he had." Obi-Wan's eyes went past him to the ramp, to the small shapes on it, and stayed a moment longer on the smallest. "Watch the ice at the door. It is quicker than it looks."

"I've been."

"You have." A pause, in which the older man seemed to weigh something and put it down again. "Bring them all home, my friend."

It was the sort of thing a master said at a hangar. It was not the way he said it. Anakin had sixty years of Obi-Wan's voice in him, counting both lives, and he knew the sound of a sentence that had been edited on the way out. He waited. Obi-Wan did not go on. He put a hand on Anakin's shoulder, briefly, the way he had taken to doing, and turned and went back up the stair without looking round, and Anakin stood with his shoulder warm and watched him go and thought: *you are afraid of something on that ship, and it is not me.*

"Padawan," Huyang said. "If you have finished being seen off."

* * *

The *Crucible* left the atmosphere at the sixth hour and the children were sick, as Huyang had foreseen, in the order he had foreseen, Tam first and loudest. Anakin held the boy's forehead over the refresher basin and felt, through the hull, the ancient ship find the lane and go to lightspeed, and the cabin tilted a degree and righted and the stars went to lines in the port.

"Better out than in," Tam said, wiping his mouth. "Master Sen says."

"Master Sen's right."

"Did you get sick? When you went?"

"No," Anakin said, which was true; he had been a boy who had flown pod-racers, and the *Crucible* had seemed to him then like being carried about in a cupboard. He wiped Tam's chin with the boy's own sleeve and sent him back up the passage, where Ilyo was explaining to Brannick in a carrying whisper that if you were sick in hyperspace it never came down.

The ship had one long cabin with the forge in the middle of it, cold now, and benches along the sides, and a round port at the back where the children sat in a heap to watch the lines go by. Anakin walked the length of it twice because his legs wanted walking and because the body he had was fifteen and had not learned to sit still. On the second pass he stopped by the forge. The housing clamps were the same. The gimbal was the same. Someone had rewired the heat stage, badly, perhaps a century ago.

"You wanted to open that," Huyang said behind him, "the first time. You stood where you are standing and you said the heat stage was inefficient. You were ten."

"It is inefficient."

"It is older than your Order's charter, young man, and it has made more sabers than your Order has had Knights." The droid's head tilted; the lenses adjusted. "I remember your crystal. Blue. You cut two housings for it because the first was not good enough, and you would not let me help you with the second, and you carried the first about in your pocket for a tenday afterwards as if it were a dead pet."

He had forgotten the first housing. He stood and let the memory come up and found it whole: the weight of it, the burr he had not been able to file off, the ache of having made a thing badly. He had made a great many things since. He had made a saber in the dark on Mustafar with his teeth clenched and the housing black and no one to help him, and he had not kept the first of those either.

"You remember them all?" he said.

"I remember the ones who came back." Huyang turned to the children. "Sit, all of you. Sit. Master Yoda will speak, and you will listen, or I will have the six of you polishing the forge until Ilum."

Yoda had been in the forward seat with his eyes shut since the hangar. He opened them now and came down the cabin on his stick, and the six younglings arranged themselves on the deck in front of the forge in a row, by height, which put the Tano girl on the end, and Anakin took the bench behind them and folded his long hands and made himself small.

The lesson was the one Anakin remembered. The crystal was not found; it called. One did not go into the caves to look for a thing but to be found by it. The caves would show each child what it needed to see, and each child must see it and go on, and the crystal was at the end of the going on, not at the end of the seeing. Yoda said all this in fewer words than Anakin would have used, and the children listened with their mouths open, and Tam asked whether a crystal could call the wrong person and Yoda said, "Hmm. The wrong person, is there?" and Tam thought about it so hard his face went red.

"Afraid, are you?" Yoda asked them, generally.

Five said no. The Tano child said nothing. Kessa, beside her, put a hand on her arm, and the girl let it stay there, and Anakin saw her let it, and saw the care in the letting, and wrote it in the list in his head under the long column of things that were different.

"Good," Yoda said, to the five. "Lie to me, you do. Good lies, for children." The ears went down. "In the ice, no lies. What you fear, the ice will show. What you are, the ice will show. Choose, then, what to carry out." He let it sit. "Rest now. Two days, Ilum."

The children went to their bunks in a mob and Anakin stayed where he was and found that the old eyes had come round to him and were not moving on.

He had expected it. He had been expecting it since the Council put Kenobi's Padawan on the manifest, and he had built against it: a surface Yoda could walk on without falling through, a boy of fifteen who was pleased to be going and anxious to do it well and thinking, underneath, about a girl in a politics class. He laid it out now and sat behind it and waited.

Yoda did not walk on it. He only looked. The look went on long enough that Anakin heard the engines change pitch in it, and his own pulse, and a child laughing through the bunk-room wall. It was not a probe. He would have felt a probe; he had felt Sidious's, every tenth day, like a hand on the back of the neck. This was a very old person looking at a young one, and it was worse.

"Changed, you are," Yoda said. "Grown."

"I'm fifteen, Master. It's what we do."

"Hmm." The stick shifted. "Grown, I said. Not taller." The ears lifted a fraction. "Eleven, you were, when I took you to the ice. Angry. Angry at the ship, at the cold, at the crystal for being slow. A boy who thought the galaxy a thing to be fixed." A pause. "Not angry now."

"I'm older."

"Older." Yoda said the word as if he were turning it over for a hallmark. "Grieved, you have. For whom, I do not ask." The old eyes held him. "But careful, be. A man who has grieved, the ice knows. More than a boy, it will ask of you."

Then he turned, and went up the cabin on his stick, and Anakin sat behind his false surface with his heart going and realised that none of what Yoda had said had been for the Council. Some of it had been for the Order, perhaps; the long look had been the Order's. But the last had been the old creature's own, and it had been kind, and it had gone in under the surface without touching it, the way a knife goes in under a nail.

* * *

Ilum came up white in the port on the second morning and the children fell silent for the first time since Coruscant.

Anakin had seen it from the dark side of the ship the last time, as Vader, in the last year of the war, when the Empire had cut the planet open with mining lasers and the sky had been full of ore barges. He had stood on a bridge and felt the crystals scream and had not cared. He looked at it now with a boy's eyes, whole, blue-white, a ring of weather at the pole, and felt the thing in his chest that the body did at such moments, which was to go hot and tight and fill his eyes. He blinked it clear. Brannick had his face flat against the port.

Huyang put them down in the valley below the temple as the sun was coming over the ridge. The cold hit as the ramp went down, a cold like a struck bell, and six children stopped as one on the lip of the ramp and then Tam went down it with a shout and the rest followed. Anakin went last with Yoda. His breath smoked. He had forgotten the feeling of cold on a face. Twenty-three years without weather, and now this, which went in at the nose and hurt.

The temple was cut into the mountain, a face of carved ice the height of a Coruscant tower, and across its door hung the waterfall, frozen hard, a sheet of grey ice thick as a wall. The children stood in front of it in a row in their sand-coloured tunics and looked up and up. Wix said something, and Ilyo said, "He says it's bigger than he thought," and Wix said something else, and she said, "He says that's all he's going to say about it."

Yoda went up the steps. He put his stick against the floor of the vestibule and shut his eyes, and the children shut theirs because he had, and Anakin, who knew what came next, kept his open.

The sun came over the ridge. It struck the great ice lens above the door and came through it as a bar of light and lay on the frozen waterfall like a hand, and the waterfall began to run. It began at the top, a thread of water, and the thread became a sheet and the sheet came down with a noise like a crowd, and behind it the door stood open and black and breathing cold.

Anakin, whose eyes were open, saw it then: on the carved stone above the falling water, where nothing alive had any business on Ilum, sat a convor, green-feathered and round-eyed, its feathers unruffled by a wind that was flaying his face. It was the bird from the Temple roof. It was not looking at the door or the children or the light; it was looking at him. He blinked against the glare off the ice, and when he looked again there was only bare stone, and Yoda, beside him, had not so much as turned his head.

"Oh," Kessa said, very small.

"Go," said Yoda. "Before the sun sets, return. Alone, each of you. Alone, the crystal calls."

Tam went first, because Tam went first into everything. Brannick went with his scarf over his mouth. Ilyo went with her bag, until Huyang said, "The bag," and she came back and left it. Wix went in and was gone like a shadow. Kessa took one step and stopped and looked back, not at the ship or at Yoda but at the girl beside her, and the Tano child said, "Go on," in a voice that was a child's voice, light and kind, and Kessa went.

The girl stood on the threshold. She was ten and small for ten and the dark in the door was taller than the Temple. Anakin stood ten paces behind her with his hands in his sleeves and saw her shoulders rise and fall once, and then the small body squared itself, and there was nothing of a child in the way it did it. She went in without looking round.

He let out a breath he had not known he was holding. Yoda, beside him, said nothing at all.

* * *

They waited in the vestibule, where the cold was only terrible and not fatal. Huyang went back to the ship to wake the forge. Yoda sat on the ice with his stick across his knees and his eyes shut, and Anakin stood, because sitting on ice was a thing the body objected to, and watched the door, and counted the hours by the bar of sun across the floor.

He knew what the caves did. He had gone in at ten, furious, and they had shown him his mother in the slave quarters with her hands in a bowl of water, saying his name, and he had walked through her as if she were smoke because Qui-Gon had been dead a year and he had already learned what walking through things cost. He had come out with the blue crystal in his fist and Yoda had looked at him then much as Yoda had looked at him on the ship. He had not thought about it for thirty-five years. He thought about it now, and about six children in the dark being shown what they feared, and found that his hands had gone to fists in his sleeves and made them open.

Tam came out at the third hour, which Anakin had bet himself he would, because Tam was a boy who ran at a thing and got it over. He came out of the dark at a run and skidded on the vestibule ice and went down on his backside and held up his fist and shouted, "Blue!" and then, "Master, it talked to me, it actually talked, it said my name," and Yoda opened one eye and said, "Your name, it said? Or your name, you heard?" and Tam sat on the ice with his crystal and his mouth open. Anakin pulled him up by the collar. The boy's hands were white with cold and the crystal in them was the size of a thumbnail and lit like a coal.

"One," Anakin said, to no one.

Wix came out at the fourth, walking, with both mouths shut, and would say nothing at all except, through Huyang's translator on the comm, that it had been green and that he had seen his clan-mother. He sat down beside Yoda and put his long head in his hands and Yoda put one small hand on his back and left it there. Two.

Ilyo came out at the fifth with a cut on her palm where she had held the crystal too hard, and the crystal was blue, and she showed Anakin the cut before the crystal, because it was the cut she was proud of. He wrapped it with a strip of his own sleeve. She told him, while he wrapped it, that the ice had shown her a room with no door and that she had sat down in it until there was one, and she said it in the tone of a child explaining a sum, and he looked at her small green face and thought that she would have made a good soldier, and then thought what that meant, and finished the wrapping. Three.

Brannick came out at the sixth and he was crying, openly, the way Nautolan children cried, with the whole face, and he had lost his scarf, and his head-tails were stiff with frost, and he had a green crystal in his hand and would not let Anakin see it until Anakin had promised not to touch it. "It showed me the ocean," Brannick said. "It showed me home and I couldn't get in." He had been brought to the Temple at two. He had no home that he could remember. Anakin crouched on the ice in front of the boy so that he had to look up to him only a little and said, "But you came out," and Brannick nodded, with his whole face, and Anakin held the boy's frozen head-tails between his hands until they went soft. Four.

The bar of sun had crossed the floor and was climbing the far wall.

Kessa came out at the eighth hour. Anakin heard her before he saw her, small boots on ice, not running. She came into the light with a blue crystal in her hand and her face set in Master Sen's expression, the one that gave nothing away, and he saw how hard she was holding it there. She came straight to him. She did not show him the crystal. She said, "Is Ahsoka back?"

"Not yet."

The expression broke and the child was under it, ten, Mirialan, frightened, with her friend's name in her mouth. "She went left. At the first split. I went right. I called to her and she didn't—" She stopped. "You're not supposed to call. Master Yoda said. I called anyway."

"That's all right."

"Is it?" Kessa looked at the door. "She's always back first. In everything. She's always back first and then she waits for me."

"I know," Anakin said, and heard himself say it, and did not know how he knew. Five.

The sun went down the mountain.

It went the way it went at this latitude, not gently but all at once, the bar of light shortening across the far wall and going red and then gone, and the cold came up through the floor as if the mountain had drawn breath. Huyang's voice on the comm said the forge was ready and that five was a good number and that he had had years when it was three. The waterfall over the door, which had run all day in a thin sheet, began to thicken. Anakin watched it. It went from water to glass at the edges first, and the glass spread inward, and the sound of it changed from a crowd to a whisper.

He looked at Yoda.

Yoda had not opened his eyes. He sat on the ice with his stick on his knees, and four children sat around him and one stood, and the old face was as still as the mountain.

"Master," Anakin said.

"Her path," Yoda said, "her own it is."

He said it without opening his eyes. He said it the way he had said everything on the ship, without weight, as a thing that was simply so. The waterfall whispered. Kessa made a sound. Anakin looked at the thickening ice across the door and at the dark behind it and at the old creature on the floor who had sat on this ice for eight hundred years and watched children not come back, and he understood that it was true, that it was the Order's truth and a kind one, that a crystal that called did not call twice and that a child the caves kept had been kept for a reason the Order would not question. He had believed it at ten. He had been taught it at ten.

He had also stood on a gantry over a reactor shaft, with the lightning still in his bones, and watched his son's face, and learned a different thing about what one did when a door was closing on someone.

The ice had reached the middle of the door. There was a gap the width of a man's shoulders and it was closing from both sides.

Anakin went up the steps. Kessa said, "Padawan—" and Tam said, "He's not allowed—" and Huyang on the comm said, "Skywalker," in the voice he had used for a thousand years on boys who were about to do something, and none of it was Yoda's voice, and Anakin waited for Yoda's voice at the top of the steps for one breath, with his hand on the ice. It was so cold it burned.

"Hmm," Yoda said behind him.

That was all. Anakin turned sideways and went through the gap into the dark, and the ice met behind him with a sound like a word.

* * *

The dark was not dark. He had forgotten that. The ice took what light there was and carried it, so that the walls had a glow in them like a thing seen through water, and the glow was enough to walk by if one did not mind what one walked into. He went forward with one hand on the wall. The cold was total. It went in through the tunic and the skin and sat in the long bones of a fifteen-year-old who had not yet grown into them, and the body wanted to stop and curl and sleep, and he told it no, and it went on.

He reached for her in the Force, and the caves took the reach and gave it back as his own echo.

He had expected that. He stood at the first split, where Kessa had gone right, and went left, and the ice closed in and opened out, and at the next turn he put his hand on the wall and the wall was warm.

He took the hand away. He knew. He went on anyway, because the only way through the caves was through, and at the next turn the walls were black glass and the glow in them was red, and the floor shook under him with the slow heave of something enormous and molten going by below, and the air stank of sulphur and burned cloth, and he was on the bank.

He stopped. He made himself stop and look. The river went by on his left, fat and slow and white at its heart, and the black sand under his boots was hot through the soles, and up the slope above him stood a man with a blue blade in a low guard and his face wet, and the man's mouth was open and the thing he was saying was not a sound yet.

Anakin looked at him for a long moment. Then he said, aloud, in a voice that cracked on the cold because the body was fifteen and the cold was real even if the fire was not, "I know. I was there," and walked up the slope, through the place where the man stood, and the heat went off him like a cloak dropped, and the walls were ice.

He went on. He was shaking. He let himself shake; it was a body's right.

She was at the next bend, and she was twenty.

Not the senator in the hangar with her hands at her throat. He had braced for that, and the caves, which were older than he was and had done this before, gave him the other one: Padmé at the lake, the first week, in the yellow dress, turning from the balustrade with the light on the water behind her and her mouth beginning to smile, before any of it. Before Geonosis. Before he had told her about the camp. Before he had been anything but a boy she had decided, against her own judgement, to love.

He stood and let it hurt. That was the trick of it; he had learned it on the bank. One did not refuse a thing by looking away. He looked at her face until he had the whole of it, the way it had been, and he said, not aloud this time, *you were right about me too, and I never got to tell you*, and she went on smiling, because she was ice and did not know, and he went through her and she was gone and the cold was in his teeth.

The bridge was last.

He came out of a passage into a cavern he could not see the top of, and across it ran a span of ice the width of a man's two feet, with no rail, over a drop that went down into blue and then into black, and the caves had made it, out of his memory, with a craftsman's care: the gantry over the reactor shaft on the second Death Star, the catwalk where he had stood with his master's lightning coming down on his son and made the last choice of his life. He knew the gantry's length. He knew the exact place on it, two-thirds across, where his legs had gone. The caves had put a crack in the ice there.

He laughed. It came out of him before he could stop it, a boy's laugh, high and strange in that place, and it went up into the dark and came back. "No," he said to the cavern. "That one I'd do again."

He walked across. He did not look down. At two-thirds the ice groaned under him and he felt the crack go and did not change his pace, and it held, because it had never been about whether it held, and he stepped off the far end onto solid ice, and the cavern behind him was a passage like any other, and the caves, he felt, had finished with him. There was a kind of silence in the ice that had not been there before. He had been examined, and the examiner had put down the pen.

He stood in it and breathed, and let the wall he kept round himself go thin, thinner than he had let it go since the night he woke, and reached.

She was close. She was very close, and she was not alone, and the thing that was with her was not of the caves.

* * *

He found her by a wall of ice.

The passage opened into a chamber no bigger than the Padawan quarters, and the far side of it was a single sheet of ice, blue-white, flat as a mirror and taller than the door at the temple, and in front of it stood a child.

She had her back to him. She was ten years old. The tunic hung on her the way it hung on all of them, a size made for the next year, and the short montrals and the lekku barely past her shoulders were white with frost, and the small body in front of the ice was shaking so hard he could see it from the doorway, a fine fast shaking like a wire in wind. Her right hand was at her side, in a fist. The fist was lit. Green light came out between her fingers, steady and clear, and lay on the ice in front of her, and in the ice there was a thing.

He saw it as she saw it. He did not mean to. He had let the wall go thin and she was ten paces off and what she was looking at came through the air between them like heat off a stove.

The ice was not showing her the caves. It was showing her a floor of black stone, and a dark in which a red light moved, and in the red light a shape: tall, black, cowled or helmed, he could not say which at this distance, with a red blade held low in one hand, and the blade's light climbing the black shape and finding no face in it. The shape was not moving. It was standing, as he was standing, and looking at the child, as he was, and the child was looking back at it and could not stop.

He knew what it was. He had known from the doorway, before he had the shape clear, the way one knows one's own hand in the dark. He had stood like that. He had held the blade like that, low, in the one hand, because the other had been for the choking. He had stood on a black floor and looked at a small figure in white and told her she would die.

The girl in front of him was ten and had never left the Temple and could not have seen that thing in any life she had led.

He did not think. He had done his thinking on the bridge. He went across the chamber in four strides and put a hand on each of her frozen shoulders, from behind, and reached into the child's mind to take her out of it, the way he had taken Luke's hand, the way one took a drowning thing by the hair—

—and met a wall.

Not the ice. A wall in her: smooth, cold, made. He went up it and it did not give. It was not a child's shield, the soft panicked clench the younglings made when Drallig touched their minds in class. It was built. It had layers. The outer was noise, engine-noise and hangar-noise and the chatter of a hundred people, laid over everything like a tarpaulin; under it a surface, plain and plausible, a girl's fears and a girl's wants, set out to be found; and under that the true wall, blank and hard and old, with the seams of it worked over and over until they did not show. He knew the seams. He knew the noise. He had chosen hangar-noise because a Padawan who had grown up in a hangar would find it easy to hold. He had built this, in the war, on a troop transport between Christophsis and the next place, in the dark, with a fourteen-year-old sitting cross-legged on a crate in front of him and her face screwed up, and he had taught it to her layer for layer, first the noise, then the surface, then the wall, over three nights, until she could hold it asleep.

He had taught it to one person in his life.

His hands were still on her shoulders. The shaking had stopped. The green light in her fist had not changed. In the ice, the black shape stood with its red blade, and the child had stopped looking at it, because the child had felt what had just touched her and what had just stopped, and he felt her feel it, through the wall he had made, which she was holding, now, with everything she had.

"Who taught you that?" he said.

She turned.

"You did."

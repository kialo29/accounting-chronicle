## Chapter 32: The Negotiator

Obi-Wan Kenobi stood in the centre of the Council Chamber with both feet flat on the floor and nothing holding him up but his spine, and counted it a small victory that nobody had offered him a chair.

The sling had come off before Tatooine. Vokara Che had taken one look at him on his return, said something in Ryl that he chose not to have understood, and signed him fit to stand for an hour at a time. The shoulder still pulled when he breathed in. He breathed in anyway. Twelve seats, all filled, and the morning coming through the tall windows so that the floor was a wheel of light with him at the hub of it. He had stood here a month ago with a hole in him and told them the courier was his order, and it had held. A man could get used to that, which was the danger.

The report had gone in the night before. Eleven pages, written on the transport home with Anakin asleep across the aisle, long legs folded wrong, the braid come loose from behind his ear. Obi-Wan had written it with the sleeping boy in the corner of his eye and had left out everything that could not be explained.

"Master Kenobi." Windu did not look up from the datapad on his knee. "Your report."

"You have it, Master."

"I have read it. The Council has read it. I would like to hear it."

So he told it, in order: the tower room at Oba Diah; the survey team of eleven, alive, on Eriadu; the droids on the gantry, Trade Federation hull-codes under the paint, six years old and sold on as surplus, which the Pykes did not have the credit to buy by the hundred; the money, which had come from somewhere; and the hunter.

"A Mandalorian," he said. "Silver-blue armour, old pattern, a jetpack. He was on the upper gantry for the whole of it. He did not fire. He did not close. His helmet recorded, and when it was over he left with the recording, and I would very much like to know who he delivered it to."

"You saw that he recorded."

"I saw the indicator. One learns to look for it."

"Mercenaries record," Windu said. "They are paid on proof."

"They are. And a man in that armour costs more than the Pyke Syndicate has ever paid for anything that was not spice. He was not hired to fight. He was hired to watch." Obi-Wan let that sit for one breath and no longer. "Senator Verrane of Tyrrhus asked for me by name, through the Chancellor's liaison, on a matter that any Knight in this Temple could have handled. I was sent. I was shot. I suggest to the Council, with respect, that the Pykes were paid to kill a Jedi on Oba Diah, and I would like us to ask who benefits."

The light moved on the floor. Plo Koon, who had not moved since Obi-Wan came in, did not move now.

"The Pykes are criminals," Windu said. "Criminals have killed Jedi before. They will again. They had a Republic survey team and a Jedi came to take it from them, and they did what they are. Your Mandalorian is a mercenary; there are three hundred of them on the Outer Rim and half of them wear old armour. Senator Verrane has been checked. His office, his accounts, his staff, his committee seat and his tariff. He is a man from a poor world who wanted a famous Jedi to rescue his voters and got one." A pause, dry as the voice. "You were asked for by name because you are the one they had heard of."

"I'm flattered."

"You should be concerned." Windu set the datapad down. "You have given this Council a Senator who is clean, a hunter who did nothing, and droids that anyone with a cargo hauler could have bought at Cato Neimoidia for scrap prices. And you have asked us to see a conspiracy. I do not see one. I see a Jedi who was shot and who would like it to have meant something."

That was fair, and it was meant to hurt, and it did neither. The Council had been wrong in this room so many times in his memory that one more did not cost him anything.

"Then I withdraw the word conspiracy," he said, "and I'll settle for the word *paid*. Someone paid the Pykes. Someone paid the hunter. I only ask that when the next Jedi is asked for by name on a matter beneath them, the Council remembers that I asked."

"Noted." Windu turned a page he did not need to turn. "And the slaver on Tatooine. The mission that took you out there. What did it find?"

"Sand, Master. In quantity. The slaver had sold up and gone before I arrived."

"Master Kenobi." Plo Koon's voice came through the mask the way it always did, as if from a room further off. "The hunter recorded. Have you considered what, precisely? A boy's style, perhaps. A lift made with an open hand."

Obi-Wan looked at him. Behind the goggles there was nothing to read, and he had known Plo Koon for twenty years and in another life had put his name on a list.

"Me," he said. "Falling, mostly. I would not have thought it worth the price of the armour."

"No," Plo said, and left it there, and Obi-Wan understood that it had been both a question and a kindness, and that Plo Koon knew exactly what that helmet had caught of the boy, the style no Master had taught and the hand held open, and had decided for reasons of his own to let the answer stand.

Yoda had said nothing. He sat with his hands on the head of his stick and his ears a little lowered and his eyes on Obi-Wan's face, and he had not spoken once, not to agree with Windu and not to disagree, and the silence was not absence. Obi-Wan had learned to read it over four years of lying to the old Master. Yoda was not waiting for the Council to finish. He was waiting for the while to end.

"The report is received," Windu said. "The Council finds the deaths on Oba Diah the work of the Pyke Syndicate acting in its own interest. The matter of the hunter is referred to the Judicial Department. The matter of the Senator is closed. The matter of your courier," and his eyes came up for the first time, "is on the file, in your words, and will stay there."

"I would expect nothing less."

"Filed. Thank you, Master Kenobi."

He bowed, from the waist, with the shoulder complaining, and went out, and the door sighed shut behind him on twelve people who had heard everything he had said and nothing he knew.

* * *

The letter was waiting in the quarters when he got back, routed through the Archives as all correspondence from the Lost Twenty was, with Jocasta Nu's seal on the slip and, he suspected, her eye on the content. Anakin was in the dojo. He put the kettle on, because that was what one did, and keyed the holo.

Text, not voice; Dooku had chosen. Blue, in a script so even it looked set rather than written. Obi-Wan read it twice.

*Master Kenobi,*

*I learn, late and by the usual roundabout channels, that you were injured on Oba Diah and have been some weeks in the care of the Temple healers. I write to say that I am sorry for it. Qui-Gon would have been sorrier, and angrier, and would have said so in fewer words than I am able to manage.*

*I have for some years intended to ask you to Serenno. There are papers of his here that the Order did not want when he died and that I think you ought to see, and there is a conversation about his legacy, what he taught and what he was not permitted to finish, that I would prefer to have in a room and not across a comm. If your recovery allows it, the house is quiet in the autumn and the air is a great deal better than Coruscant's. You would be welcome to bring the boy. I knew Qui-Gon's opinion of him. I have never had the chance to form my own.*

*With regard, and with the hope that it does not come too late to be of use,*

*Dooku.*

The kettle clicked off. He did not get up.

It was a beautiful letter. He let himself feel it, because it was true and Dooku had written it to be felt. The old man had loved Qui-Gon as much as he had loved anything, and six years into the service of the thing in the Senate he still did, and the invitation was made of that love and of the hook inside it, and the two could not be separated, and Dooku would not have wanted them to be.

*You would be welcome to bring the boy.*

He sat with it. He had lain on his back in a hangar on Geonosis in another life, leg opened, arm opened, and watched this man's blade lift over him with a courtesy that had not altered by a hair, and been saved by a boy who lost half an arm in the saving. Three years after that, on the floor of a dying ship, the same boy had crossed two sabers at the old man's neck and been told, very quietly, by the voice in the chair, to do it. Obi-Wan had not seen that. Anakin had told him, on a roof, with his face turned away. *He had no hands. The Chancellor said do it, and I did it, and I think I knew then.*

And now the old man wanted to see the boy. In a quiet house, in the autumn, with the air good, and whatever he had felt in that boy's mind at the reception waiting to be looked at properly.

Obi-Wan made the tea and wrote the reply by hand, which took most of an hour, because it had to be perfect and because the shoulder made his writing slant.

*Count Dooku,*

*Your letter reached me this morning and I was glad of it: for its kindness, and because it is the first word I have had of Qui-Gon's papers, which I had believed lost with the rest of him. I am grateful to know they are not.*

*I am unable to travel at present. The healers are of the opinion that a man who has been shot should not be permitted to enjoy himself; the Council is of the healers' opinion; and I find that between the two of them there is very little opinion left for me to hold. My Padawan's studies also keep him on Coruscant. He has fallen behind this year, which is my fault and not his, and I am not inclined to compound it with a holiday, however good the air.*

*May I ask after the papers by a slower road? If copies could be sent to the Archives in Qui-Gon's name, Madame Nu would have them where he would have wished them kept, and I would read them with the care they deserve, and with the care you have shown in keeping them.*

*Qui-Gon spoke of you often. He never said anything of you that I would not repeat.*

*With regard, and in his memory,*

*Obi-Wan Kenobi.*

He read it back. He took out one adjective. He put it back. He sent it through the Archives, to Jocasta Nu's attention, so that there would be a record that Master Kenobi had been offered Serenno and the boy, and had declined the one and not seen the other, and had been impeccably polite throughout.

The holo chimed before he had set down the stylus: Jocasta Nu, in miniature, acknowledging the correspondence and noting, in the same level breath, that Padawan Skywalker's trace in the Archive query log was still logged and still unreported, and what did he wish her to do with it? "I set the boy that exercise," Obi-Wan said, which was near enough to true that it cost him no flicker, "and I would be obliged if you left it logged under my name." She inclined her head a fraction, said that the Archives did not alter entries, only append them, and let it stand.

Then he drank the tea, which had gone cold, and went to find the other two.

* * *

The third waterfall by the east door fell into its pool from a height of twelve metres and made, behind itself, a ledge of wet black stone that nobody used, because it was cold and the only way to it was a rim of rock with the water on one side and the drop on the other. Ahsoka had found it, of course. She was sitting on it now with her knees up and her boots not quite reaching the edge, ten years old and small against the stone, the lekku to her shoulders beaded with spray, and Anakin was beside her with his back to the wall and his long legs out, and between the two of them, under the moss, was the sample box that held their letters.

Obi-Wan came along the rim with one hand on the rock and did not look down, and when he reached them Ahsoka moved over without being asked, and he sat.

"Well?" Anakin said.

"Filed."

"All of it?"

"Pykes acting in their own interest. The hunter to the Judicial Department. The Senator closed. Master Plo asked me what the Mandalorian had been recording and I told him it was me, falling, and he let me." He took the pad out of his robe. "And this came."

He read them Dooku's letter. He read them his reply. The water fell past the end of the ledge in a sheet, and the light came through it green, and when he had finished neither of them said anything for a moment.

"He wants me," Anakin said.

"He wants to see you. In a quiet house. With the air good."

"He wants to see whether I'll try to kill him." The boy's voice was flat and old. "He felt it. At the reception. He put his hand in and he found something that knew him, and he's been turning it over for two months, and he's just asked you to bring it to his house so he can look at it in a good light."

"Yes."

"And you said no."

"I said no in the most courteous terms available to the language, and I copied Jocasta Nu, so that if anything happens to either of us in the next year there is a record that the Count of Serenno asked for you and was refused." Obi-Wan put the pad away. "It is the only thing I can do with a letter like that. Decline it beautifully and make sure somebody saw."

"He'll write again."

"Each one kinder than the last, and each one mentioning you less. That's how it's done." He shifted the shoulder. "Now. I would like, if the two of you can bear it, to be dull for half an hour. We have been reacting since Oba Diah and I want to stop."

Ahsoka said, "Go on, then," in the voice she used when nobody else could hear her, the woman's dryness out of a child's mouth.

"One." He held up a finger. "Anakin's cover is above everything. Above mine. Above hers. Above the chips, above Kamino, above the Chancellor. The reason we are still sitting here is that the only two people who know what he is are a Jedi who lies to the Council and a youngling who lies to Master Plo, and the moment that changes, every clock in the galaxy starts running at once." He looked at the boy. "No more Oba Diahs."

"You were shot."

"I was. And you cut a droid in half with a stroke Cin Drallig has never taught, and choked a man to death with an open hand in front of a camera, and Mace Windu has your report with the word *theft* in it and the Chancellor has whatever that camera saw. If I am shot again, Anakin, you will let me be shot. You will fight like a talented boy who is frightened, because that is what you are supposed to be, and if I die of it you will grieve like one, and you will not, under any circumstances, let the thing out of its box in front of a witness."

The boy's jaw worked. He was fifteen; it was visible. The flush came up his neck and he looked at the water and not at Obi-Wan and said, after a while, "Fine."

"The audiences continue. Every tenth day. The surface he finds is the surface you've built, and the wall stays where it is, because if the wall comes down he will know, and if the wall changes he will know, and the only thing that has saved us so far is that he cannot see what is behind it and has concluded someone else built it. Let him go on thinking that. Let him think it's Dooku."

"He's stopped thinking that."

"Then let him think whatever he thinks, and give him nothing new to think it with. You sandbag. You lose to Orrin Bellas in the dojo on alternate weeks. You are bad at astrogation."

"I'm not bad at astrogation."

"You are now."

Ahsoka made a sound that might have been a laugh. "He's never known how to be fifteen."

"I know how to be fifteen."

"You know how to be fifteen the way I know how to be ten," she said. "Badly, and from the outside."

"Two." Obi-Wan held up a second finger before it could become a quarrel, because it was the kind of quarrel they could have for an hour and had. "Sifo-Dyas."

That stopped them both.

"He is on the Order's books as missing. Not dead: missing. Nobody has touched the file in five years, because he was a strange man with strange opinions and the Council was glad, I think, to stop looking. Sifo-Dyas was Qui-Gon's friend, and Dooku's; the three of them were a story in this Temple once." He let the water fill the pause. "I am going to petition to reopen it. As a personal matter. A Knight asking, on his late master's account, that an old friend of his master's not be left on a shelf. It will be granted because it costs nothing and because Master Yoda will want to know why I asked, and he will not ask me."

"And the file," Anakin said slowly, "goes to Ord Mantell."

"The file goes to Ord Mantell, where there is a docket with his name on it from the year he vanished, and from Ord Mantell the docket goes toward the Rishi Maze, and from the Rishi Maze a man with a legitimate writ and a good reason can go anywhere he likes and come back and say what he found. I will not have to explain how I knew. The file will have known for me."

"Kamino," Ahsoka said.

"Kamino."

"And when you get there."

"That," Obi-Wan said, "is the part I wanted to be dull about."

She had turned to face him, small square shoulders, chin up, and under it the woman who had cut a chip out of a man's head on a ship full of men who wanted her dead and had never once, in the telling, raised her voice.

"They're six," she said. "Rex is six. The first batch. They look twelve and they're six, and every one of them has a thing in his head the size of a fingernail that will make him shoot you when a man on Coruscant says a number into a comm. You asked me on the roof what I'd do first, and I told you, and I'm telling you again. The chips. Before Sidious, before Dooku, before anything. We find the army and we take the chips out."

"Of how many?"

"All of them."

"There are two hundred thousand of them, Ahsoka, if the Kaminoans are to be believed, and a million more by the time anyone needs them."

"Then we start."

"And the Kaminoans, who report to a man named Tyranus whose face they have never seen, will notice when a Jedi begins cutting open their product, and will tell him, and he will tell the Chancellor." He said it gently. He had no gentler way. "I believed, until the two of you told me otherwise, that they turned because they were ordered to and were afraid not to. I have had to learn that they were made to. I do not know what one does with an army of children who have been made to. I would rather know than not. I am not certain that is the same as being ready."

"You don't have to be ready," she said. "You have to go."

"I am going. I only wanted it said, on this ledge, that I am going without a plan for the end of it, so that when the end of it arrives nobody can say I promised one."

Anakin had said nothing through this. He was looking at the water, and Obi-Wan, who had been told about a dying man's head in the boy's lap and a chip in a bloody cloth, saw that he was on that ship and not on this ledge at all.

"Three," he said, and the boy came back. "Senator Amidala."

The silence that followed had a different quality from the others. Anakin did not move. He did not look away from the water and he did not look at it either; he simply went still, the way the body goes still when it is waiting to find out whether it is going to be hit.

"She has written through the Senate liaison to ask after Padawan Skywalker's health since the incident on Oba Diah. Her committee is taking evidence on Syndicate conduct in the Rim. I am going to answer her myself: my Padawan is in excellent health and has no account of his own to give, which is true, and the committee may have my evidence, in person, as often as it likes. And I am going to make it clear, in the way one does, that anything the Senator wishes to know about Oba Diah, or about Jedi matters generally, she may have from the Temple, through me, at any hour. So that she need never ask the Chancellor's office. So that the Chancellor's office need never have a reason to send for her."

"You'll be her channel," Ahsoka said.

"I will be her channel. It is the only kind of distance I can manage. I cannot keep her out of the Senate and I cannot keep him out of the Chancellor's office, but I can make the Jedi the easier door, and a busy woman takes the easier door." He looked at Anakin. "If you have an objection, now is the time."

Anakin said nothing.

Ahsoka watched him say nothing. Obi-Wan watched her watch him. She had known the woman, in a life she had told them about in pieces, and had liked her; and the boy had told them, on the roof, in a voice like a door, what he had done to her on a landing platform. Neither of them said any of this. The water fell. After a while the boy said, "No. No objection," and it was in his fifteen-year-old voice, the performing one, and Ahsoka looked away from him as if she had been told something.

"Four," Obi-Wan said. "Dooku."

"Nothing," Anakin said at once.

"Nothing. Yes. He is the one who can reach Sidious, which makes him the one we cannot touch and the one who will, eventually, come to us. He has written. He will write again. He will find a reason to be on Coruscant and a reason to be in a room with you, and when that day comes we will have had a year to decide what you are going to let him find. Until then we leave him entirely alone and we are very, very polite." He eased the shoulder. "Those are the four. Cover; Sifo-Dyas; the Senator; the Count. Is there a fifth?"

"Yes," said Ahsoka. "You, falling off things."

"That is not a plan. That is a hobby."

"Stop it."

"I have stopped. I was shot while stopping. It is remarkably hard to stop in a way that satisfies everyone."

She did laugh, then, very small, and put her hand over her mouth as if a Master might hear it through twelve metres of falling water; and Anakin, beside her, did not laugh, but the corner of his mouth went, and the bond at the back of Obi-Wan's mind, thin as wire, warmed by one degree. It would have to be enough. There was nothing else on the ledge but the three of them, the box under the moss, and the long cold year coming down on them like the water.

* * *

Windu was in the antechamber when he came up for the afternoon session. That was not an accident; nobody waited in that round grey room who had not meant to wait. Windu was standing at the window with his hands behind his back and did not turn round when the lift opened.

"Master Kenobi."

"Master Windu."

"Walk with me."

They went out onto the walkway below the chamber, open to the air, the Senate District laid out beyond the rail with the great dome catching the light. Obi-Wan did not look at the dome. He had looked at it from here in another life, on the last morning of the Order, and did not need to again.

Windu walked to the rail and stopped. He stood for a while with the wind in his robes.

"The boy frightens me," he said.

Obi-Wan waited.

"I have sat on this Council across from Masters I did not trust and been right. I have sat across from children and been wrong, and I would like to be wrong now." The clipped voice did not rise; it never did. "He killed two men on Oba Diah. One of them was found with his throat crushed and no mark on him. The other had been cut through a droid first. I have seen the droid. The plating was eight centimetres and it was one stroke, and Cin Drallig looked at the cut and said he had not taught it and did not know anyone alive who had. Those are the facts in your report. You wrote them yourself."

"I did."

"Vokara Che walked that boy's mind in the spring with his consent and the Council's, and came back and told me there was a wall in it that was old and scarred and had been built over years. Years, Obi-Wan. He is fifteen. Something has been building in that boy for longer than you have had him, and I do not know what it is, and I do not know who taught him to shield like a thing that has been hunted, and I do not know who the Chancellor sees every tenth day when he looks at him." He turned from the rail. "I do not think you are lying to me. I think you are not telling me. I have learned the difference. And I do not know, any more, whether I am afraid of what the boy is, or of what you are doing to keep it from us."

The wind came along the walkway. Obi-Wan Kenobi, who had written this man's name on a list four years ago, stood with his bad shoulder to the rail and found that he had, after all, only one honest thing to say, and that the Negotiator in him, who had spent the morning turning twelve Masters and the afternoon turning a Count, had nothing to add to it.

"He frightens me too," he said. "That's why I'm staying."

Windu looked at him. It went on for some time. Obi-Wan let himself be looked at, which was harder than anything he had done in the chamber, and did not drop his eyes, and did not reach for a joke, and did not explain.

Then Windu nodded, once, as if a figure had been entered somewhere and had been found to balance, and turned, and went in to the session without another word, and left him on the walkway with the wind.

## Chapter 2: Too Many of Them

Obi-Wan was already up, and Anakin heard him before he saw him: the small domestic noises of a man in a kitchen who has done the same thing every morning for years, the click of the heating element, the faint rattle of a tin, the particular silence of someone waiting for water. He stood in the doorway of his own room with his boots in his hand and listened, and the listening was so sharp and so entire that he had to tell himself to stop it. A boy does not stand in a doorway listening to his master make tea as if it were the last transmission from a dying ship. A boy yawns. A boy scratches.

He scratched. His scalp, under short hair that was his own. He had been doing that all night, on and off, finding things: the braid behind his right ear, thin and tight and slightly greasy; the ridge of a bone in his wrist; the fact that both wrists were there to have bones in them.

He had done the thing with the bond before dawn, because it had to be done before Obi-Wan woke and he had not known when Obi-Wan would wake. It had taken him most of an hour. The wall he had built in the first instant of waking was the wall he had always built, the one Sidious had taught him and he had spent twenty years improving out of spite, and it was not a thing a fifteen-year-old carried. He had taken it down a course at a time, as if dismantling a gun emplacement in the dark, and built in its place something low and sullen and adolescent: a door shut not because there was anything behind it but because the boy inside wanted it known he was shutting doors. Resentment at a volume Obi-Wan would recognise and sigh at. He had been that boy, once. He remembered the exact temperature of it.

What he did not remember was Obi-Wan's face when he came into the common room.

The man was at the counter with his back to the door, in a plain under-tunic with the sleeves pushed up, pouring. He looked over his shoulder when Anakin's feet touched the floor and he smiled, and the smile was wrong.

It was not wrong in any way Anakin could have pointed to. It was Obi-Wan's smile: the slight one, more in the eyes than the mouth, that said he had seen you coming a long way off and had decided to be amused by it. But it arrived too fast. The Obi-Wan of this year, of the year Anakin had been fifteen, had not smiled when his Padawan came into a room. He had looked up. He had said *good morning* in the tone of a man confirming a fact. He had been kind in the way a well-made tool is kind, by doing its job without comment, and Anakin had spent that year and the three after it trying to make him say something that was not a correction.

This Obi-Wan said, "You look as though you slept in a ventilation duct."

"I slept fine."

"You slept the way a Wookiee sleeps. I could hear you through the wall." He set a cup on the table. Two cups. He had always made two, Anakin remembered, or thought he remembered, and Anakin had always let his go cold. "Drink that. You have Drallig at the tenth hour and I will not have you dropping a training saber on a nine-year-old because you could not be bothered to wake up properly."

Anakin sat down.

He sat because his knees had told him to, in the half-second in which the sentence went in and his body decided what it would do before he could stop it. The cup was in front of him. It steamed. He put his hands around it, which was something a boy did, and the heat went into his palms and through them into a man who had not felt heat in his hands for twenty-three years without a gauge telling him the number, and he breathed, which was still a shock each time, and said, "Drallig."

"The Hawkbat Clan. Their saber class." Obi-Wan sat down across from him with his own cup and looked at him over it. "You assist, every tenday. You have done for the better part of two years. I am aware that it is not your favourite morning, but I do not believe it has ever before caused you to forget it existed."

"I didn't forget."

"No?"

"I was thinking about something else."

"That," said Obi-Wan, "I believe." He drank. The beard was shorter than Anakin remembered, and redder; the grey had not come into it yet. It would come in at the war. Anakin had watched it come. "You shut the door rather hard last night."

There it was.

He had expected it and it still went through him. He kept his hands on the cup. He let the sulk he had built behind his eyes thicken a little, the way a boy's would when touched. "I was tired."

"Mm."

"I'm allowed to be tired."

"You are allowed to be anything you like, within reason and the Code." Obi-Wan set the cup down. "I only mention it because it was rather like being in a room when the lights go out. One notices." He looked at Anakin for a moment longer than the sentence needed. Then he reached across the table, reached across with his right hand, in a plain under-tunic in the morning light, and put it on Anakin's shoulder, and squeezed, once, and let go. "Drink your tea, my friend. You are going to be late, and Cin Drallig does not forgive lateness; he merely stores it."

Anakin drank his tea.

He drank it and it was tea, and it was hot, and the hand had been on his shoulder for perhaps a second and a half, and he could still feel the four fingers and the thumb as if they had been pressed into wet clay.

Obi-Wan had never done that.

Not at fifteen. Not at nineteen. There had been a hand on his shoulder on a landing platform once, when the Chancellor's people were watching, a hand that was performing. There had been one on Mustafar. No. There had not. There had been the opposite of a hand. He had gone looking for this touch for a decade, had wanted it so badly that he had made a whole life out of not being given it, and here it was, at a kitchen table, at fifteen, from a man who had just said *my friend* to a boy with a braid.

My friend. Obi-Wan said that to equals. To Dex, to Bail Organa. He had said it, at the last, to a man in a mask on a dark landing, and Anakin cut that off before it finished, because he knew where it ended and he could not go there with a cup in his hands.

He thought: *I have forgotten. I am remembering a different year. He was always like this and I made him cold in my memory because cold was easier to hate.*

He thought: *No.*

He thought: *Write it down.*

"You're staring," Obi-Wan said.

"You've got tea in your beard."

"I have not." He checked, though. Anakin watched him check and something in the watching nearly broke: the small vanity, the hand going up to the jaw, the slight affronted dignity. He had not seen this man do this since he could not have said when. He had not seen this man. Not this one. He had seen an old man in a desert, through a helmet, for the length of one fight, and the old man had done nothing so human as check his beard.

"Go," Obi-Wan said. "Boots. Belt. Both of them. The children can smell fear and so can Drallig, and he has a longer memory."

* * *

The training halls were on the fourth level, on the eastern side, under the Council towers. He knew the way. His feet knew it, which was not the same thing, and he let them have it.

The Temple in the morning was noise. He had not been prepared for that. Twenty-three years in a place where every sound was his own breath, and then a night in a sleeping building, and now this: the corridors full of people, robes and boots, the low voices of Masters and the high voices of younglings being shepherded somewhere in lines, a door hissing, a laugh, somebody's name called from a stairwell. Light through the tall windows in slabs. The smell of stone and cleaning solvent and, somewhere, bread. A Twi'lek Knight nodded to him in passing and he nodded back and did not know her name and was certain he had once. Two Padawans his own age went by arguing about astrogation. A youngling of four or five sat on a bench outside the Halls of Healing with one boot off, crying with enormous dignity, while a crèche-master knelt in front of her and said something about socks.

It was so ordinary and so alive that he could not breathe in it. He stopped at the top of a stair and put his hand flat on the wall and the wall was cool and the stone was the stone and under his hand, for a second, it was warm, and black with smoke, and the light on it was orange.

*The 501st came up the Processional Way in two columns and he walked at the head of them. Their boots on the steps. The Temple's doors stood open; someone had opened them, someone had thought it was a delegation; and the light inside was the same gold it always was, and he went into the gold with his saber in his hand and it was already lit.*

He took the hand off the wall.

A boy of twelve or so, a Padawan, bumped him from behind and said *sorry* without looking and went on. Anakin went on. He went down the stair and along the gallery and through the archway into the hall of the initiates, and he counted the children as he came in because counting was a thing he knew how to do.

Twenty. Sand-coloured tunics, brown belts, small boots. Hawkbat Clan was the nine-to-eleven cohort, and they stood in four rows of five in the middle of the long hall with training sabers in their hands and the sabers not yet lit, and they were loud. That struck him first and hardest: the noise of them. A human boy was telling a Rodian girl something at a volume that suggested he thought she was a floor away. A Nautolan boy and a human boy were comparing their hilts and shoving. A Mirialan girl near the back, with her hair in a tight dark braid, stood very correctly with her saber at her side and looked bored out of her mind. Somebody's belt was done up wrong. Somebody was yawning. The smallest of them barely came past his belt; the tallest was a lanky human boy who might one day be Anakin's own height and was all elbows getting there.

Children. He said it to himself the way he had once said *target* and *range*. Children. A room of children, with sticks.

Battlemaster Cin Drallig stood at the far end of the hall with his hands clasped behind him, watching Anakin come in with the expression of a man noting the time.

He was alive. That was the thing. He was alive and his hair was still mostly brown and he was wearing the plain dark tabard he taught in and there was a training saber tucked in his belt, and he said, in exactly the voice Anakin had heard across this hall ten thousand times and once, "Padawan Skywalker. You honour us."

"Master Drallig. Sorry."

"You are not late. You are merely not early, which in you I have learned to treat as a kind of lateness." Drallig looked him over. The look was quick and professional and it did not find anything; Anakin had built himself at the top of the stair to be looked at. "You look peaky. Kenobi feeding you?"

"Tea."

"Yes. Well." Something almost like humour. "Take the back two rows. Grips and stances; we are on the second Shii-Cho sequence and half of them are still holding the thing like a spoon. Correct what you see. Do not demonstrate anything I have not taught them, however much they beg, and they will beg."

"Yes, Master."

"Hawkbat Clan," Drallig said, not loudly, and the hall went silent as if a blade had come down through the noise.

Anakin took the back two rows.

He did it. That was the thing he would remember, afterwards, when he could remember anything about the hour: that he did it. He walked down the side of the hall along the ranks of children while Drallig called the sequence from the front, and his body knew the walk and his voice knew the words, and he said *thumb along the emitter, not around it* and *weight forward, you're sitting on it* and *that's your left foot, Dorreth, I need the other one*, and the children did as they were told, or did not, or did for three seconds and forgot, and he came back and said it again. Ten children in his two rows. He learned their names from Drallig's roll and from their own mouths, when they told him, which they did without being asked, because children did. Ilyo. Brannick. Wix. Tam Dorreth, who was the loud one. The Mirialan girl who stood so correctly was Kessa Ruun and her grip was the only decent grip in the hall and he told her so and she flushed up green and did not know where to look.

*The Council chamber. The round room, the twelve chairs, the dark outside the windows because it was night. They had hidden behind the chairs. Six of them, seven. Someone had told them to go up as high as they could, and they had, and they had waited, and the door had opened.*

"Sorry," he said to the Nautolan boy, who had said something. "Say that again."

"I said is it true you built a podracer."

"Yes."

"Did it win?"

"Once." He looked at the boy's hands. "You're choking it. Here." He put his own fingers over the boy's fingers and moved them an inch down the hilt, and the boy's fingers were damp and small and bony and the hilt was a training hilt, light, blunt-ended, meant for a child's reach, and it was, for the length of a breath, the lightest thing he had ever held. "Feel that? The balance is in the hand now, not the wrist. You'll last longer."

"Last longer than what?"

"Than you did yesterday."

The boy grinned. He had very sharp small teeth. Anakin went on down the row.

There was a girl, Rodian, Ilyo, whose stance was close to right and whose feet were a hand's width too narrow, and he knelt to show her, because she was small and a thing shown from above is not the same as a thing shown at one's own height. He knelt on the mat on one knee, and the knee went down and the joint took his weight and did not grind or lock or report a fault, and he had to let that pass through him too, and said, "Look at my feet." She looked. Her eyes were enormous and wet and black and interested. "Yours are like this." He put his boots together. "Where does the ground go if I push you?"

"Away?"

"Where do *you* go?"

She considered. "Also away."

"So." He opened his stance. "Now push me."

She pushed him. She put both hands on his shoulder and shoved with her whole body, and she weighed about as much as a loaded rifle, and he did not move, and she laughed, a loud, surprised, honking laugh that made Drallig at the front say *Ilyo* without turning round, and then did it herself, feet apart, and looked down at them, and looked up at him, satisfied.

He stood up.

Drallig was walking the front ranks now, calling the sequence with his hands clasped behind him, *one, and two, and guard, and over*, and Anakin heard the voice as he had heard it as a boy in this hall, calm and dry and infinitely patient, the voice of the Order's own patience, and he turned his head towards it the way a man turns towards a lamp.

*The same hall. The same mats. Not the same light. The Battlemaster came through the smoke with his blade already up and he was older and his hair was white and he said nothing at all; he had seen what was behind Anakin and had done his sums. He was good. He was the best the Order had with a blade in a hall, and he was old, and he had taught the boy who was killing him every stroke the boy did not use. It took eleven seconds. Anakin had counted. He had always counted. The last thing Drallig's face did was understand.*

"Over, and guard, and *reset*," said Drallig, alive, at the front of the hall. "Skywalker, the back row."

He looked at the back row.

He had been working the fourth rank from one end and had reached the third child in it when the thing with the Rodian girl had happened, and he had not yet got to the far end. The far end was in the corner, by the wall. There was a Togruta initiate in it.

She was small. Smaller than Ilyo; the smallest in her row, perhaps the smallest in the clan. Her montrals were short, barely risen, white and blue, and her lekku came down only to her shoulders and curled at the ends against the sand-coloured cloth of her tunic. White markings on an orange face. Her tunic was a size too large and had been taken in at the belt by someone who did not sew. She was holding her training saber the wrong way round, hilt in a backhand grip, the blunt emitter pointing down and back along her forearm instead of up and out, and she had been holding it that way, he saw, for some time, because the stance she had built around the grip was complete, and comfortable, and hers.

"Tano," said Drallig, from the front, without heat. "The other way. We have discussed it."

Anakin's knees went.

They did not go all the way. The body he was in was fifteen and strong and did not know what its mind knew, and it caught him, somewhere above the floor, with a lurch like a ship catching on its repulsors a metre from the ground. He put a hand on the wall. The wall was cool. He had stopped breathing and he started again, and the breath went in like something swallowed wrong, and nobody looked at him, because Drallig had said a child's name and the children were all looking at the child.

She turned the saber over. It took her a moment. She was not slow about it; she was, he thought, precise, in the way of someone doing a thing properly that she had done improperly on purpose. She held it the right way up and looked at the front of the hall and waited.

He knew her.

He had known her from the back of the room, from the curl of the lekku against the tunic, before Drallig spoke. He had told himself it was any Togruta, that there were Togruta in every clan, that the Temple had taken in dozens of them in the years before. Before. And then Drallig had said the name and the name had gone through him and out the other side and taken something with it, and what was left standing by the wall in a Padawan's tunic with his hand on the cold stone was not a boy and not a man but a thing in a black suit on a stone bridge in a temple that was falling down, looking through a crack in a lens at a woman who had said she would not leave him.

He had told her what that meant. He had meant it.

She had been thirty-three. She had been a Padawan of fourteen with a saber too short for the grip she wanted, and a girl of seventeen walking down the Temple steps away from him, and a voice on a comm in a war that was over, and the thing on the bridge. He had had a name for her. He had not used it since.

She was ten.

He made himself look. The hilt was too long for her wrist; he could see it from here, the way the pommel sat past the heel of her hand, the way she had had to shift her grip to keep the emitter from dipping. Her boots were planted. There was a scuff on the left one. A child in the second row had turned round to look at her and she looked back without expression until the child turned away.

He did not reach for her. In the Force she was one small bright thing among twenty and he did not reach for her, because he could not have borne what he found, or did not find.

"Skywalker," Drallig said again, mild.

"Yes, Master."

He walked down the row.

He did not know, afterwards, how. He walked, and the children parted for him, and he came to the end of the row and stood in front of her, and she had to tip her head back to look at his face and he could not let her, so he knelt. He went down on one knee on the mat as he had for the Rodian girl and it brought his eyes level with hers, more or less; hers were blue, and very steady, and they went to his face for one half of one second and then away, to the hilt in her hand, to the thing he was presumably there to do.

"Let me see," he said.

She held it out. The right way up. He took her wrist.

His hand was shaking.

It was not a tremor; it was a shake, the kind that lives in the forearm and comes out through the fingers, and he could not stop it. He watched his own hand close around her wrist, around the bone of it, which was small, which was a child's, which he could have broken by closing his fist, and he saw that she was watching it too. Not his face. His hand. Her eyes were on his fingers where they shook against her skin, and they stayed there, and she did not flinch and she did not speak and she did not, in any way at all, look up.

He heard himself say, "Thumb along the emitter."

"Yes," she said.

It was the only word. Her voice was a child's voice: high, a little hoarse, Togruta vowels. It went into him like something cold into a wound and lay there.

He moved her thumb. The joint of it was tiny under his. He opened her fingers one at a time with his own and laid them along the hilt where they were meant to go, and he was not teaching her anything, and they both knew it; her fingers went where he put them and lay there as obediently as a doll's. He did not look at her face. She did not look at his. He thought of the way she had been holding it, reversed, the emitter back along the forearm, the grip he had watched her build an entire form around in a war that had not happened, and he thought that if he corrected it he would be correcting her out of the only thing in this room that was true. And he thought that was madness. She was ten. She had picked it up wrong the way children picked things up wrong; she would be told, and told, and would grow out of it or be made to.

He let go of her wrist.

He put his hand on his knee where it could shake against something, and he said, "Good," and then, "Carry on," which was not something a Padawan said to a youngling, and stood up.

She turned the saber over.

He saw her do it. It was not defiance; there was nothing in her face at all. She simply turned the hilt in her hand, deliberately, the way she had before, until the emitter pointed down and back along her forearm, and set her feet, and looked at the front of the hall, and waited for the sequence.

He did not correct it.

He stood beside her for the length of a breath with his back to the children and his hand at his side and he did not correct it, and then Drallig said *one* and the room moved, and he moved with it, back along the row, past the Mirialan girl, past Wix and Brannick and the loud boy, and corrected a grip, and corrected a stance, and said *weight forward* to somebody, and did not look behind him.

*The round room. The chairs. He had come in and they had come out from behind the chairs because he was Master Skywalker and they had been told that the Jedi would come, and one of them, a boy, fair, nine or ten, with his training saber held in both hands exactly as he had been taught, had stepped forward out of the others and looked up at him. "Master Skywalker," the boy had said. "There are too many of them. What are we going to do?"*

*He had not answered.*

*He had never answered.*

"And guard," said Drallig. "And reset. Better. Dorreth, I said better, not louder. Again."

* * *

The class ran forty minutes. He finished it.

He could not have said how. He would try, later, lying on his back in the dark in a boy's bed, to reconstruct the second half of that hour and he would find he had almost none of it: the Nautolan boy asking again about the podracer; a lit training saber catching Tam Dorreth across the knuckles and Tam yelping and the clan laughing and Drallig not laughing; Kessa Ruun's braid. The clan bowing. Drallig's hand lifted in dismissal and the noise coming up again like water into a lock. Twenty children making for the door and the small one in the corner going with them, not first and not last, somewhere in the middle, where a child who did not want to be noticed would go. He did not watch her go. He watched the wall.

"Skywalker."

"Master."

Drallig was looking at him. The look was longer this time. "You did well enough. The Rodian girl's feet were a good catch." A pause. "The Tano child."

"Yes, Master."

"Was there something?"

He thought about it. He actually thought about it, with the Battlemaster's eyes on him and the hall emptying behind him, and the thinking took no time at all and arrived nowhere. "She holds it backwards."

"She does. She has done since she was seven. Her clan-master has had words, I have had words, Master Plo has had words." Drallig's mouth did something that in another man would have been a smile. "She holds it correctly for exactly as long as she is being looked at. I have known Knights with less discipline." He unclasped his hands. "You were kind to her. I saw. That is not a fault. Go and eat something, you look like a wet rag."

"Yes, Master."

He bowed. Drallig bowed. He walked out of the hall the way he had come in, past the mats and the racks, through the arch and along the gallery, and his legs did it and he was grateful to them, and at the second stair he turned left instead of right and went into the refresher at the end of the corridor and locked the cubicle door and was sick.

There was not much. Tea. He had not eaten; Obi-Wan had said *eat* and he had not. He knelt on the tiled floor with his hands on the rim and brought the tea up, and then nothing, and then nothing again with his whole narrow body clenched around it, and the sound he made in the small tiled room was a boy's sound, thin and awful, and he could not stop it. His eyes ran. His nose ran. He hung there with his forehead on the cool edge of the bowl and waited for the body to be finished with him.

Somebody came in and used the basin and went out. He stayed where he was.

She was ten.

He said it to the bowl. He said it in his head and then, very quietly, aloud, in the voice he had, which cracked in the middle of it. *She is ten.* She was ten years old and she weighed nothing and her tunic was too big and she held a training saber the wrong way round because she was a child and children did that, and she had looked at his hand because his hand had been shaking and a child would look at a shaking hand, and she had said *yes* because he was a Padawan and she was an initiate and that was what one said. There was nothing in it. There was a child in it. The last Togruta he had touched had been thirty-three years old and had put her hand against the cracked lens of his mask and he had told her she would die, and that was not in this room, and had never been in this room, and would not be for twenty-three years, if ever, if he could stop it, if he could keep her from ever standing on that bridge with that face.

She was a child. She was the thing he had come up the steps to kill.

He sat back on his heels. The cubicle was very small. His knees were against the door. He wiped his face with the heel of his hand and looked at the hand, which had stopped shaking, which was fifteen years old and had never held anything heavier than a training saber and a hydrospanner, and he thought, with a clarity that was almost peace, that he did not know what she had seen.

Not the girl. The thing she had seen was his hand, shaking. He did not know what she made of it. Children made things of things. He did not know what Obi-Wan had seen across a kitchen table, or Drallig across a hall; he did not know why there was a hand on his shoulder where there had never been one, or a child in the corner who held the blade the way she had held it in a life that no one in this building had lived. He did not know what was wrong, or whether anything was, or whether it was only him: a dead man's memory, gone soft in the suit, remembering a colder master and a later child and calling the difference a sign.

He could not tell. That was the whole of it. He could not tell, and he would have to find out, and in the meantime he would have to go and eat something so that his master would not look at him again the way his master had looked at him over the tea.

He got up. He flushed the bowl and unlocked the door and rinsed his mouth at the basin and did not look in the mirror, because there was a boy in it, and he was not ready for the boy.

Malachor was seven years ago.

Malachor was twenty-three years from now.

He went to find food.

## Chapter 19: Interlude: The Count's Report

Count Dooku left the reception at the eleventh hour, which was the hour at which a man of his age and standing was expected to leave, and he had made a lifetime's practice of doing what was expected at the hour it was expected, so that the rest of what he did might go unremarked. He said his goodnights. He bowed to the Chancellor last, as a guest should, and the Chancellor took his hand in both of his and said how good it had been of him to come so far for Qui-Gon's memory, and smiled, and let him go.

The speeder was his own, driven by his own man from Serenno. The Senate dome fell away above and behind, lit from within like a lamp with its wick turned high, and Dooku sat with his hands folded on the head of his cane and looked at nothing, and for the first time in a number of years that he did not care to count, he was not entirely in command of what he was thinking.

He had touched the boy's mind. He had been told not to; he had done it anyway, lightly, for a breath, the way a connoisseur runs a thumb along the grain of a piece he has been told he may look at and not handle. His Master had wanted him to look, and one did not look at such a thing with the eyes.

He went through it again, in order, because order was what he had instead of comfort.

The surface first. A boy's surface, and a competent one: the tidy resentments of fifteen, a mother on a desert world, speeders and the smell of them, a girl in a politics class. All of it plausible. All of it a little too neatly set out, like a room arranged for a visitor, with the chairs turned to face the door. Every Padawan of talent learned to build one by twelve.

Then the wall.

He had known at once what it was, and the knowing had gone through him like cold water, because he recognised the grammar. Not the Jedi grammar, which was all stillness and release, a shield like the surface of a pond; this was built. It was laid in courses. It had been raised against a pressure and had held, and had been breached, and had been raised again over the breach, and he could feel the old lines under it the way one feels a mended bone through the skin. It was a discipline he had been taught in a derelict foundry by a man whose face he had not been permitted to see for the first year, and he had taken six years to learn it, at seventy, with half a century of the other grammar to unlearn. The boy was fifteen.

And then, for one second, through the wall, the thing itself.

He had expected fear, or the sullen, formless dislike that the young had for the old, or nothing. What came through was none of those. It was hatred, and it was aimed. It had his name on it. It was not the hatred of a boy for a famous man who has left his Order and written unkind things about it; he had met that often and it was a thin, borrowed thing, somebody's master's opinion wearing a Padawan's face. This was his own. It had the precision of a history: the hatred a man feels for someone who has taken something from him, and knows exactly what, and has had years to turn it over. There was a shape to it that he could almost see the edges of. Something lost. Something given up on one's knees. He did not know what. He had never, before this evening, been in the same room as Qui-Gon's boy.

And the boy's face had not moved.

That was the part he returned to in the speeder, with the lanes of light sliding across the window. He had been looking into the boy's face when it happened; he had chosen the moment so that he would be. A boy's face, narrow, growing, the braid behind the ear, the sleeves of the formal robe a finger too short at the wrist. The hatred had come through the wall with the force of a door kicked in, and the face above it had gone on being politely attentive to a Count's one remark about a dead man's last message, and then the door had been shut, not slammed, shut, and the boy had said *Count* and bowed the exact depth a Padawan bows to a visiting dignitary who is no longer a Jedi. Not a hair's depth more. Dooku had taught that bow to Qui-Gon, who had taught it to Kenobi, who had taught it to this.

He had not been so well handled in years. He did not like how it tasted.

* * *

He had watched the boy afterwards, from the terrace, because his Master had asked him to look.

He had watched him with the Senator from Naboo. He had seen the girl come down the north steps in her blue and go to the boy with her security captain a step behind, and he had seen the boy receive her the way a Padawan receives a Senator, with courtesy and nothing in it, and he had seen her face do what a young woman's face does when she has expected warmth and been given manners. She had gone back up the steps to Organa and the Chandrilan woman, and the boy had stood where she left him for the space of three breaths with his hands held still at his sides in the way of someone who has had to tell his hands to be still.

He had watched him with Kenobi on the terrace. That had been longer and quieter and harder to read. The master had leaned on the rail; the Padawan had stood a half-pace away from him, which was closer than a Padawan stood and further than a son. At one point Kenobi had turned his head and said a short thing, and the boy had answered with two words, and whatever they were, Kenobi had looked for a moment at the plaza as if it had been taken away from him. There had been no hand on the shoulder. Dooku had noted that. Kenobi was a man who touched; he had touched the boy's elbow in the gallery, and his collar, and on the terrace he had not touched him at all, and the not touching had the look of a decision.

And the boy had found him watching, across forty metres of wet stone, and had not looked away. It was not a boy's look. It was the look of someone who has measured the distance between them, and the ground, and the light, and knows what he would do with it.

He had inclined his head. He had not gone nearer. His Master had said, *look, and do not touch,* and he had already disobeyed the second part once that evening.

* * *

The Works at night were a city that had died and not been told. Dooku's man set the speeder down on a loading apron where a foundry had been and did not ask where the Count was going, and Dooku walked the rest, with his cane sounding on the ferrocrete and the smell of old chemistry in the air, past the dead chimneys and the gantries that no one had taken down because taking them down would have cost more than leaving them.

His Master was waiting for him in person. He had expected the hologram; the hour was late, and the Chancellor had a Senate in the morning. But there was a shape in the dark at the far end of the long room, a hood and the pale hands folded in the sleeves, and the voice when it came was the other voice, the one the Senate did not hear.

"Well?"

Dooku knelt. The knee complained; he did not. "Master."

"You touched him."

"I did."

"I told you not to."

"You told me to look. I looked with the instrument that sees." He kept his head down. "You wished to know. I am telling you."

A silence. Then, out of the dark, not displeasure. Interest. "Tell me."

He told it as he had ordered it in the speeder: the surface, the wall, the second through the wall. He did not embellish. He described the grammar of the wall and his Master said nothing, which meant his Master had already found that much for himself. He described the hatred. He said that it had his name on it, and he said it without inflection, as if it were a line in an inventory.

"Yours," said Sidious.

"Mine. Not the Order's. Not a boy's. A man's, and old, and particular."

"You have never met him."

"I had never met him before this evening."

"And his face?"

"Did not change." Dooku lifted his head. "He shut it on me as one closes a door on a draught. Master, I know a fifteen-year-old's control. I trained three of them. This is not that."

The hood tilted, a degree.

"Then say what you came to say, Tyranus."

He had composed the sentence on the loading apron and he gave it whole, because it was the one true thing he had brought.

"The boy is not yours, and he is not mine. Someone has had him."

The pleasure that came out of the dark then was a thing he had felt only a few times in six years and never without a small cold movement at the back of his neck. It was the pleasure of a mind that has been bored for a very long while and has found, at last, a thing it cannot immediately account for. His Master's voice was soft and almost fond.

"Then we will find out who."

* * *

They did not sit; there was nothing in the room to sit on. His Master walked, slowly, a few paces along the dark, and back, with the sleeves folded, and spoke as he walked, and Dooku stood where he had risen and listened.

"Consider what we know. For six years I have walked in that boy as I walk in my own house. Four months ago the doors closed. The healer found a wall and called it old. You find a wall and call it built in the right grammar, and behind it a hatred for you with a history you did not give it." A pause, a turn. "I will not insult you by asking again whether it is yours."

"It is not."

"No. You have not the patience for a long seduction." Said mildly, as a fact about the weather. Dooku let it pass. "So. Someone has walked where I walked and left a wall behind them, and has done it so well that the boy's own master suspects and cannot name it, and the Council hunts a shadow through its own corridors and has not thought to look at the one child it watches most. It was not you. It was not I. Therefore it was a third, and I do not know a third."

Dooku said nothing. There had been, four months ago, in this room, a third possibility that his Master had looked at and not named, and he had not been invited to name it since.

"We will not find the hand by asking the boy," Sidious said. "The boy lies as well as you do. Better; you I can read. We will take something from him and watch where he looks." He stopped walking. "The master."

"Kenobi."

"Kenobi. The Jedi lose their masters, Tyranus. It is the one thing they do reliably. They send them out into places they do not understand with a Padawan at their heels, and the master dies, and the Padawan is given a cup of tea and a new name for the thing he feels. It is how the Order has made every Jedi worth having. It is how it made Kenobi." A beat, exactly placed. "It is how it made you."

Dooku did not move.

"Qui-Gon was lost to a mission the Council should never have sent him on," he said.

"Yes. And look what it gave us." The hood turned toward him fully. "Kenobi will be lost to a mission the Council should never send him on. The boy will be there, because the boy is always there. And we will see what the boy does when the thing he has been building his wall around is taken from him. If he weeps like a Padawan, I will know less than I know now, and I will have Kenobi out of the way, which I have wanted for four years. If he does something else—" the voice dropped, not for effect, Dooku thought, but because it was the part his Master actually cared about— "then I want it seen. Not reported. Seen. I want a recording, and I want it in a form the boy cannot deny and the Council cannot explain, and I want it in my hand before anyone else knows it exists."

"Fett," Dooku said.

"Fett records everything. It is the only thing about him I find endearing. He oversees; he does not lead. He is not to touch the boy. He is to see that others do, and to stand where the light is good." The pale hands turned once, open, as if showing that they held nothing. "The Pykes, I think. They owe you. And they have a lightsaber in a drawer they do not know what to do with, which makes them the sort of people who will take a Jedi hostage if they are told it is a negotiation."

"It will need to reach the Council through the proper channels."

"Everything reaches the Council through the proper channels. That is what the channels are for." A last turn in the dark, and the voice went, in one breath, from the one the Senate did not hear to the one it did, warm and a little tired. "Go and arrange it, my friend. Ask for the hero of Naboo by name. The Senate loves a hero, and the Council cannot refuse the Senate twice in one year."

Dooku bowed. It was the right depth. He had taught it.

* * *

The hologram to Kamino went out from a relay on Serenno that did not exist, through two others that did, and arrived in a white room Dooku had seen once and never wished to see again. Jango Fett stood in it in his armour with the helmet under his arm, which was a courtesy; it meant he was not recording.

"Count."

"Fett. Oba Diah, within the month. The Pykes will hold a Republic survey party and a Jedi will be sent to talk them out of it. You will not lead. You will oversee, and you will record, and what you record you will deliver to me and to no one else, uncut. There will be a second Jedi, a Padawan. You will not touch him. If the first Jedi dies in the course of it, that is the Pykes' affair and not yours."

Fett's face did nothing, which was what his face did. "Price."

"Name it."

He named it. It was high, and less than it should have been, because Fett knew who paid his retainer. He had conditions. He would choose his own ground. He would not go in under the Pykes' command; he would go in beside it. He wanted a clean route out and a ship that was his. He wanted it understood that if the Padawan turned out to be what Dooku was not telling him the Padawan might be, he reserved the right to leave.

"You will not need to leave," Dooku said. "You will need to stand still and watch."

"That's the part that costs." Fett shifted the helmet to the other arm. "I'll be off-world eleven days, twelve. The boy stays here. I want that in the ledger."

"The boy stays where he is."

"Then we're done." The hologram cut.

The Pykes were slower, because a syndicate was a committee with knives, and it took three intermediaries on three worlds before Lom Pyke's answer came back in the form of a question about mineral rights. There was, it seemed, a Republic survey team already on Oba Diah: eleven people under a woman named Deshan Orr, mapping a spice-bearing ridge the Pykes had understood to be theirs and the Republic had understood to be nobody's. Lom Pyke was happy to be aggrieved about that. He would, for a consideration and the quiet return of a certain small item he had kept since a certain Jedi had been dealt with on his world six years ago, make his grievance loud, and hold the eleven for their own safety, and ask the Republic to send someone sensible.

Someone sensible would be asked for by name. Senator Caius Verrane of Tyrrhus, whose shipyards the Count of Serenno had kept from going under two years before and who had never been allowed to forget it, would rise in committee and say that only the hero of Naboo could be trusted with eleven Republic lives, and the Chancellor would look grave and say the Council must decide, and the Council would decide what the Chancellor told it.

It took Dooku four days, from a room in 500 Republica with the shutters drawn, and at the end of them there was a sequence of events laid out ahead of a man he had met four times and a boy he had met once, and none of it bore his name.

* * *

On the last night he stood at the window with the shutters open, which he did not usually do, and looked across the lit distance to the five spires of the Temple holding up the dark.

He had been a Jedi for sixty years. He had told himself, when he left, that he was leaving a thing that had failed, and he still believed it, and it still cost him something each time he said it, and he had begun, in the last months, to keep a private account of the cost that he showed to no one. Tonight he added a line to it. He had stood in the dark and listened to his Master say *it is how it made you,* and had felt the old loss turn over in him like a body in a bed, and had said nothing, and his Master had known he would say nothing, and had said it anyway, to show that he could.

That was the usual cost. It was not what kept him at the window.

What kept him there was the second through the wall. He had reported it faithfully. But there was a thing his Master had not asked for, because his Master did not think in that direction, and so Dooku had not said it, and found now that it was the first thought in six years that was entirely his own.

It was this. Somebody had had the boy, and had taught him a wall, and had given him, somehow, a hatred for Count Dooku with the shape of a history in it: the shape of a thing taken and a man made to kneel. Dooku had no such history with the child. He had gone through every encounter of his life, in the speeder and since, and could not find it. And the thing that kept him at the window, that kept his hands still on the sill the way the boy's had been still at his sides, was not the question of who had done it.

It was that some part of him, old and careful and very tired, did not entirely wish to know.

Below, the lanes of the Senate District went on about their business, and the Temple's spires stood where they had stood for a thousand years, and somewhere in them a boy of fifteen was asleep, or was not, behind a wall that had been raised against a pressure and had held.

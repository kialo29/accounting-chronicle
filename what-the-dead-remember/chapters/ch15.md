## Chapter 15: Snips

Huyang built lightsabers the way other people told stories, and Anakin Skywalker, who had heard this one before, sat at the back of the *Crucible*'s workroom with his hands between his knees and let it be told.

"The crystal does not want to be hurried," the droid said. He had a hilt casing in one hand and Tam Dorreth's wrist in the other, turning the wrist so the boy could see the emitter from the side. "Nor do I. Nor, young man, do you, though you believe otherwise. Set it down. Now look at it."

Tam looked at it. He was eleven and loud and had been loud for three days of hyperspace, and the crystal on the bench in front of him, blue, the size of his smallest fingernail, shut him up the way nothing else had. The workroom smelt of hot metal and lathe oil and of children: six of them in sand-coloured tunics at six benches, sleeves pushed up, bent over parts laid out on cloth. Ilyo's green fingers were quicker than anyone's. Wix hummed through both his mouths and did not know he was doing it. Brannick had put his emitter in backwards and was pretending he had not. Kessa Ruun had her tongue between her teeth.

And at the sixth bench, nearest the door, a Togruta girl of ten with her lekku barely past her shoulders and her montrals too short to be anything but a child's was assembling a lightsaber slowly, and wrong, on purpose.

He had been watching her do it for an hour and it was the best thing he had seen in twenty-six years. She let the focusing lens slip once and said *oh* in a girl's voice; she asked Huyang a question she knew the answer to and listened with her head on one side. Every mistake was true-sized, one a child of ten who had never held a hilt would make, and she made it with such plain craft that he wanted to stand up and applaud, and could not, because he was Kenobi's Padawan escorting the Gathering and she was Tano sixth of six and they had agreed on the ice that in front of other people they had never met.

"Hands," Huyang said, and she held them out, and the droid turned them and looked at the palms as he had looked at every child's palms for a thousand years. "Yes. Small. They'll grow. A shorter grip than the boys, and the emitter at a steeper angle; you hold things close to the body. I remember a Togruta who held things close to the body. Two hundred years ago. Terrible temper."

"I don't have a temper," Ahsoka said.

"Everybody has a temper," said Huyang. "It is where one keeps it that matters. Fit the lens."

She fitted the lens.

Tam got his blade lit first, because Tam got everything first, and the blue of it went up through the workroom like a shout and he shouted with it, and Huyang said "*Off*, thank you, *off*," and the others laughed, and for a moment it was not a ship full of the future, it was six children with a new toy on the way home. Ilyo's blue. Wix's green, which he looked at from both sides and then closed, carefully, as if it were a book. Brannick's green, after the emitter had been turned the right way round by a droid who said nothing about it. Kessa's blue, held in two hands, her face rinsed with the light, and the Mirialan girl turned to the bench beside her the way she turned to that bench for everything, to be seen having done it.

Ahsoka looked up and smiled at her friend, and the smile was a child's and was also, Anakin thought, the kindest lie in the room.

Then she looked down and thumbed the stud.

He had known what colour it would be. Knowing did not help. The blade came up green in a room full of blue and green and he was on Christophsis with the dust in his teeth, on Teth with the stairs going up for ever, on Ryloth, on Felucia, on the deck of the *Resolute* with the hangar doors open and that colour going away from him into the black at the head of a boarding party. A thousand battlefields. He had never once seen it lit for the first time. He had turned up, that first day, to a Padawan who already had it.

She held it still and low, the way Huyang had told her, and did not look across the room, and the green light sat on the white of her face and did not shake.

"Well," said Huyang. "That will do nicely. Off."

She turned it off. Anakin found he had his hand round the edge of the bench hard enough to hurt, and let go.

* * *

Coruscant came up under them on the eighth day, grey and gold and endless, and the Temple hangar swallowed the *Crucible* with a hiss and a smell of coolant. Knight Sabo Thane stood on the deck with a datapad, checking the children off as they came down the ramp with their sabers on their belts and their chins up. Yoda went down first and did not look back. Master Sen was there with her hands in her sleeves to gather Hawkbat and march it away to be fed and put to bed, and the children went to her in a straggle, Tam loudest, Ahsoka in the middle with Kessa's arm through hers, looking at nothing.

And Obi-Wan was there.

He stood at the foot of the ramp in brown with his hands clasped and the look on his face that Anakin had learned to think of as the counting look: a man adding something up and arriving at the same sum twice and not liking it. It went to Anakin first. It went up him, boots to braid, and found him tired and thin and fifteen, and the bond, which Anakin had opened to a hair's breadth on the approach because closed would have been a confession, carried back something that was not relief, exactly, and not surprise, and was warm.

Then it went to the Tano child.

It stayed on her a beat longer than a man's look stays on a youngling he has no business with. Then it came back, and for a moment Obi-Wan's eyes rested on the strip of hangar floor between the Padawan at the foot of the ramp and the girl going away in the clan, as if there were something on the floor there. There was nothing on the floor there. Anakin had made very sure.

"Welcome home," Obi-Wan said. "You look appalling."

"Thank you, Master."

"Did it go well?"

"Everyone came back with a crystal."

"That," said Obi-Wan, "is not what I asked," and took him by the shoulder, and turned him towards the lift, and did not say anything else about it for three days.

* * *

The practical problem was this.

A Padawan of fifteen might speak to an initiate of ten in a corridor. He might say *mind the step*. He might, assisting Battlemaster Drallig in the Hawkbat hall every tenth day, correct her grip, once, in front of nineteen other children and the Battlemaster, and not again. He could not sit with her, or walk with her, or eat at a table she was at. The Temple was a city of eyes trained from infancy to notice what did not fit, and the Council was already hunting a shadow in the building and did not need another one. Obi-Wan had looked at the floor between them. That had been enough.

So they did it in the Room of a Thousand Fountains.

He had forgotten the size of it. He had been in it once, afterwards, as the other man, and the memory had overwritten the place so thoroughly that he walked into it on the second morning home and stopped in the doorway like a tourist. It was a storey and a half of green under a vault of glass, terraces and galleries and dripping stone, ferns taller than a man, a smell of wet earth and cold water, and the sound: not one sound but a thousand, falls and runnels and the slap of the big pools, so that two people could stand a body's length apart and not hear each other. Jedi came here to be alone together. It was the only room in the Temple where staring was bad manners.

The third waterfall, counting from the east door, came off a lip of black stone two storeys up and dropped into a pool the width of a speeder, and behind the curtain of it, where the rock had been cut back by a landscaper eight hundred years dead to let the moss grow, there was a ledge. Everything there was wet. A child could reach it by walking the rim of the pool and ducking, and so could a narrow boy of fifteen if he did not mind his sleeves, and behind the water the room went away entirely and all you could hear was the fall, and the light came through green.

He put the case there on the third day: a sealed sample box from the mechanics stores, waterproof, the size of a hand, with a strip of grey flimsi folded inside. He wedged it under the moss at the back of the ledge, walked out through the water, sat on a bench with his hair dripping and meditated for an hour for anyone watching.

On the fifth day the case had been opened and his flimsi had gone and another was in it.

He read it on the ledge with the water a hand's width from his face. It was three lines of Togruti script, neat and small, with the curling ascenders he had seen on a thousand transport manifests and never learned, and he looked at it for a long time, and then he laughed, alone, behind a waterfall, for the first time in twenty-six years in a body that did not hurt when it laughed.

Because his own note had been in cipher. He had written it without thinking, in the hand he had written everything in for twenty-three years, keyed to the serial of a respirator he had not yet been fitted with, and she had opened it and found a dead man's shorthand, and had answered in kind.

He wrote, in Basic: *I can't read that.*

She wrote: *I couldn't read yours. What did it say?*

*Hello. Also, don't use the east walk after sixth bell; Jocasta Nu goes that way to the Archive stair and logs everything she sees.*

*That was a lot of cipher for hello.*

*What did yours say?*

*Hello. Also, your hair is too long at the back and Drallig noticed.*

He put his hand up to the back of his head. It was.

They could not go on in Basic. A Temple Guard could read Basic; the clan-mother, if she found a strip of flimsi in a ten-year-old's boot, certainly could. They needed a third thing, and it took them most of a tenday to agree it, and the argument was the first they had had since Mandalore, and he would not have traded it for a ship.

*Mando'a*, she wrote. *I have enough.*

*I know eleven words of Mando'a and nine of them are things Rex said when he dropped things.*

*Aurebesh, shifted, then. We pick a number.*

*The number of ships you crashed with me on board.*

*Four.*

*Nine.*

*Felucia was a landing.*

*Felucia was a crater with a ship in it. Nine.*

*We'll split it.*

*You cannot split a shift. Six and a half is not a cipher. Are you fifteen?*

*Yes, actually.*

A pause in the exchange; a day in the world. Then:

*Six. Tano, sixth of six.*

He did not need it explained. He had read the Gathering list on Obi-Wan's pad the night it came through: *Tano, sixth of six.* She had been last on every list in this Temple for seven years and she had put it in his hand as a key, and he sat on the wet ledge and thought that if she had been fourteen and in front of him he would have ruffled the montrals, and that he was going to have to learn not to want to.

The signature was a separate argument.

*S.*, she signed, under the six.

*S.*, he signed back, and then, because he could see it coming: *That's going to be a problem.*

*You pick another letter, then. I was born first.*

*You were born second this time.*

*I was born* seven years *earlier this time, Skyguy, I was in this building when you were still being chased round a hangar by a Toydarian, and if you call me that other thing in writing I will put it in the clan-mother's hand myself and tell her you sent it.*

He read that one three times. Then he folded it into the lining of his belt, where he kept the things he could not afford to have found, and wrote back, in Aurebesh shifted six: *Understood, Snips.* And did not sign it at all.

* * *

They had promised each other the bond on the *Crucible*, and had not made it there: Yoda was aboard for all eight days, and with Yoda aboard there was no privacy to be had.

The bond they made on the nineteenth night after the *Crucible* came home, behind the water, at the second hour, when the Temple was as empty as it ever got.

He went first and lay on the ledge in the dark with the fall a hand from his face. It occurred to him that this was the quietest he had been in twenty-six years, and then that it was not: the suit had been quieter, the breath and the breath and nothing else, and this was better. His back was wet through. He was fifteen and lying on a ledge in a garden at two in the morning waiting for a girl, and if Obi-Wan found him here there was no explanation that did not end in the Halls of Healing.

She came along the rim of the pool without a sound and ducked the curtain and sat down beside him with her knees up and her back to the stone.

"You're sure," she said at last. The voice was a child's; it had no other voice to be. The thing underneath it was not.

"No."

"Good. Nor am I." He could see the pale shapes of the markings on her face and nothing else. "Thin. We said thin."

"Thread."

"If it goes the way it went before, I'll cut it. In front of Yoda if I have to."

"Cut it. I won't stop you."

"You couldn't," she said, which was not true and they both knew it, and then: "All right."

He had not done this deliberately since he was twenty-three. The first time it had made itself, the way a path makes itself across grass; he had woken one morning on Christophsis and known where she was without looking and been annoyed by it. This was the other thing, the thing Masters did with new Padawans in a quiet room, with their hands on a child's temples. He did not put his hands anywhere. He sat with his wet back against the stone and found the wall he had built across the front of his mind for a quarter of a century, the scarred one, the one Vokara Che had put her hand on and called old, and found a place in it where it was thin, and made it thinner.

And let her find it.

It was a shock to both of them. He felt hers go through her like a dropped plate. For him it was this: that the last time he had felt this presence beside him in the dark she had been seventeen and he had been a man, tall, two-handed, with the war round them both like weather, and the thing that came up the thread to him now was that presence, exactly that presence, the quick dry heat of her, a woman of thirty-three who had buried him and run a rebellion and walked into a Sith temple to find him and been told to die. And it was wearing a child. The body it was in was small and sat close to the stone with its knees up. He could feel the size of her. He could feel her feeling the size of him.

And her shock was the mirror. She had expected the boy. What met her was forty-five years old. It was twenty-three years in a machine. It was a reactor shaft, and a hand on a mask, and a son's eyes, which she had never seen and which came to her now not as a picture but as a weight, the weight a thing has when it is the last thing. And under all of it, so far down she had to go looking, the boy.

She pulled back. He felt the thread go taut and then slack and then settle at the width they had agreed, thin as a hair, a thing you could carry through a day of classes and a clan-mother's inspection and a Council member's look and nobody would see it unless they already knew.

"Oh," she said, in the dark.

"Yes."

"That's heavy. You're very heavy."

"I know."

"I'll get used to it."

"You don't have to."

"I know I don't have to," she said, and he felt her, down the thread, decide to, which was a thing he had not felt anyone decide about him in a long time.

They sat with it. The water fell.

"It's strange being shorter than you," she said after a while.

"You were always shorter than me."

"Not by this much. I used to come up to here." She put a hand up in the dark; he could not see where. "Now I come up to your belt. It's humiliating. I'm going to have to look up at you for four years. I'd got used to looking down at Caleb."

"Caleb is seven."

"Caleb is tall for seven. He's going to be enormous. He's going to be—" She did not finish it. He did not make her.

"You'll grow," he said.

"I know I'll grow. I've done it. That's what's humiliating; I know exactly how far." She drew her knees in. "And you. You used to have a voice."

"I have a voice."

"You have two. You had two in the hangar. You said *everyone came back with a crystal* and the *crystal* came out an octave up and Thane looked at you."

"It does that."

"It's going to do that at the Chancellor."

"It's done it at the Chancellor. He finds it charming." He heard her make a sound that was nearly the laugh he remembered, the one from the gunships, and it went into him under the ribs and hurt, because it was the war, exactly the war, two people in the dark being rude to each other while the sky fell; and the sky had fallen, and was going to fall again, and the people under it this time were a boy and a girl in a garden. "Say *crystal*," she said.

"No."

"Say it."

"*Crystal*," he said, and it broke clean in the middle, and she laughed properly, with her hand over her mouth, a child's laugh in a child's hand, and he lay back on the wet stone and let her.

* * *

She asked him in the same voice, when the laugh had finished. She had never in her life needed a run-up.

"What did you do in the Temple."

Not *tell me*. A flat question, the kind you ask about a route or a casualty count. He looked at the underside of the water, where the night-lamps came through it bent and green, and he told her.

He told her the order of it. The gunships in the dark and the Processional Way and the stairs; the sound a Temple makes when it is being killed, which is not loud. He told her the men behind him had been the 501st and that she had known most of them by name and he had known all of them and had not thought once about the names. He told her where Drallig had met him: here. In this room, under the glass, with the water going, two Padawans at his back and the blade up in the old guard, the one he had taught them both. He told her what he had done to the Padawans first so that Drallig would have to watch it. He told her how long the Battlemaster had lasted, which was longer than anyone else in the building, and that at the end the old man had looked at him not with hate but with a kind of professional grief, the look of a teacher at a stroke he had never taught, and that Anakin had used the stroke, about forty paces from where they were sitting.

He told her about the Council chamber.

He did not make it shorter. There had been eight of them, under the chairs and behind them, in the circle of twelve seats at the top of the tower with the city on fire through the windows, Bear Clan and a few from the clan above, the oldest perhaps nine, and they had come out when they saw the robe and the braid, because he had been Master Skywalker, because he had taught some of them to hold a hilt. One had come forward and asked him what they should do. He did not say the boy's words; he had said them once, to the ice, and would not spend them twice. He told her he had looked down at the boy and the boy had been looking up, and that he had lit the blade.

"All of them," she said.

"All of them."

"How long."

"Not long."

"You went room to room afterwards. With the men."

"With the men. Then Mustafar. You know the rest."

"I know the rest."

Silence, and water. He could feel her down the thread and made himself not reach: a woman in a child's body holding very still, the way you hold still on something that may not take your weight.

"Say the excuse," she said. "Whatever it is. Say it now so I don't have to wait for it."

"There isn't one."

"There's always one. *He had me. She was dying. I thought I could—*"

"I thought I could save her," Anakin said. "That's true. It isn't a reason. I stood in that room and I knew what I was doing and I did it because by then I wanted to, and I'd want you to put the blade through me if I ever stand in it again, and that's all there is. Don't look for the rest. I looked for twenty-three years. There isn't any."

She breathed out. It took a long time.

"All right," she said. "Here's mine. I'm not going to tell you it wasn't you. Everyone's going to want to tell you that, when they find out; Obi-Wan's going to want to tell you that, if he—" The smallest catch. "People are going to want to make it a different man. It wasn't. You've just told me, and I believe you, and it was you, and I'm not going to forgive you for it. I don't think it's mine. The people who could are in that room and they're nine." The child's voice did not change. "I'm going to be angry with you for the rest of my life, which is a long time, because I've done it twice."

"I hear it."

"And I'm going to work with you anyway."

He turned his head.

"Because I did it alone," she said. "Afterwards. I walked out of here and did sixteen years of it alone, and most of it was carrying things, and I am ten years old and there is one person in this building who knows what's coming and it's you, and it is not possible for me to care, tonight, that you're also the one who did it. I'll care tomorrow. I'll care in Drallig's hall when you correct my grip. But I'm not doing it alone again, and I'm not letting you do it alone either, because the last time you were alone you went to the Chancellor's office, and I'm not having that. So." She held out her hand in the dark, small, the palm up. "Terms."

He looked at the hand.

*Christophsis, and the dust. The shield coming down over the city like a bowl being lowered. A shuttle on the plaza and a girl on the ramp, fourteen, orange and white, a saber at her hip too big for her and a chin that had never once been put down, telling Obi-Wan that Master Yoda had sent her, telling Anakin she was the new Padawan learner, telling him, when he said he did not take Padawans, that he had one now, and standing there with the dust on her as if she had already decided how it would go.*

He took the hand. It was a child's hand and it gripped like a vice.

"Terms," he said.

* * *

They drew it up on flimsi, behind the water, with a stylus and the green light. Two columns, in Aurebesh shifted six, because neither of them wanted either of their own hands on it.

*What she knows that he does not.*

Rex. Alive after the chip, which she had told him in the cave; but alive long after that, a decade after, old and white-bearded on a dead world in a rusted walker, found by a boy, fighting again. Anakin wrote it down and did not look at it.

The Inquisitors. He had, as the other man, commanded them and loathed them and used them; he had known the Pau'an by his title and the way he said *Lord Vader* with the second word a fraction late. He had not known what she wrote next: *Temple Guard. Here. Now.* That the Grand Inquisitor had been one of the masked men with the yellow pikes who stood at the Council tower doors, and was standing at one of those doors tonight, and that she had been flinching from a particular Pau'an under a particular mask for seven years and could not prove it. He put the stylus down for a moment.

Kanan Jarrus. A name on a report, in his other life; a Jedi on Lothal, a nuisance; he had never seen the man's face. She wrote *Caleb Dume* under it, and he thought of the boy of seven in Bear Clan who followed her about with his hair in his eyes. *Blinded*, she wrote, *by Maul; you have that from the ice*, and went on. Ezra, under that: *a boy, Lothal*, and no more, and something went down the thread that she caught before it reached him.

Maul. Mandalore, the last week of the war, the siege, the capture, the thing he had told her about Anakin in a dark cell that she had refused to hear. Anakin had not known. He had been on Coruscant, and then in the Chancellor's office, and then here, and then on the lava bank, and nobody had thought to tell Vader what his Padawan had been doing that week.

*What he knows that she does not.*

Luke and Leia. She had them from the cave; he wrote them anyway, because a list was a list. Obi-Wan on Tatooine, under another name, watching a boy from a ridge for nineteen years. The Death Star, which she had never heard of, and what it had done to Alderaan, and that Obi-Wan had died on it, under his blade, with his own put up, on purpose; she went very still at that one and he let her. The Emperor. The shaft. She had half-believed it on Ilum and half-believed it now; she made a small mark beside it in Togruti that he could not read and did not ask about.

And Dooku. That on the bridge of a ship over Coruscant, with the Chancellor tied in a chair, he had taken the old man's hands off and crossed two blades at his neck, and the Chancellor had said *do it*, and he had done it, and had known, kneeling there in the smell, that the Chancellor had sold his own apprentice for the price of a nod.

"He'll do it again," she said. "To someone."

"He'll try."

They looked at the two columns.

It took him a moment to see it, and he felt her see it in the same moment: the shape the two lists made between them when you held them up to the light. The things she knew that he did not were about a war. The things he knew that she did not were about the end of it. And the things neither of them could write down, because they would not fit in a column and because to write them was to say them, were all about one man.

A Master who had searched the Archives for a planet nobody in this Temple had heard of and sat forty minutes at a terminal looking at nothing. A Master who was warm, who put a hand on a shoulder, who said *my friend* to a boy of fifteen as if he had been practising it. A Master who had let a Padawan write to his mother for three years against every custom of the Order and argued it before the Council and won. A Master who had waited in Monument Plaza for six years, every tenth day, so that the Chancellor should never once have the boy for a whole afternoon, and had never once stopped trying to end the audiences. A Master who had stood at the foot of a ramp and looked at a Togruta youngling he had no business looking at, a beat too long, and then at the floor between her and his Padawan, as if there were something written there.

"He looks at me," she said. "He's looked at me since I was four. I told you. I'd know."

"You said."

"I don't know what I'd know."

"No."

Neither of them said it. It sat behind the water with them like a third person, and they did not look at it, with great care, the way you do not look at a sleeping child you have just carried in.

"We test him," Ahsoka said.

"Later."

"Later. Not here. Not with a question; he'll answer a question. Something he'd have to *do*."

"I'll think."

"You'll think." She folded the flimsi in four and held it out, and he put it with the other in the lining of his belt, and felt her, down the thread, go back to being ten: the shield coming up over her like a hood, the child settling into the small body as if into a chair.

"Bed," she said. "Sen does the dorm at the fourth hour. And you have Drallig at first bell, and the Chancellor at the end of the tenday, and your hair is still too long at the back."

She ducked under the water and was gone. He lay on the ledge a while longer in the dark with the fall a hand from his face and the thread, thin as a hair, running out through the stone and the gardens and the dormitory walls to a bunk where a girl was climbing in quietly beside a sleeping Mirialan, and he did not pull on it, and it did not break.

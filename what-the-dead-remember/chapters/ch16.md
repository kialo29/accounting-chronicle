## Chapter 16: Of All the Children

Yoda took tea the way he took everything, slowly and with his eyes on the person rather than the cup, and Obi-Wan had learned long ago that the only defence was to drink his own. The chamber was the small one under the Council floor where the old Master kept the hours he did not keep anywhere else. One lamp. A window the width of a hand. The *Crucible* had docked the evening before, and the Temple's stone still carried the sound of it, the way stone did: a tremor that was not quite a tremor, a cargo of children come home.

"Sit," Yoda said, though he was already sitting. "Tired, you look."

"I have been told."

"You have been told. You have not listened." The old Master set his cup down. "Of Ilum I will speak, and then you will tell me what you think, and then I will go and sleep, which is more than you will do. Hmm?"

"Go on."

"Six went in. Five came out before the sun went down. Your Padawan stood beside me at the door and counted them, as I counted them, and when the fifth was out he looked at the sky, and I told him to wait." Yoda's ears moved, a fraction. "Custom, it is. You know this. The door closes. The child inside learns what the cold has to teach. Dawn opens it, and the child comes out alone, with a crystal or without one, and knows what it is."

"I know the custom."

"He did not wait."

Obi-Wan looked at the tea.

"Said nothing, your Padawan. Gave me no argument. Did not ask. Walked in through the closing ice as if it were a curtain, and the door shut behind him, and for the length of a night I sat with Huyang and five children with crystals in their hands and a boy who was not where I had put him." The small clawed hand turned over on the table, empty. "At dawn the door thawed, and out he came, and she with him, the two of them through it together, she a pace behind."

"Together."

"The Tano child. Sixth of six. Small she is. Walked out on her own feet, she did, a pace behind him, and when the ice was under them both she went to the Ruun girl and wept into her shoulder as a child weeps, and he stood apart and looked at the sky again." Yoda watched him. "Her crystal, green. His face, grey. Said I to the both of them, and to neither, *Found you, she did.*"

Obi-Wan's hand had closed round the cup. He opened it. The tea had gone cold some time ago and he had not noticed, which was not like him, and he noticed that.

"Found you, she did," Yoda said again, gently, as if setting it down a second time in case it had rolled. "Not *found her, you did*. That I said also. But the other, I said twice. Hear it, do you?"

"I hear it."

"Hmm." The old Master's eyes did not move from his face. "It is a breach of custom. A report to the Council, I will make, and the Council will say what Councils say, and a mark it will be on the boy, and nothing more, because I will tell them nothing more. Nothing more, there is. A boy went after a child because he was afraid for her. Good, that is, in a boy. Dangerous, in a Jedi." A pause. "Which is he, your Padawan?"

"He is fifteen."

"So. An answer, that is not."

"It is the one I have."

Yoda drank. The window had gone from black to a dirty blue; the first bell was an hour off. "Watched him, I did, on the ice," he said at last. "For the Order, and for myself. More than a boy went into that cave. More than a boy came out, carrying more than he took in. What the ice showed them, I do not know. Ask them, I will not; tell me, they would not." He set the cup down a final time. "Ask him, will you?"

"I will ask him how he is," Obi-Wan said, "and he will say *fine, Master*, and I will believe about a quarter of it, which is the usual rate."

"Hmm." It was almost a laugh. "Go. Sleep. Lie to me in the Council at the sixth hour; lie well."

He went. On the stair he found that his hand was not quite steady on the rail, and he stood a moment in the dark of the tower and let it be unsteady, since no one was there to see, and then he put the hand in his sleeve and went down.

*Found you, she did.*

He did not explain to himself why the words had landed where they had. He had a list for things he did not explain, and he did not write them on it.

* * *

He began to see them after that. It was not that they had been invisible before; it was that he had not been looking, or had been looking in the way one looks at weather. Now he looked, and they were everywhere, and they were never together.

The Room of a Thousand Fountains, the third morning after the *Crucible* came home: Hawkbat Clan on the grass by the east door with their clan-mother, learning to sit still, twenty small sand-coloured backs in a crescent and Imari Sen with her hands in her sleeves. Across the water, on the bench under the ferns where he himself had sat at fifteen and been miserable, his Padawan with a datapad. Forty paces between them, a stream, a stand of blue reeds. The boy did not look up. The girl, seventh from the left, short montrals, lekku barely to her shoulders, did not look round. For the half hour he stood on the upper walk and pretended to read, neither of them looked, and the not-looking had an architecture to it. A child looks. A child looks at everything. A boy of fifteen with a datapad looks up when a youngling drops a cup, and a cup was dropped, by the Rodian girl, and twenty heads turned, and his Padawan's did not.

The corridor outside the initiates' refectory, a tenday later: the Tano child walking with the Ruun girl, the two of them in step, the Mirialan talking; Anakin coming the other way with Bellas and three of the politics class. They passed. Nothing. The girl's eyes stayed on her friend's face. The boy's stayed on Bellas, who was saying something about Serenno. Obi-Wan, at the far end of the corridor with Vos-Kerrick, saw the Togruta's left hand drop to her side as they passed and open, and close, once, the way a hand does when it has been told not to reach for something.

The hangar bay. The third-level stair. The archive walk after sixth bell, twice. He did not go looking for them, and he did not need to. In a Temple of two thousand souls his eye found two with no effort at all, the way one's tongue finds a broken tooth, and each time the same thing: the boy, the girl, the distance between them kept with a precision that nothing on the training floor had ever taught either of them, and never one word.

*Of all the children in the Temple*, he thought, on the archive walk, watching a small figure in sand-coloured tunic go up the east stair without turning her head, and did not finish the thought. *That one.*

He had been afraid of her since she was four. He did not know why. He had told himself a number of things about it over six years, and some of them had been plausible, and it was long past time he stopped telling himself things.

The Chancellor's tenth day fell, as it always fell, and he waited in Monument Plaza in the thin autumn light for the two hours it took, and the boy came out of the Senate Office Building with a face that told him nothing and said the office had been warm and the Chancellor had asked after Ilum. "He knew about the caves," Anakin said. "Not what happened. That I went in." "Everyone knows that, my friend. It was in the Council minutes by the ninth hour." "Yes," the boy said, and looked at the Senate dome, and said nothing else all the way home.

The anniversary reception was a good way off still. The Council had not refused it. When the invitation came he would ask them, once, informally, to decline it, and he expected to lose.

Jocasta Nu had told him, before the *Crucible* left, that someone using a dead librarian's maintenance account had read the general log from terminal nine, and that the trace, when she ran it, had come back to Padawan Skywalker; and she had asked him, in her dry way, whether she should take it to Master Yoda or leave it with him, and he had said leave it with me, and she had. He had not raised it. The boy knew, then, about the forty minutes two years ago at terminal four and the name he had typed and the nothing that had come back; and he had not raised that either. They were, he thought, two people standing on either side of a door each had locked, both holding keys, both waiting for the other to knock.

* * *

Plo Koon found him in the gardens under the Council tower, where the Kel Dor Master went in the evening to stand among the trees he could not smell. They had been friends a long time in the way of two people who had both been Padawans to difficult men. Plo did not sit. He stood with his hands behind his back and his mask turned to the dusk, and talked, for a while, about nothing, which was his courtesy.

"Little 'Soka," he said eventually, "came back from Ilum a different shape."

"They all do."

"Hm. They do." The breath in the mask was slow and even. "She was the last out of the cave. You will have heard."

"I heard."

"The last out, and the first to speak, on the ship. The others were building their sabers with the droid and she sat with hers in her lap and asked him whether a crystal could choose wrong. Huyang said no. She said, *Then why does it feel like mine already?*" Plo's mask tilted. "He had no answer. I have thought about it for a tenday and I have no answer."

"Crystals are strange."

"They are. She is stranger." A pause, the length of a breath. "I brought her from Shili myself, Obi-Wan. She was three years old and she did not cry when I lifted her. Her mother cried. The child looked at me as if she were checking something. I have never forgotten it. I told myself: she is a strong child, she is a calm child, the Force is with her early. Seven years I have told myself." He turned, slightly. "She is older than her years. I do not mean she is clever. Clever children are easy. I mean that when she is alone in a room and thinks no one is watching, she sits like someone who has buried people."

Obi-Wan looked at the trees.

He could have said: *I know.* He could have said: *I have watched her since she was four and I do not know why, and she frightens me, and your Padawan's master is afraid of a child of ten, and I have never told anyone.* He could have said: *She holds the saber backwards and your Padawan's master knows where he has seen that and will not look at it.* He could have said a number of things, and all of them were true, and none of them were his to say, and some of them he had not let himself think in words.

"Children are older than their years in this building," he said. "We take them from their mothers at three. What did we expect?"

"That is not an answer."

"It is the one I have, Plo."

The Kel Dor was quiet. Then he put his hand on Obi-Wan's shoulder, as if it were Obi-Wan who had been talking about a child he loved, and left it there a moment, and went back to his trees.

* * *

Some weeks later, near the fiftieth day, he set the boy to the drill called the closing circle, which was the fourth sequence of the Third Form, and which Obi-Wan had taught Anakin at twelve.

It was not difficult. That was the point of it. Soresu was not difficult; Soresu was tedious, and the closing circle was the most tedious thing in it, eleven parries in a ring that tightened a hand's breadth with every turn until the blade was moving in a space no wider than a man's chest, and then the circle opened again and you went round the other way. A remote, a blindfold, a count. It taught nothing except patience, and it could not be done at all by anyone who did not have patience, which was why he had made the boy do it every morning for a year, and why the boy had hated it, and why, at thirteen, in a temper, Anakin had done it blindfolded against two remotes while Obi-Wan ate breakfast and had not been touched once. He had the sequence in his bones. He could do it asleep; he had, Obi-Wan was reasonably sure, once done it asleep.

"Closing circle," Obi-Wan said, in the small dojo on the second level with the door shut. "Blind. One remote. I'll count."

Anakin looked at him. The boy had grown again; the practice tunic was short at the wrist and the braid had been re-plaited badly, by himself. There was a pause where there should not have been a pause, and then he said "Yes, Master," and tied the cloth over his eyes, and Obi-Wan sent the remote up and began to count.

The first ring was fine. The second was fine. On the third the boy's blade was a finger's width too far outside on the fifth parry, and the remote stung him on the forearm, and he hissed, and brought it back. On the fourth ring he was late on the ninth. On the fifth he dropped his guard on the left as the circle tightened, which was the one place a Padawan who had done this a thousand mornings could not drop it, because the body remembered that the left was where the circle bit; and the remote found his ribs, and his shoulder, and his ribs again, and the training saber's hum wandered up and down as his wrist went loose, and at the sixth ring he stopped, and pulled the cloth off, and stood breathing with his face flushed to the hairline, and said, "Sorry. I lost it. I'll go again."

Obi-Wan said nothing.

He had watched the whole thing with his arms folded, and he had watched it the way one watches a man forge a signature, not for the shape of the letters but for the pen. The misses were wrong. Not the fact of them; the places. A boy who was tired, or careless, or sulking, missed at the wide end of the ring where there was room to miss. This boy had missed at the narrow end, where there was none, where the sequence was so deep in the hands that a boy with a fever did it right; and between the misses, on the seventh and eighth parries of the fifth ring, when the circle was at its tightest and the remote was firing twice a second, the blade had been exactly, beautifully where it should be, a fraction early, with a kind of lazy economy that Obi-Wan had never taught him and had seen somewhere, and had put away.

He had failed it on purpose. He had failed it badly on purpose, and the badness was the confession, because a boy who had something to hide would hide it well, and this one had hidden it like a child putting a broken cup behind his back while still holding the handle.

"Anakin."

"Master."

"Put the saber down."

The boy put it down. He stood with his hands at his sides and the blindfold crushed in one of them, and he was fifteen, and narrow, and the flush was going out of his face and leaving it pale.

"You are hiding something from me," Obi-Wan said. "I would rather you lied to me well than badly."

There was a silence in the dojo that had the quality of a held breath. Anakin's eyes went to the floor, to the saber, to the door, to his own boots. His mouth opened and shut. Something went down the bond, thin as it had been for fifty days, a flicker that was not words, that was close to *please*, and was gone before Obi-Wan could be sure of it. The boy did not say *I'm not*, which was what a boy would say. He did not say *what do you mean*. He stood there with the blindfold in his fist and his braid over his collar and said nothing at all, with his whole body, the way a child says nothing when he has decided that nothing is the only thing he can afford.

Obi-Wan waited long enough to be certain that was all he was going to get. Then he unfolded his arms.

"All right," he said.

"Master—"

"All right. Pick that up. Put it on the rack." He went to the door and opened it and stood with his hand on the frame. "Change your tunic. Wash your face. Meet me in the hangar in a quarter of an hour."

* * *

Knight Sabo Thane had a speeder bay on the north lip of the hangar and a face that gave nothing away, which was why Obi-Wan liked him. The speeder was an old open two-seater the Temple kept for errands in the Senate District, dented along one flank, fast enough. Thane handed him the chit and did not ask, and Obi-Wan signed it and did not say.

Anakin came down the hangar in a clean tunic with his hair still wet and stopped three paces off, and looked at the speeder, and looked at him.

"Where are we going?"

"We are not going anywhere. You are." He held out the chit. "The day is yours. You are excused from Drallig at the first bell tomorrow, I have told him, and he said something I will not repeat, so you owe me for that. Take the speeder. Bring it back with fuel in it and the paint no worse than it is."

The boy did not take the chit. "Why?"

"Because you have not had a day since the ice, and before that you had not had one since I cannot remember, and a Master who is any use at all knows when to stop looking." He kept his hand out. "I am not going to ask where you go. I am not going to reach for you. If anyone asks me where my Padawan is I will say I gave him the day and I did not enquire, and they will think me careless, and that will be true." A beat. "Go on."

Anakin took the chit. His hand, taking it, was a boy's hand, big-knuckled, the nails bitten, and it was not quite steady, and he closed it round the thing and looked at it as if it were in a language he had once known.

"Master," he said. "I—"

"Don't."

The boy shut his mouth.

"Don't," Obi-Wan said, more gently. "Not today. If there is a thing to say, it will keep, and if it will not keep then you had better be sure of it, and you are not. Go and drive somewhere too fast. Do not go to the Works; it is full of people who will sell a Padawan's saber for the price of a drink. Do not go to the Senate District either, or if you do, do not tell me." He stepped back. "I mean it, my friend. Go."

He watched the speeder lift off the lip and drop into the lane, the boy's head bare and the braid lifting in the wind, and bank south, and go into the traffic, and be gone. He did not follow. He stood in the hangar mouth with his hands in his sleeves and the wind off the city on his face and did not reach down the bond, though it was there, thin and slack as a line let out; he let it stay slack. It was the one thing he could give. It was not nothing. It was a hand taken off a shoulder, and he had spent four years learning to keep the hand there, and it cost him more than he had expected to take it away.

He knew the boy would go somewhere. He knew that where he went would tell him something, if he looked. He chose not to look. It was, he thought, walking back up through the Temple with the afternoon going gold on the stone, the first thing he had chosen in some weeks rather than merely done.

In their quarters he made tea and let it go cold again. He took the night-pad out of the drawer where he kept it and sat with it on his knee. Two columns. *Written. Not written.* The first was long. The second was longer. He looked at the second for a while and did not add to it, and then, at the bottom, in a hand that was smaller than his usual, he wrote: *He failed the circle at the narrow end.* He looked at that. He drew a line under it and wrote nothing else.

The boy came back after the ninth bell. Obi-Wan heard the speeder in the hangar, far below, and the lift, and the boy's step in the corridor, quieter than it had been a year ago, and the door, and did not get up. "I'm back," Anakin said through the wall. "Fuel's in it. Paint's fine." "Good." "Master?" "Go to bed, Anakin." A pause. "Goodnight," the boy said, and the inner door closed, and after a while the bond settled into the shape it took when he slept, and Obi-Wan sat with the cold tea and the pad and the lamp, and did not ask himself where the speeder had been, and found that he knew, and did not look at that either.

* * *

He slept, eventually, and the desert came.

It always came the same way. There was no beginning to it. He was simply there, on the ridge, with the sand under his knees and the two suns going down and the wind that never stopped, and below him the farm: the low domes, the pale lip of the courtyard pit, the vaporators standing out across the flat like a row of thin patient men. He had watched this farm for a long time. He did not know, in the dream, how long. He knew the shapes of the domes the way he knew his own hands. He knew where the woman hung the washing and where the man parked the speeder and which of the vaporators was always failing; he knew the hour the boy came out to it with the tool bag, and he waited for that hour, every time, with his hand pressed flat on the sand as if he could feel the boy's step through it.

The boy came out. Small, at this distance. Fair-headed. He went to the failing vaporator and knelt and opened the panel, and the wind took his hair, and Obi-Wan on the ridge did not breathe.

He never turned round. That was the dream. That was all the dream had ever been: the ridge, the farm, the boy's back, the wind, and a feeling in Obi-Wan's chest of such weight that he woke from it, always, as if he had been dropped.

This time the boy turned round.

He stood up from the vaporator with the tool in his hand and he turned, slowly, as if someone had called him, and looked up the slope at the ridge, at the man kneeling there, at Obi-Wan; and across all that distance and that light the boy's face was clear, and young, and known, and it was—

He was awake.

He was sitting up in the narrow bed with the sheet in both fists and the room black and the lamp off and the Temple silent round him, and there was a name in his mouth. He could feel the shape of it. His lips had made the first of it and his breath had stopped on the second, and he sat there in the dark and held it, the whole name, behind his teeth, the way one holds a mouthful of water on a march.

He did not say it aloud.

Through the wall the boy was asleep. The bond lay thin and slack and untroubled, a line let out into still water. Nothing had come down it. Nothing had woken the younglings on the lower levels. Whatever had turned round on that ridge had turned round for him alone, and he sat with it in the black and the quiet with a name he did not say aloud and had not said aloud in a very long time, and his hands, when he opened them at last and laid them flat on the sheet, were not steady, and he let them not be, since there was no one there to see.

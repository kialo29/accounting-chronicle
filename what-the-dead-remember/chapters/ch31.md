## Chapter 31: Shmi

The lie was Obi-Wan's, and Anakin stood beside him in the Council chamber and watched him tell it.

It was a small lie, well made. A trade contact of Dex's in Hutt space had passed word of slaver activity on the Mos Espa road, a shipment of Twi'leks moving through Tatooine under Hutt paper, nothing that merited a Knight at full strength but something that ought to be looked at, and Master Kenobi, who was not yet at full strength, proposed that he look at it. A short assignment. A convalescent one, if the Council liked the word. He would take his Padawan, who knew the ground. He said all this standing straight with his left arm held a little too still at his side, the sling gone two days and the shoulder not yet forgiving him for it, and he said it in the voice he used for the Senate, courteous and faintly bored, as if it had not occurred to him that anyone might wonder what a boy born on that planet might want there. Yoda said nothing. Windu looked at Obi-Wan, and then at Anakin, and then at Obi-Wan again, a long flat look with the whole of Oba Diah in it, and said, "Ten days," and that was all. Walking out down the tower stair, Anakin thought that this man had once been unable to lie to a Council to save his own life, and had learnt to do it to save a boy's, and that it was a shared craft between them now, a thing they did well together; and that he was not sure he ought to be proud of how good Obi-Wan had got.

He did not ask why. He had not asked for it. Obi-Wan had come into the common room four nights after the roof, with the list face down on the table between them and the tea going cold, and said, "Your mother is alive," as if it were a thing Anakin might have forgotten, and then, "I think you should see her while she is," and had gone to bed.

* * *

The heat found him on the ramp.

He had known it would. He had told himself so on the way down through the atmosphere, in the cramped Temple courier with the light coming gold through the port and Obi-Wan asleep against the bulkhead with his bad arm cradled in his good one. He had told himself: it will be hot, you were born in it, it is only weather. And then the ramp went down at Mos Eisley and the air came in and laid itself on his face and his hands and the back of his neck like something alive, and the boy's body took a breath and could not find the end of it, and he stood on the ramp with his mouth open like a child who has never seen the sun.

Twenty-three years. The suit had regulated everything. It had kept him at a temperature chosen by a medical droid and had never once let the weather of any world touch what was left of his skin, and here was Tatooine at midday, two suns, the pale one high and the orange one lower, and the heat went through his tunic and found the narrow back and the thin arms and the flush that climbed his throat whether he wished it or not, and he was sweating before he reached the bottom of the ramp. He put his hand up to shade his eyes and the hand was wet. He looked at it.

"It is only weather," Obi-Wan said behind him, mildly, and Anakin realised he had said it aloud, in the ship, more than once.

The last time he had walked Mos Eisley he had been looking for the hut of a dead man, with troopers ahead and behind, and the town had emptied in front of him the way water empties from a cracked basin. He walked the same streets now at the shoulder of the same man, who was thirty-one and limping very slightly and buying a speeder from a Jawa, and the town did not empty. A Rodian jostled him. A child ran past with a water-skin. The smell was the same, dust and animal and machine oil and the sour tang of vaporator brine, and he stood in it and did not shield, for once, against anything.

The speeder was old and the front repulsor dragged left and Anakin drove it, because Obi-Wan's arm would not take the yoke for three hours, and because it was his planet. They went out over the salt flats with the two suns moving round on their left and nothing in any direction but what there had always been. He had last driven this road at nineteen, with his mother a month gone and the Force screaming in his ears; he drove it now with his hands quite steady, and felt the road remembering him.

Anchorhead went past, a few domes and a cantina and a tower for the water. Then the homestead.

* * *

It was smaller than he had kept it.

The dome stood up out of the sand with its entry arch and its two low wings, bleached the colour of the sand, and beyond it, in rows that walked away to the horizon, the vaporators stood with their vanes turning slowly, and the pit of the courtyard was a square of shadow in the ground to the left of the dome with the stair going down into it. He had buried her on the rise beyond the vaporators. There was nothing on the rise. He looked at it, once, as the speeder came in, and then he looked at the man coming up out of the dome, and he put the speeder down.

Cliegg Lars was broad and plain and had both his legs. Anakin had not known he was braced for that until the man came out of the arch and walked to meet them, walked, two legs, a farmer's easy rolling gait across the sand, and something in Anakin's chest that had been clenched for an hour let go. Cliegg had a face the suns had made and a working man's hands and he looked at the two of them, the Jedi and the boy, with the particular courtesy of a man who has never once asked a Jedi for anything and does not intend to begin now.

"Master Kenobi," he said. "She said you'd come when you could. Didn't say it would be today." His eyes went to Anakin and stayed. "You're him."

"Yes, sir."

"You've her look about the eyes." That seemed to be all he meant to say about it. "She's below. Owen." This to the boy who had come up behind him and stopped in the arch with his arms folded. "Take the speeder round. It's dragging a repulsor, anyone can hear it."

Owen Lars was sixteen and already had the shoulders he would have at fifty. He did not move. He looked at Anakin with the look of a boy who has decided that the thing in front of him will bring trouble to his house and means to be ready for it. He looked at Obi-Wan longer. Then he came and took the speeder's control stalk out of Anakin's hand without a word and drove it round the side of the dome, left repulsor dragging, and Anakin stood on his mother's land and tried to remember how to walk.

"Go on," Obi-Wan said quietly. "I'll follow."

He went down the stair into the courtyard.

She was at the bottom of it.

She had heard the speeder and had come out of the kitchen with her hands still wet and she had not dried them, she had stopped at the foot of the stair with her hands held a little away from her sides, dripping, and she was looking up. She wore a work apron over a rough blue dress. Her hair was pulled back and tied and there was grey in it at the temples, two wings of grey that had not been there when he was nine and that he had seen, in the other life, only in a tent in the dark by the light of a fire outside, matted with blood. Her face was lined by the suns. Her eyes were the same. She looked at him coming down the stair, a boy with a braid behind his ear and a sand-coloured tunic dark with sweat, and her mouth opened and nothing came out of it, and then she said, "Ani. Ani." She dried one hand on her apron at last and held it up toward his head, not touching. "I told Cliegg you'd be taller than this."

He had thought he could do this.

He got to the bottom of the stair and she put her wet hands on his face. He was taller than she was. He had not been, the last time she held him, the first time, the time he had thought of every day for thirty-six years. She had to tip her head back. She put her hands on his face and her thumbs under his eyes and looked at him the way she had looked at him at the door of the hovel in Mos Espa with the ship's engines already running behind him, as if she were learning him for the last time, and he put his arms round her and held on.

She was alive. She was warm and she smelt of cooking and the brine and of herself, and her heart was going under his ear where it had gone when he was small enough to be carried, and she was saying his name into his hair. Ani. Ani. He had forty-five years in him and a war and a suit and the deaths of more people than this planet held and he stood in her arms and was nine. He could not help it. It came up through him like water through sand.

"Let me look at you." She held him off. The thumbs again, under his eyes. "You're thin. They don't feed you."

"They feed me."

"You're thin." She looked harder. He saw her find something. He did not know what she had found; he had got his face into the shape of a boy's and his presence down to the size of a boy's and the wall up, and still she looked at him, his mother, who had known every lie he told from the age of three, and her eyes went over him the way they had gone over him when he came in from the podrace yard with his lip split and said he had fallen. "What's happened to you?"

"Nothing. Mum. Nothing's happened. I'm all right."

She looked at him. She did not say that she did not believe him. She never had. She put one hand flat on his chest as if to feel what was going on inside it, and kept it there, and looked, and then she let it go and said, "Well. You're here," and turned him by the shoulder towards the kitchen door, and that was how he knew she had not believed a word.

* * *

Owen found him at the far row an hour before the suns went down.

He had gone to the vaporators because he could not be in the dome. Cliegg had said over the midday water that the two end units on the north row had been giving a third of their yield for a month and that he had a man coming from Anchorhead who had not come, and Anakin had said he would look, in the boy's voice, the eager one, and had gone up the stair into the heat with a tool roll from the shop under his arm and had been glad of it, of the walk out along the rows with the sand burning through the soles of his boots and the vanes creaking and the sweat running into his eyes, because it was a thing to do with his hands.

The vaporators were old GX-8s with the refrigeration coil in the base and the intake vanes on a mast, and the two end units had the same fault, which was not the fault Cliegg thought. He had the base plate off the second one and was lying on his back in the sand under the housing with his arm up inside it to the shoulder when he heard the boots.

"That's the compressor," Owen said, above him. "Told him it was the compressor."

"It's not the compressor."

"It's the compressor. Yield drops when the heat drops. Compressor's not cycling at night." A pause. "You'll have been on Coruscant. They don't have vaporators on Coruscant."

Anakin lay under the housing with his hand on the thing that was wrong, which was a brine-line sensor crusted with salt so that it reported full and shut the coil off early, a two-minute fix with a wire brush, and thought about the many things he could say. He could say that he had built his first vaporator relay at six out of parts Watto threw away. He could say that he knew this boy, that he had come once to this farm in the suit after Yavin and stood over the two graves Luke had dug by the homestead before Yavin, and had not let the troopers touch them. He could say a great many things, and the boy above him was sixteen and had been right about a compressor on this farm before, and lived here, and would go on living here when Anakin had gone, and would be the one who kept it running.

He wiped the sensor with his thumb and let the salt fall where Owen could not see it.

"Could be the compressor," he said. "Show me."

Owen got down. He got down on his knees in the sand and then on his side and put his head in under the housing beside Anakin's, close enough that Anakin could smell him, sweat and sand and the oil they used on the vanes, and he reached up past Anakin's arm and put his hand on the compressor and said, "Here. Feel. Should be ticking over. It's not."

It was not, because the coil was off, because the sensor had told it to be. Anakin put his hand where Owen's was and felt the stillness and said, "You're right," and Owen said, "Told you," and then, after a moment, in a different voice, the voice of a boy who has been right in front of a stranger and finds that it has not satisfied him as he expected, "Dad'll want to know what it needs."

"Cycle it manually tonight and see if it holds. If it's the compressor it'll drop again by morning."

It would not drop again by morning. Owen would come out at dawn and find both units at full yield and would decide that the manual cycle had cleared it, and would tell his father so, and would be believed. Anakin lay beside him under the housing and listened to the vanes turn and did not care. He had been right about everything, once, for nineteen years. It had been a very expensive way to be right.

"She reads them out," Owen said. He was looking up at the housing, not at Anakin. "Your letters. At supper. Dad pretends he's not listening."

"I know. She said."

"You write a lot about speeders."

"There's not much else I'm allowed to write about."

Owen thought about that. "They read them? The Jedi?"

"Someone does."

"Hm." The boy's hand was still on the compressor. "And him. He reads them."

"He lets them go. That's the thing he does. Nobody else would have." Anakin heard the man in his own voice and put the boy back into it. "He's all right. He's — he's all right, Owen."

"He looks at the house," Owen said, "like he's buying it," and got up, and brushed the sand off, and stood for a moment with the suns behind him, sixteen and square and already the shape of the man who would stand at the foot of that stair and say not this year either, and then went back along the row towards the dome without waiting, which was, Anakin understood, a degree. It was more than he had got in the other life, where Owen had put out a hand in a courtyard and said his name with a stranger's care.

He put the base plate back on with the tool from his belt, a slim Temple hydrospanner with his initials scratched in the handle because Padawans lost them, and then, because the far units were a long walk from the dome and it was a thing a boy who liked machines might do, he spent twenty minutes at the top of the mast with the comm relay, so that if the vanes on these two stopped turning the dome would know it. Then he went in.

* * *

Supper was in the dome, in the low room with the curved walls and the table that had been Cliegg's father's, and there were five of them round it, and Anakin watched Obi-Wan be looked at.

He had not thought about it until it was happening. He had thought about the vaporators and the courtyard and the rise beyond the rows and about not shielding and about shielding, and had not thought: Shmi will sit across the table from him. She did. She served the stew and the flatbread and the cup of blue milk each that was all the farm allowed itself, and she sat down across from Obi-Wan Kenobi and put her hands together on the table and looked at him with the whole of her attention, and said, "I wanted to thank you. For the letters. I know what it cost. I know it's not done."

"It is not done," Obi-Wan agreed. "I have been told so several times."

"I don't know what I'd have had without them. I had nine years of him. Then nothing, until the letters. I would have gone on with nothing. People do." Her hands stayed together. "You didn't have to."

"No," Obi-Wan said, and nothing else, and Anakin saw him not say the rest.

He saw it. He knew this man's face better than he had ever known his own, which he had not seen without a mask for twenty-three years, and he watched Obi-Wan sit across a farm table from Luke's grandmother, in Luke's house, with Luke's uncle at sixteen eating stew beside him, and be thanked, and he knew exactly where Obi-Wan was. Not here. Not at this table nineteen years from now with a different woman serving, either; he had never sat at that table. He had stood on a rise in the dark and watched the window and gone home to the hut and the tea things. He had brought the child and handed him over and Owen had not let him in the door, and now Owen's stepmother was giving him bread with her own hands, and Obi-Wan took the bread and said thank you and his left hand, under the table, was holding the edge of the bench.

"He was a good boy," Shmi said. "Is he a good boy, Master Kenobi? He'll tell me he's all right and I'd like to hear it from someone who'd say if he wasn't."

Obi-Wan looked at Anakin across the table. Anakin looked at his bowl.

"He is," Obi-Wan said, "the best I have known. I would not say so in front of him on Coruscant, it would be bad for him. But he is." A breath. "He frightens me, sometimes. He is very able. But he is good, Shmi. I will say that anywhere."

Cliegg grunted. Owen ate. Shmi looked at her son with her head on one side for a long time, and then she got up to fetch the water, and put her hand on the back of his neck as she passed, and left it there a moment, and went on.

* * *

She caught him at the foot of the stair when the others had gone to their beds.

Cliegg kept a farm's hours and Owen with him; the lamps in the dome were out by full dark and Obi-Wan had gone up the stair to the speeder to sleep where the comm was, he said, which was his way of leaving the house to Anakin. He had meant to go after him. He had got as far as the foot of the stair with the courtyard dark round him and the stars in the square of sky above and his mother came out of the kitchen with no lamp and said, "You're off early."

"Before the suns. We have to be in Mos Eisley by midday. Master Obi-Wan has to —"

"Look at slavers. Yes." She came and stood by him. She was in her nightclothes with a shawl over them and her hair down and grey, and she looked, in the starlight, like an old woman, which she was not and never would be. "I won't be up. I'm not going to say goodbye to you twice in a yard, I did it once. So." She put her hand out. "Give me that."

He did not know what she meant. She reached round his belt and took the hydrospanner out of its loop, the slim one with his initials, and turned it over in her hands and put it in the pocket of her shawl.

"Mum —"

"You'll want it back." She said it quite plainly. "So you'll come. That's all. I'm not asking you anything. I'm not asking you what happened to you, I can see that you can't say and I can see that it's bad, and I'm not going to make you lie to me in my own house, I had enough of that from Watto." Her hand came up and found his face in the dark. "You're all right. You said. So you're all right. Go to bed, Ani."

She kissed him on the forehead, which she had to reach for, and went in, and he heard the door of the room she shared with Cliegg close, and he stood in the courtyard of her house with the loop at his belt empty and could not move.

He would not come. He knew it the way he knew his own hands. He would come in four years on a borrowed speeder bike with the Force howling and find the loop at his belt empty and her gone a month, and whether that was this life or the other he could not, standing here, tell. He had told her he was all right. He had told her nothing. He had done the one thing he had come to do, which was to let her hold him, and the other thing, the thing he had known since the ramp at Mos Eisley that he was going to try to do, he had not done yet.

He went up the stair into the dark.

* * *

The rise was a quarter of an hour's walk beyond the last row.

He went out along the north row past the two units he had fixed, their vanes turning now in the cold wind that came off the flats after dark, and past the end of the row where the farm stopped and the planet began, and up the slope of the rise with the sand sliding under his boots, and sat down at the top of it where he had sat before.

There was nothing there.

He had thought he would be able to see it. He had thought the ground would know. He had knelt here with Cliegg's hover-chair whining behind him and Padmé's hand on his shoulder and had put a stone on the sand with her name cut into it; and he had stood here in the suit, after Yavin, with the troopers waiting at the bottom of the rise, and had looked at the stones for a long time and had not been able to kneel, because the suit did not kneel; and he sat now on the same sand under the same stars with the farm a dark shape below and one green light in it, Obi-Wan's comm, and the rise was a rise. It was sand. Nothing had ever been put in it. He put his hand on the ground where her grave would be and it was only ground, and the grave was in him, where it had always been, and the not-grave was under his hand, and he could not tell which was less bearable.

She was in the dome asleep with his hydrospanner in her shawl.

He sat for a long time. The cold came up through him. The boy's body shivered and he let it. He thought of nothing in particular and then of a particular thing, which was a camp. He knew where it was. It would not be where it was now in four years, the Tuskens moved with the seasons and the water, but he knew their range, he had learnt it in the suit from the Imperial survey charts with a thoroughness he had never explained to anyone, and he knew, more or less, within a night's ride, where the band that would take her was lying up this season. There were perhaps forty of them. Some of them, tonight, were children.

He stood up, and went down the far side of the rise, away from the farm.

He had his saber. He did not take it off his belt. He walked out across the flat with the moons on his left and the empty loop at his hip, and he let himself think it through, because he had never once in the other life thought anything through. He could go tonight and be back by the second sun and nobody would know. He could do it cleanly, which he had not done then: one blade, in the dark, the sentries first, the men, and leave the rest. He could make it so that the band which took her did not exist to take her. He had the skill. He had the practice. And he was fifteen and nobody would believe it of him, and the one man who would know had already lied to a Council for him twice.

He walked until the farm's lit window was gone behind the rise and there was nothing but the flat and the moons and the far line of the Jundland dark where the mountains were.

Then he stopped.

He did not know what stopped him. It was not mercy; he had never been able to make mercy out of nothing, and there was nothing out here. It was not the children, or not only. It was something more like arithmetic. He stood on the flat with the wind pushing at him and understood, quite coldly, that he had come here to do the same thing he had done before, four years early and better organised, and that the man who did it better organised was not an improvement on the man who had done it screaming. He had been that man. He had been him in a Temple with the fires going up the stairs. He had been him on a bridge with a boy who called him father. He knew exactly what it felt like to walk into a place with a blade and come out with everyone in it dead, and it felt like this. It felt like standing on a cold flat at night with a good reason. Every one of them had had a good reason.

He stood there until the shivering stopped being the cold.

Then he turned round, and walked back.

* * *

Obi-Wan was waiting by the speeder.

He was sitting on the sand with his back against the rear repulsor housing and his cloak round him and his bad arm cradled in his good one, where the comm light in the cab was a small green point above his head, and he had been there, Anakin saw from the sand round him, for hours. He had not come out to the rise. He had not come after him onto the flat. He had sat here in the cold with the farm dark beside him and the rows of vanes turning and had waited to see which way the boy came back from, or whether he did.

He looked up when Anakin came round the end of the row. He looked at the saber on his belt and at his hands and at his face, in that order, and Anakin let him.

"You didn't go."

"No."

Obi-Wan nodded once, slowly, as if confirming something on a list. He did not say anything else. He did not say well done, or I knew, or anything a master might say to a Padawan who had been out all night with a weapon. He put his good hand down on the sand and pushed himself up, with a breath, and stood beside the speeder with the cloak falling back off his shoulder, and then he turned, not to Anakin, and looked at the homestead.

He looked at it for a long time.

The dome, pale under the moons. The square dark of the pit with the stair going down. The two wings, the entry arch, the rows of vaporators walking away into the dark with their vanes going round, and beyond them, low and black against the stars, the rise. There was no lamp lit in any of it. There was no sound but the vanes and the wind. Obi-Wan stood with his hand on the side of the speeder and his face turned to the farm and did not move, and Anakin stood a little behind him and to one side and watched him look.

He knew what he was seeing. He had never seen it and he knew. He had been handed the whole of it, four nights after the roof, in Obi-Wan's dry flat voice, as a thing that had happened to someone else: the hut, the Wastes, the walks at dusk to a place on a ridge from where the farm could be seen and could not see him. A boy in the yard at dawn with his head under a vaporator housing and his hair going white in the suns, and a man on a ridge who had carried him there as an infant and had never been let in the door, watching him grow, year on year, and going home. Nineteen years. The whole of Luke's childhood, from a ridge.

Obi-Wan was looking at the rise. He was looking at the rows. He was looking at the place at the end of the north row where a boy had lain on his back under a housing this afternoon with his arm up to the shoulder in the works, and Anakin understood that he was not seeing the boy who had lain there. He was seeing another one. He was standing on his ridge.

Anakin did not say anything. He let him look. The vanes went round. The first of the suns was still an hour under the rim of the world, and the two of them stood by the speeder in the cold and the dark, and the one looked, and the other watched him looking, and neither of them moved to wake the house.

## Chapter 12: Interlude: The Door

The Works kept no time, which was its virtue. Nothing in the district had been switched on in forty years; no shift had been called, no clock wound, and the only sounds that reached the room where Sidious stood were the settling of old iron and, a long way down, water finding its way across a foundry floor poured before he was born. He came here when he wanted to think without the Senate humming in the walls. He had been thinking, on and off, for twenty-two days, and he had enjoyed every one of them.

The boy's mind had been a garden. That was the word he had used for it privately, six years ago, and he had never found a better. A small walled plot, hot and bright, with the mother in the centre of it like a well. He had gone in every tenth day since the boy was nine. He had learned the paths. He had planted things, with care, and pulled up what Kenobi planted, and watched the Jedi's little lessons wither in that heat because the ground was wrong for them. A grievance against a Council that would not trust him. A fear of being sent away. The speeder he was not allowed. The master who waited in the plaza. The mother. Always the mother, and always the way back to her, which ran through this office and nowhere else. It had been slow, pleasurable, uncomplicated work, and he had been very good at it.

Twenty-two days ago the boy had sat in the chair across from him, and Sidious had gone in as he always went in, with the kindly question and the hand on the shoulder, and had found the garden, and the mother, and the speeder, and a girl's name he had not heard before, and beneath all of it, where the ground should have been, a wall.

He had stood in front of it a long moment, in the part of the audience where he was saying something about the Senate's gratitude. It was not a Jedi's wall. He knew Jedi shielding: a clean room, a closed door, serenity laid over the mind like a cloth, and all of it learned from a book. This was old. It had been built in courses over years and it had been struck, hard, more than once, and mended where it was struck, and the mending was rougher than the building. He had put his hand to it, so to speak, and felt it hold, and felt it not care that he was there.

He had pressed nothing. He had smiled, and asked after the boy's letters home, and let the audience run its hour. And at the end of it, standing, the boy had said, *Thank you, my Master,* and corrected himself a breath later to *Chancellor*, flushing to the ears the way fifteen does; and the cadence of it had gone through Sidious like warm wine. Not the words. The cadence. It was how an apprentice said the word. It was how one had said it to him once, kneeling, with horns bowed almost to the floor.

He had gone back to his office and sat with the window at his back and had recognised, with something very close to delight, that he had been surprised.

It had been years.

He did not waste a moment on the impossible. The wall was there; it had been built by someone; that someone had either taught the boy or was the boy's borrowed hand. He had taught that discipline, in its whole form, to two people in his life. One of them had fallen in two pieces down a reactor shaft on Naboo six years ago, and Kenobi had been knighted for it. The other was on Serenno writing pamphlets.

He stood in the dark of the Works and considered Tyranus.

It was not out of character. Dooku was a man of reasons, and a man of reasons could always find one more. He had been Qui-Gon Jinn's master; the boy had been Qui-Gon Jinn's find; it would not be beyond him to think of the child as an inheritance left in the wrong hands. He had the knowledge. He had the pride. And an apprentice who took a pupil of his own, quietly, to present later as a gift, was a thing that had happened before among the Sith, and had usually ended with the gift being used on the giver.

Against it: Dooku had never met the boy. Sidious would have known. There was very little about his apprentice he did not know, including several things Dooku believed were his own. And the wall was old. That was the detail that did not fit any shape he tried it in. Six years of pamphlets would not build it. Six years of anything would not. It had the look of a thing built in a place where it was struck every day.

He let that go for the present, as one sets a piece aside on a board.

The rest he had assembled the way he assembled everything, from the Temple's own leakage. The Jedi believed themselves sealed. They were a city of ten thousand with a public gate, and a Knight came to the Senate Office Building every tenday to carry mission requests, a pleasant man called Aben Tuur who talked, over caf, as men talk who have never been listened to properly. From him Sidious knew that thirty-one children under twelve had woken one night crying from the same dream, and that four of them had dreamed they were the one holding the blade; that the Council had a word for it now and the word was *shadow*. From the Padawans' politics instructor, who dined with a senator from Chalacta who dined with Mas Amedda, he knew that Kenobi's boy had put the Battlemaster's best pupil on the floor of the training hall with a single stroke nobody had taught him, and was doing penance for it each morning before first bell, in front of Drallig, who had said nothing the first morning and then asked him to do it again.

And from Kenobi himself, sitting in that same chair with his hands folded and his courtesy on like armour, he knew that the boy's master had been asked for one evening, the anniversary reception, and had said, *I'll put it to the Council,* which in six years of this had meant no every time.

Sidious found he was smiling, alone, in the dark, and allowed it.

A struck wall. A dream the whole nursery shared. A stroke the Battlemaster had never seen. *My Master,* in that cadence. Something had come into the Temple, and the Jedi were hunting it, and the Jedi were looking in every direction except at the one boy they had never liked. He thought it was very possible the Order would do his work for him before the year was out; it had a long history of that. But he did not intend to wait for it.

He woke the projector.

The figure that formed in the air was seated, which was Dooku's way of reminding him that on Serenno it was not yet dawn, and rose. The robe was dark; the beard was precise; the hands were folded at the waist in the manner he had carried out of the Order with him along with the posture and the contempt.

"Master."

"Tyranus. When were you last on Coruscant?"

A pause of exactly the right length. "The spring session. The Institute's address. You were in the gallery."

"I was. And since?"

"Serenno. Twice to Raxus on the Institute's business. Nowhere else."

"Have you touched Kenobi's Padawan?"

The pause this time was not the right length, and that was itself information. "I have never seen the boy."

"That is not what I asked."

"No, Master." The old man's chin came up a fraction. "I have not. I would not. He is Qui-Gon's; I have always understood he was yours."

Sidious watched the blue light of the face and the stillness under it, and read it, and believed it almost entirely. The remainder he put with the other piece he had set aside, and did not look at it yet.

"Something has," he said. "The boy's mind is shut. It is shut in a manner you would recognise, and that I would recognise, and that I did not teach him. I wish to know who did. You will come to Coruscant."

"For what occasion?"

"The anniversary of Naboo. The Chancellor receives the heroes of the battle, as he does every year, and this year the Jedi will send Master Kenobi and his Padawan, because I have asked for them in a form that cannot be refused twice. A Count who has made such a name for himself denouncing the Senate's corruption can surely attend a reception in honour of the Senate's one honest victory. It will be remarked on. Let it be remarked on. You will be my guest."

"You want me to look at him."

"I want you to look at him. Nothing more. You will not touch what I have not told you to touch." He let that sit. "Kenobi has kept the boy from me for six years with a wall of small courtesies, and I have let him, because a boy who is kept from a thing wants it. He has now put it to the Council whether the child may be spared for an evening. The Council will tell him what I tell it. In the meantime Yoda is taking him to Ilum in two days, with the initiates, for the Gathering."

"Yoda," Dooku said, and nothing else, and a great deal.

"Just so. Yoda is not a fool, whatever else he is. He will stand the boy on the ice and watch him, and he will see something, and he will not know what he has seen, and he will say nothing about it to anyone, because that is what Yoda does. That gives us some weeks." Sidious inclined his head. "Go back to bed, Tyranus. Write something stirring about the Rim."

The figure bowed, and folded, and was gone, and the dark of the Works came back in.

There was the Senate to think of, and he let it come, because it was restful. The Naboo girl's bill was a nuisance of the kind he found almost endearing: a reform of Outer Rim franchise law, drafted in Amidala's apartment with Organa holding the stylus and Mothma holding the numbers, requiring the great trade houses to disclose their holdings in the systems they shipped from and placing the disputes in a tribunal the Rim would sit on. It was well made. It would go to committee, and in committee it would acquire friends, and the friends would improve it until it did nothing, and the girl would vote for it anyway because it had her name on it, and he would thank her for it in public. He had done this before. She did not yet understand that she was being taught.

The term was the thing. The law had its arithmetic, and the arithmetic was nearly done with him, and there was no crisis yet of the size that made a Senate forget its own rules. There would be. The Rim was dry tinder; it only wanted a man of standing to say aloud what everyone on Raxus and Sullust and Eriadu already muttered, and say it in good sentences, and be seen to be sincere. The pamphlets were doing that. The Institute was doing that. He had read Dooku's last address with real pleasure, the way one reads a letter one has dictated and finds improved in the copying. Another year, perhaps two, and the Rim would be unhappy in an organised way, and the Senate would be frightened in a disorganised one, and the Chancellor would be asked to stay.

And the reception was a stage, which was all a reception was for: the Naboo girl at his elbow as the hero of the hour, the Jedi in their brown with the boy among them, Dooku in black at the far end drawing every eye that was not on the Chancellor. He would stand in the middle of it with a glass he did not drink from and let them all look at each other.

He turned the projector off at the wall and stood in the full dark, and the thought he had set aside came back, because he had known it would.

Two people. He had taught that discipline to two people. One of them was on Serenno, and had just told him the truth. The other had gone down a shaft on Naboo in two pieces, and Sidious had felt him go, the way one feels a lamp go out in another room. He had not gone to look. There had been a great deal to do that week.

He left it there. He was a patient man, and there were doors it was more useful to leave shut and know the whereabouts of than to open; and the boy would be on Ilum in two days, on the ice, with Yoda watching, and that was enough to be going on with.

* * *

On Serenno it was still an hour short of light, and Dooku did not go back to bed.

He went out instead onto the upper terrace, where the house looked down three tiers of garden to the valley and the valley looked up at nothing, and stood with his hands behind him in the cold. The house was the old house; his family had built it when the Republic was young and had not improved it since, and he had come back to it at seventy with his lightsaber and his name and had changed nothing but the locks. There were nineteen people in it who kept it running. He saw perhaps two of them in a week. The lamps were lit before he rose and the terrace was swept before he walked on it, and he had come to think of this as the proper condition of a house: that it should work, and that one should not have to watch it working.

Qui-Gon's boy.

He turned the phrase over; he had used it without deciding to. He had never seen the child. He knew what everyone knew: that Qui-Gon had found a slave on Tatooine and brought him to the Council like a man bringing a sacred relic down from a mountain, and the Council had refused him, and then Qui-Gon had died, and the Council had taken the boy anyway because the dead are harder to refuse. He knew Kenobi had him. He had met Kenobi four times in his life and thought him exactly what Qui-Gon would have made of a clever, anxious, obedient young man: a good Jedi, which was to say a wasted one. And he had read the Council's own phrase for the boy in the minutes that someone had seen fit to leak to the Institute: *the chosen one*, with no capitals, as if it were an embarrassment, which to them it was.

He had never gone to see him. It had been a decision, taken once, and not revisited: the boy was the Master's, and the Master's things were not touched, and in any case he had not wanted to stand in front of a child and see Qui-Gon's judgement looking back at him out of a stranger's face. He had told Sidious the truth. He had told it in the voice he had used on the Council for forty years, and he had felt the Master weigh it even through a projector.

Almost believed. He was seventy-six years old and had been almost believed by two institutions now.

He let himself think about the first one for as long as it took the sky over the eastern ridge to go from black to slate. Qui-Gon dead on Naboo, in a generator room, run through by a thing the Council had not believed existed until it was standing in front of them, and the Council in their circle afterwards with their hands in their sleeves, saying *grieve* and *trust* and *the Force* as if the words were a wall one could stand behind. Twelve chairs and not one of them had gone to Naboo. He had gone. He had stood in the hangar where they had burned Qui-Gon and had understood that the Order was a body kept alive after the mind had left it, and that it would go on moving, and taking children, and sending them out to die for Senate committees, for as long as it was permitted to. He had gone back and said so, for a year, and been listened to with great courtesy, and then he had stopped saying it and begun doing something about it.

Yaddle he did not think about. That was a room in the house he kept locked. It had been necessary; he had been told so by the only person whose word on necessity he accepted; and he had done it himself, which was the most he could say.

Light came down into the valley in a long slant and found the river.

He had obeyed. He had obeyed for six years, and he had never been able to decide whether obedience was the thing he had left the Order to escape or the thing he had left it to find. He had wanted a master who was worth it. He had found one; he was still, most days, certain of that. And he was proud, which Sidious knew and used, and which Dooku knew Sidious used and had decided, at seventy, was an acceptable price for being used by a competent man rather than ignored by twelve incompetent ones. It was only lately, on this terrace before dawn, that he had begun to notice that a price could be acceptable and still be paid.

The Chancellor's reception. He saw it entire: the rotunda done up in Naboo's colours, the Queen's party in their paint, the Senator in something too plain for the occasion, the Jedi standing in a group the way Jedi stood at receptions, as if waiting for a shuttle. And himself walking in, a Count, a critic of all they stood for, and every head turning, and the Chancellor's warm old hand coming out to take his. It would be reported. The Master wanted it reported.

And a boy. Somewhere in the room, in brown, with a braid behind his ear, fifteen years old, with a wall in his mind that the Master could not get over and had not built, and that Dooku had been sent to look at as one sends a man to look at a horse.

He found that he wanted to see it. That was the unlovely truth. A Sith wall in a Jedi child, raised in a Temple full of people trained to notice exactly that and failing to; Kenobi, of all people, standing in front of it with his courtesies; Yoda about to take it to Ilum and stand it on the ice. He had spent sixty years in the Order and six outside it and he had never seen a thing he could not account for, and he had just been told, by the one person he believed, that there was one.

He did not like the errand. He did not like being sent, or being almost believed, or being told to look and not touch, as if he were a man who touched things. He would go. He would stand in the Chancellor's rotunda in black and let the Senate stare, and he would find the boy, and he would look.

The first of the house came out onto the lower terrace with a broom, saw him, and went in again without a sound. Dooku stayed where he was until the sun was fully on the river, and then he went in to write something stirring about the Rim.

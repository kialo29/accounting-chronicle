## Chapter 22: Oba Diah

Oba Diah had no sky to speak of; it had a lid. Obi-Wan Kenobi watched it come up through the courier's forward port, a world the colour of wet slate under a cloud deck that never broke, and the spires rising out of it like something that had been poured and then left to set. They were not towers in any sense a Coruscanti would have recognised. They were too narrow and too many, black, with lights in them at irregular heights, and from the low approach they looked less built than grown, the way a mineral grows in a dark place where nobody has thought to stop it.

The pilot, a Temple courier who had said perhaps forty words in ten days, said, "There's our beacon," and Obi-Wan said, "So there is," and went aft to get his cloak.

He had been calm for ten days. Calm was a discipline and he was good at it; he had been good at it for a long time, longer than anyone aboard this ship could have guessed, and it had held through the jump out of Coruscant and the dull stretch past Ord Mantell and the last two days in which he had read the survey team's file four times and his own briefing twice and had not opened the case under his bunk, which was heavier than it ought to have been and which he had not needed to bring and had brought. Calm held. It held now, with the spires coming up and his hands steady on the clasp of the cloak. What it did not do was reach his body. His body had known since the Council chamber, since the words *mineral rights* and *Pyke* had been said in the same breath by Master Windu reading from a flimsi, and it had been telling him in the small ways bodies have (a jaw that would not quite loosen, a sleep that came late and left early) that this was a place he should not go.

He was going. Eleven people were in a room at the top of one of those spires, and the Council had agreed that Padawan Skywalker would remain at the Temple at his master's own request, and a senator from Tyrrhus whose name Obi-Wan had had to look up had asked for Master Kenobi by name, and all of that could be thought about later, in order, with tea. Now there was a landing pad coming up out of the dark, lit along its edges in a cold green, and a line of tall figures standing on it with their hands folded in front of them, and he had a job.

"Stay with the ship," he told the pilot. "If I am not back in six hours, you will not come looking for me. You will go up and make the jump and tell the Council exactly that."

The pilot looked at him. "Six hours."

"Six," said Obi-Wan, "is generous," and went down the ramp into the smell of the place, which was burnt and metallic and faintly sweet, like a refinery that also made candles.

* * *

The Pykes were tall. He had known that, and it was still a thing to stand in front of: a row of them a head and a half above him, narrow all the way up, their long skulls hooded and their faces hidden behind masks that were half breathing apparatus and half ornament, lensed, dark, with filigree worked into the metal at the temples as if someone had decided that if they must be hidden they would at least be hidden expensively. They did not fidget. Their hands were folded. When the one at the centre stepped forward, the others did not watch him do it; they watched Obi-Wan.

"Master Kenobi." The voice came through the mask flattened and precise, a courteous voice, a voice that had practised courtesy as other people practise the blade. "Lom Pyke. You are welcome on Oba Diah. The Syndicate regrets the circumstances of your visit and is honoured by the person of it. You will take some refreshment before we begin."

It was not a question and Obi-Wan did not treat it as one. He bowed, the exact depth that a Jedi gives a head of state he does not recognise as one, and said, "You are kind. I would rather see the survey team first, and take refreshment with them, if the Syndicate can spare it."

A pause. Very slight. The lenses did not change.

"Of course," said Lom Pyke. "They are comfortable. You will see that they are comfortable. This way."

The Pykes. Of course it would be the Pykes.

He did not let the thought go any further than that, because there was nowhere further for it to go that he could afford to visit, and because the courtesy was too perfect. That was the thing his body had been waiting to tell him. He had been received in a great many places by a great many people who did not want him there, and there was a texture to that, a slight roughness in the welcome where the resentment showed through, and here there was none. The welcome was smooth all the way down. It had been rehearsed by someone who had been told what a Jedi expected and had decided to give him exactly that, and the only people who did that were people who already knew how the afternoon would end.

He walked beside Lom Pyke into the spire with his hands in his sleeves and his face pleasant and made the only decision there was to make, which was to go on.

* * *

They had put the survey team in a room near the top. He counted the levels in the lift by the way the pressure changed in his ears and made it forty or more; the door opened on a chamber that was round and high and ringed, two storeys up, by a gantry of black metal from which cargo lines hung down into the empty air like roots. There were windows, narrow and tall, and through them the other spires stood in their cloud, and the light that came in was the colour of an old bruise. Down on the floor, which was broad and bare and had drains in it, somebody had set out eleven chairs, a long table, water, bread, fruit in a bowl. It looked like the setting for a meeting that had not been allowed to begin.

Eleven people were in the room. Ten of them were sitting, in the way of people who have been told to sit, and one was standing, a woman in a surveyor's field jacket with the Republic's seal half torn off the shoulder and iron-coloured hair pulled back hard from a lined face, and she looked at him as he came in and did not look relieved. That was to her credit. He thought he would have liked her under other circumstances.

"Deshan Orr," she said, before he could speak. "You're the Jedi."

"Obi-Wan Kenobi. I am sorry it has taken this long."

"Nineteen days." She said it flat. "Nobody's been hurt. They've fed us. They took our comms and our instruments and Varl's inhaler, and when I asked for the inhaler back they gave it back. I want that on your record. They've been polite." Her eyes went past him to the tall shapes at the door and came back. "I don't know what the polite is for."

"Nor do I, entirely," said Obi-Wan, which was true as far as it went. "Tell me about the ridge."

She told him, in the precise bored voice of a professional who has told the same story to too many people who did not listen. A mineral ridge forty kilometres east, surveyed under a Republic warrant for a consortium on Tyrrhus; a dispute over a boundary drawn forty years ago on a chart that nobody could now produce; a Pyke claim to a seam that ran under the Republic concession and came up on the other side of it; her team taken off the ridge at dawn by very tall people with very good manners and brought here. "It's a land dispute," she said. "That's all it is. I've seen a dozen. Nobody takes eleven hostages over a land dispute."

"No," said Obi-Wan, "they don't," and he put his hand on the back of the nearest chair and looked, for one second, at the gantry over her head, and then he turned to Lom Pyke and said, with every appearance of a man who had just arrived at a reasonable position, "Shall we begin?"

* * *

He had been called many things, and some of them he had deserved, and one of them he had earned in rooms like this one. He had never thought much of it as a gift. It was a craft, like the blade, and he had learned it the way he had learned the blade, from a master who was better at it than he would ever be, by watching and by being thrown on his back, and the whole of it came down to a few things that could not be taught to anyone who was not already listening. You did not argue. You found what the other man wanted and what he was afraid of and you set them side by side on the table and let him look. You said less than he did. You let him win in front of his people, because a man who has been seen to win can afford to give something away. And you never once let him see that you knew the game was rigged, because the moment he knew you knew, the game ended, and the people in the room with you were no longer worth anything to anyone.

So he sat down at the long table across from Lom Pyke, with the eleven behind him and the tall masked guards along the wall, and he began.

He began with the Pykes' grievance, not the Republic's. He had read the forty-year-old boundary question on the way out and he laid it out now from the Syndicate's side, better than Lom Pyke's own people had evidently laid it out to Lom Pyke, with the dates and the chart and the name of the Republic surveyor who had drawn the line and the name of the mining house on Tyrrhus that had paid him, and he watched the lenses go very still as the Pyke understood that the Jedi had come to do his work for him. "Your claim is not frivolous," Obi-Wan said. "I would go so far as to say it is better documented than theirs. Which is why I am puzzled, and I say this with respect, that the Syndicate has chosen to make its case in a form that no tribunal in the Republic can hear."

"A tribunal." Lom Pyke let the word sit. "The Republic has no tribunal for the Rim. The Republic has a Senate, and the Senate has a great many friends on Tyrrhus."

"It has fewer than it had last year," said Obi-Wan. "There is a bill. Three senators, two of them young and one of them stubborn, and it has survived its second reading, which no one expected. Outer Rim franchise disclosure, and a tribunal with standing to hear exactly this. If it passes, and I think it will, the first cases it hears will set the manner of all the others. The Syndicate could be one of those cases. It could be seen to be." He paused. "Or it could be the reason the bill's opponents point to when they say the Rim cannot be trusted with a tribunal. I confess that if I were the mining house on Tyrrhus, I would want very much for you to keep these people another month."

It was a good stroke and he saw it land, not in the mask, which gave nothing, but in the guards along the wall, two of whom looked at each other. He did not follow it up. He let the silence do that. He poured himself water from the jug on the table and drank some and set the cup down.

"You propose," said Lom Pyke at last, "that we release the team and trust the Senate."

"I propose that you release the team into my custody, and that I carry the Syndicate's claim to Coruscant under my own seal and put it before the committee in person, and that I say there, which will be true, that the Pykes treated Republic citizens with a courtesy the Republic has not always shown the Pykes. You lose nothing you have. You gain a witness nobody can buy." He let a little dryness in. "I am told I am tiresomely difficult to buy."

Somebody behind him, one of the eleven, made a sound that was nearly a laugh and swallowed it.

Lom Pyke inclined his long head, a gesture that in another species would have been a smile and here was something more careful. "You have a reputation, Master Kenobi."

"It precedes me," Obi-Wan agreed. "I have never been able to make it wait."

They went on. It took most of an hour. The Pyke wanted the survey instruments and their data; Obi-Wan gave him the data and kept the instruments, and gave him the data again in a form he could show his own people. The Pyke wanted a written undertaking that the ridge would not be worked until the boundary was heard; Obi-Wan wrote it, on the Syndicate's own flimsi, in his own hand, and signed it with the full form of his name, and the Pyke read it twice. The Pyke wanted the team to leave by the Syndicate's own transport, under escort, as a courtesy. Obi-Wan declined the courtesy so gently that it was a minute before anybody understood he had declined it, and then he offered another in its place: the escort could walk them to the Temple courier, and he would say so in his report, with names.

And all the while his body went on telling him. It was the same message it had been sending since the Council chamber, only louder here, under this lid of a sky, in this round room with its drains in the floor and its cargo lines hanging from the gantry; it was in the way the guards did not shift their weight, and in the fruit in the bowl that nobody had touched in nineteen days, and in the way Lom Pyke gave ground, when he gave it, at exactly the speed a man gives ground when it costs him nothing because the ground is not where the fight will be. There had been something in this room once. He did not know how he knew that, either. It had been here and it had been taken away quite recently, and the room still had the shape of it.

He negotiated beautifully. He knew it while he did it, as a man knows when the blade is moving well, and he took no pleasure in it whatsoever, because he was negotiating for the floor of a trap and the only question left was whether he could get eleven people across it before it closed.

It was while he was writing the second undertaking, the one about the escort, that he looked up at the gantry.

* * *

A man was standing on it.

He had not been there when they came in. He had not come by the lift. He stood at the rail two storeys up with the cargo lines hanging past him and the bruised light from the windows on his shoulders, and he was in armour: Mandalorian, a full suit of it, plates of silver-blue over a dark flight suit, a jetpack on his back with the nozzles cold, twin holsters at the hips and the pistols in them. The helmet was the old shape, the T of the visor black and blank. He had his hands on the rail. He was not doing anything. He was watching the room below with the stillness of a man who has been paid to watch and would not have moved if the spire had caught fire, and just under the left edge of the visor, where the helmet's optics sat, a small lens turned with a soft mechanical patience and a light beside it burned a steady red.

He was recording.

Obi-Wan knew him.

That was the whole of it, and it was impossible, and it went through him like cold water. He knew the armour. He knew the way the man held his shoulders, slightly forward, as if he were always about to step off something. He knew the helmet, not as a type but as an object, the way one knows a face, with a scratch along the left cheek-plate that he could not see from here and was sure of. He had never in his life stood in a room with this man; he would have sworn to it before the Council with his hand on his own saber. And a name came up in him, whole, two short syllables and then one, and he did not think it. He put it down. He put it down the way he had learned to put things down, under the calm, in the place where the case under his bunk was, and the queries he had made in the Archives two years ago, and the Tano child's eyes across a dojo; and he finished writing the undertaking and signed it and slid it across the table, and he said to Lom Pyke, "You have a guest."

"An observer," said Lom Pyke. "A courtesy to a client. He will not interfere."

"No," said Obi-Wan. "I don't suppose he will."

He stood up. It was the first unplanned thing he had done since the ramp. He stood and turned his back on the Pyke, which was a breach, and looked at Deshan Orr, who had sat at the end of the table for an hour with her hands flat in front of her and her eyes on his face, and he said, quietly and clearly, "When I say, you will take them to the lift. Not before. Not the door. The lift."

She did not ask what he meant. She said, "Yes."

He turned round again. And it was in the turning, with his back to the eleven and his face to the Pyke, that he heard the doors on the far side of the round room begin to open: not the lift, which was behind him, but the wide doors the cargo lines had been for, the doors he had seen when he came in and placed in his mind and hoped very much not to think about again. They slid apart on the sound of a motor that had wanted oil for some time. Beyond them, in a double rank, with their long heads bowed forward on their thin necks and their rifles held across their narrow bodies, stood battle droids.

Trade Federation stock. He saw it in the same instant he saw them, the tan plating and the backpack transmitters and, on the nearest, the stencilled hull-code of a Federation landing craft that nobody had troubled to scrub off; and he noted it, with the part of him that was always noting, as a thing to be thought about later: that somebody had sold the Pykes an army, and the Pykes did not buy armies. The Pykes bought people. Somebody had given them this.

"Roger roger," said the first droid, in the flat bright voice he had last heard a long time ago and, it seemed to him, in another life, and raised its rifle.

"Now," said Obi-Wan, and his blade was in his hand.

* * *

Soresu is not a form for winning.

Qui-Gon had told him that, when he first turned to it, with the mild contempt of a man who fought to end things. It is a form for staying alive, his master had said, and for keeping other people alive, and it will never give you anything but that; and Obi-Wan had said, Then it will give me everything I need, and had been, he thought, in all the years since, more right than he knew.

He did not go to meet the droids. He went to the place between the droids and the lift and stopped there, and the first volley came in across the width of the round room and he took it.

The blade moved. That was all he could have said about it afterwards; the blade moved and he stood behind it and let it. The form was a circle, close to the body, economical, a wall built one stroke at a time out of nothing but the next stroke, and he put the wall between the eleven and the fire and held it. Bolts came in red and went out red, into the ceiling, into the floor, into the droids themselves, which went down with the small clatter they made, and more stepped forward over them. The Pyke guards along the wall had unfolded their hands and there were rifles in them. He had known there would be. They fired from the flank, from two sides, high, and he turned the circle to take that too, and behind him he heard Orr's voice, hard and flat and wonderfully loud, counting her people into the lift by name.

He held.

The thing about Soresu that Qui-Gon had never liked was that it had no end built into it. Other forms were a sentence with a full stop; this one was a sentence that went on, and the only way it finished was when the other side ran out of things to say. The droids did not run out. They came through the cargo doors in their double rank and he cut them down at the edge of the circle and they kept coming, and the Pykes kept firing from the walls, patient, unhurried, not trying very hard, and he understood (as he had understood everything today, late, and with his body before his mind) that they were not trying to kill him. They were trying to keep him here. The eleven were in the lift now, nearly all of them. He heard the doors try to close and heard someone hold them, and he heard Orr say a name, Varl, and say it again, and he moved, four steps back and to the left, to put the blade between the lift doors and the one droid that had found an angle on them, and that was the moment the circle opened.

He felt the gap before he saw it. His left side, low, the one place the blade was not, because the blade was where the lift was. A Pyke on the wall fired into it.

The bolt went through him under the ribs. He felt it as heat first, not pain, a heat so complete it was almost clean, and then the pain came in behind it and took his breath and his footing in the same instant, and he went to one knee on the bare floor with his blade up and found that he could not feel his left hand.

The lift doors closed. He heard them. Eleven; he had counted them in by Orr's voice and he had the number. That was done. That was the thing that was done.

He got up. He did not know afterwards how. There was blood on the floor between the drains, dark on the black, and the droids were coming on, and he went backwards, up, because up was where the stair to the gantry was, and the gantry ran round the room above the fire, and from the gantry a man could get to the windows, and the windows were narrow and tall and gave on to the cloud and the dark. He was thinking very clearly. He had never thought more clearly. He went up the stair with his left hand pressed to his side and his right hand holding the blade, and the blade still moved, slower, taking what came, and he came out on to the black metal of the gantry with the cargo lines hanging past him and found, with no surprise at all, the man in the silver-blue armour standing at the rail ten metres along, watching him, his hands at his sides, the small red light under his visor burning steadily.

He did not fight. He had not been paid to. Obi-Wan looked at him across the ten metres, with the fire coming up from below and the shouting of Pykes and the clatter of droids, and there was nothing to say to him that would not have been a confession, and so he said nothing.

The helmet did not move.

A bolt took him in the shoulder from below. He felt the gantry rail against the small of his back. He felt the blade go out, not switched off but gone, out of fingers that had stopped answering, and he heard it fall and strike the floor of the round room far down with a small bright sound. And then the rail was not against his back any more, because he had gone over it, and he was in the air with the cargo lines going past him, and the floor was coming up.

It came up slowly. He had time.

He had time to think of a desert, of all things, a desert he had stood at the edge of once for three days and knew in every grain, and a farm at the edge of it, white domes sunk in the sand, and a ridge above the farm where a man could sit with the wind in his beard and watch a boy walk out to the evaporators at dusk, and never once go down. He had time to see the boy's face turn up. He had time to know he had been wrong about something, wrong for a long time, wrong about the only thing that mattered, and that it was too late, as it had always been too late, to say so to anyone who could hear it.

He said it anyway. He said it out loud, with the floor coming up and the dark coming in at the edges, to a boy who was not in the room, who was in a hangar on Coruscant with his hands empty at his sides, who was fifteen years old and had never in this life been given a reason to be told.

"I'm sorry, Anakin. For all of it."

Then the floor.

Then nothing.

And in the nothing, where there should have been nothing at all, there was the bond. It was thin. It had been thin for months, a thread where there had been a rope, a door held one hand's width ajar by someone who would not say why; it had been so thin he had stopped expecting anything to come down it but the faint weather of a boy's moods. It was there now, in the dark, a single filament running out from him towards somewhere he could not see. And someone at the other end of it had taken hold.

They were pulling. They were pulling on it as if they could haul him up it hand over hand, as if it were a line and he were a man gone over a rail, and they were pulling hard enough to hurt.

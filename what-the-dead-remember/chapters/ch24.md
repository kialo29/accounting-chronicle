## Chapter 24: Ben

The word was still in the cabin when Obi-Wan came up.

He came up slowly, the way a man comes up out of bacta: the body first, reporting from a long way off that it was cold, that it was lying on a metal deck, that there was a weight on its ribs and a wrongness under the weight where something had gone in and something had gone out; then the eyes, which had been open for some time without him; then the hearing, which brought him the drone of a courier's drive in hyperspace and a boy's breathing and nothing else. And then the word, which had not gone anywhere. It had been said, in a man's voice, a low worn voice that did not belong in that throat, and it had stayed where it was said, about a metre above his face, and he lay under it and let it do what it did.

No one alive knew that name.

He had been so careful. That was the thing he found himself thinking, with a Pyke's blaster bolt through his side and his Padawan's hand flat on the dressing: *I was so careful.* Four years. He had never once let it into the bond. He had never written it, not even in the second column of the night-pad, the one for things not written. He had chosen it himself, on a ridge, in a wind, with a dead man's child a kilometre below him walking out to a vaporator with a hydrospanner too big for his hands, and he had kept it the way a man keeps the one thing he has left, and he had taken it with him into a corridor on a battle station and let it go there with everything else. Ben. The name was a hut with a door that stuck. It was a bantha-wool blanket and a kettle and the Jundland Wastes going blue at evening. It was nineteen years.

*The hut. Sand in the seams of everything. A Tusken's cry across the ridges at night, and the krayt-dragon roar he had learnt to make to send the scavengers off. The ridge above the Lars farm, a shelf of rock with a view of the domes and the vaporators, and the boy, five, seven, ten, fifteen, walking out at dawn with Owen's hat on and Owen's walk, Shmi's eyes, Anakin's hands. Owen, who had met him at the edge of the farm with a blaster rifle and told him to keep away, and had been right to. The Inquisitors, in their black and their masks, asking at Anchorhead and Mos Eisley for a man with a beard; and he had let the beard go grey and his name go to Ben and the saber stay in a chest under the floor. The girl, come out of a droid in blue light with her mother's mouth and her father's spine: Help me, Obi-Wan Kenobi. Nine years before that, a dark world, a thing in black with its helmet cracked across one eye and the eye in the crack his Padawan's eye, and the voice saying what it said, and himself saying what he said back, and leaving. The corridor. The red blade and the black mask and the breathing like a bellows in a forge. His own blade coming up to guard, and the boy at the far end of the hangar by the ship, and the choice, which was not a choice: the blade raised and held still and let fall. The heat across the throat. The light.*

*And then waking. A bed. A ceiling. The Temple's hum. A body with no ache in it, hands with no spots on them, a beard that came off brown on his fingers when he put his hand to it. Twenty-seven. And through the wall, in the second small room, asleep, eleven years old and sprawled across the cot like a thing dropped, with the thin braid across his face and his mouth open, the boy. His boy. Still his.*

He had got up that first morning and made tea, because he could not think of anything else to do with his hands, and when the boy had come out rubbing his eyes and said *Master, you're up early*, he had said *Yes* and poured him a cup, and he had not said anything else for four years.

Four years. The lists. The letters to a woman on Tatooine, carried by a courier, argued for in front of a Council that had looked at him as though he had suggested the boy keep a pet. The Chancellor: more than a hundred audiences, as many afternoons in Monument Plaza, as many times watching the boy come out of that red room with his face a little smoother than it went in, and never once a way to stop it that did not announce what he knew. The Archives at night, and Kamino, which was not there. The night-pad with its two columns. The list of the dead, which he kept in a place the boy would never look, and which had forty-one names on it now and the places, and which began with a Temple and ended with a corridor.

And none of that was what the word meant. He lay under it and understood that. The word did not mean that someone had found him out. The word meant that someone else had been there.

He looked up.

The boy was looking down. Fifteen. Sandy hair cut short, the braid behind the right ear, no scar, a face that had not finished yet. A boy's hand, narrow, two flesh hands, one of them flat on the dressing over Obi-Wan's ribs with the heel pressed in. The eyes were not a boy's. He had known that for five months. He had known it the night the bond went opaque, like a door closing, and had not let himself think the word, and had thought it anyway every day since in the place under thinking where he kept the other things. He thought it now.

He had not used his own voice in four years. He had used Master Kenobi's, which was dry and courteous and said *my friend*; he had built it the way he had built everything, carefully, out of what he remembered. It did not come. What came was older and quieter and had lived in a hut.

"Where did you hear it?"

The boy did not pretend not to understand him. That was the first mercy, and Obi-Wan would think later that it was the one that had undone him: that there was no pause for the lie. The hand on the dressing did not move. The boy's face did something that a boy's face is not built to do, a thing he had seen it do once across a bank of lava and never since.

"From my son," Anakin said.

* * *

The drive hummed. The blue went by the ports.

Obi-Wan found that he had closed his eyes, and opened them.

My son. There was only one person in the galaxy that could mean, and he had watched that person walk out to a vaporator at dawn for nineteen years, and the person had called him Ben, and had not known there was anything else to call him; had sat in the hut with the saber in his hands and said *My father didn't fight in the wars*, and he, Obi-Wan, had said what he said and had been ashamed of it for the rest of his life, which was three days. Luke. Luke had said the name, and the thing with the cracked helmet had heard it, and the thing with the cracked helmet was kneeling on the deck of a Temple courier with its hand on his ribs, and had a boy's hands, and had said *my son* as though it were allowed.

"Luke," he said.

"Yes."

"He said my name to you."

"He said it on Bespin." The boy's voice went somewhere and came back. "He was hanging from the underside of the city, and he said it. I heard it through the Force. I'd taken his hand off a minute before."

Obi-Wan tried to sit up.

He got as far as the elbow. The dressing pulled and the side went white and the cabin tipped, and the boy's other hand came round the back of his shoulders and held him there, not up, not down, just there, the way you hold a thing that has decided to fall and is still deciding; and he hung in it with the sweat coming out on his forehead and his breath going short, and he said, through it, "Say that again."

"I'll say all of it," Anakin said. "You have to lie down."

"I will not lie down."

"Then you'll fall down. There's a bulkhead. I'm going to put you against it. Don't help."

He was put against it. It took the boy some time, because the boy was slight and Obi-Wan was not, and because Obi-Wan helped. When it was done he was sitting with his back to the aft bulkhead and his legs out along the deck and his left arm in a sling made of a seat harness, and the boy was crouched in front of him with his elbows on his knees looking at the dressing and not at his face, and Obi-Wan could see, from this angle, the yoke, and the navcomp, and the blue, and the whole small cabin that there were only two of them in.

"How long," he said.

"Eleven hours. Ten now. The safe lane, not mine; I'm not racing a hole in you."

"And you're going to tell me."

"I'm going to fly. I've set it, but she's old and she wanders, and I'm not going to drop you out in the middle of a lane." Anakin got up. He was a head shorter than the bulkhead door. He stood in it a moment. "And I'm going to tell you. You've been asking for five months. You just never used the words."

"No," said Obi-Wan. "Neither did you."

* * *

He flew it from the pilot's seat with his back to Obi-Wan, and he could not keep his back to him; that was what Obi-Wan saw first and went on seeing, as the thing was told. The boy would say a sentence to the forward port and then turn round in the seat, the whole narrow body coming round with one arm over the back of it, to see what the sentence had done; and then turn back and fly; and then turn round again. A man in his forties turning in a pilot's seat with a fifteen-year-old's lack of economy. Obi-Wan had watched him do it in a speeder at twelve. He had never thought he would see it again and be glad.

"You know what I was."

"I know what you were."

"Say it. I want to know you can."

"Vader," Obi-Wan said. His side hurt. "Darth Vader. The Emperor's — " He stopped. "I was going to say *hand*. I don't know that I ever found a word for it. I had nineteen years and I never found the word."

"There isn't one." The boy turned back to the port. "Twenty-three years. I know you know most of it. The Temple. The Purge. The Inquisitors; I ran them. Kanan — Caleb; I don't know if you knew him, he was seven when — no. Not now. I'll tell you what you don't know, and you'll tell me, and we'll see where we come out."

"Agreed."

"You were on the ridge."

"I was on the ridge."

"Then you know about the Death Star, because you died on it." The boy's hands were steady on the yoke. "You don't know he blew it up. Within the day. He flew a snubfighter down a trench with me behind him, and I had him dead in the sights, and something hit my wing and I spun out, and he put a torpedo down a port the width of this cabin and the thing went up with a million people on it, and I thought: *the Force is strong in this one*, and I didn't know whose he was. I hadn't thought about Tatooine in nineteen years. I'd had it burnt out of me." He turned round. "I went back there, after. I found the hut, the chest under the floor, the blanket. I found the name on a water-ration docket at Anchorhead: *Ben Kenobi*. I stood in your house."

Obi-Wan said nothing. The hut had had a stuck door. He could not for his life remember whether he had closed it.

"I found out what he was three years later, from the Emperor, who'd had it from the records of a birth at Polis Massa. He let me find it in him and watched me find it. Then I went and got the boy." He turned back. "Bespin," he said, and went no further: it was a thing he would not describe, and Obi-Wan did not ask him to.

Obi-Wan found that he had put his good hand over his mouth. He took it away.

"Go on."

"A year. Then the second station, over Endor. He came to me. Walked into my hands on a forest moon and let me take him up to the Emperor, because he thought there was good in me, because he'd got it into his head from somewhere." The boy's voice was quite flat. "Sidious put us in a room and made us fight. He wanted the boy to kill me and take the suit. He nearly had it. Luke took my hand off, the right one, the one that was already a machine, and stood over me with the blade up, and looked at it. At the hand. At the wires. And he threw the saber away and said he was a Jedi, like his father before him." Turn, port, turn. "So Sidious did what he did to people who disappointed him. Lightning. I'd seen it. I'd felt it, once or twice, when he was teaching. He put it into the boy and the boy went down and screamed and he kept putting it in, and I stood there. I stood there, Obi-Wan. I stood and watched him burn my son and I did the sums. How long it would take. Whether I could still use the arm. I stood and did sums."

"And then."

"And then I picked him up. Sidious. I picked him up by the middle like a sack of grain, and the lightning went into the suit, into the whole of the suit, and I walked him to the edge of the shaft and put him over. He screamed all the way down. I didn't know a thing could scream that long."

The courier wandered. The boy corrected it with one hand without looking.

"The suit was gone. The lightning had done it. I had about ten minutes, I thought; it was less. Luke got me as far as a landing ramp. There was a ship burning out over the moon, the whole fleet was coming apart, and I asked him to take the mask off." He said this to the port. "He said I'd die. I said nothing could stop that now. And he took it off. He had to undo the clamps at the back; I couldn't lift my arms. And then it was off and there was air on my face, real air, for the first time in twenty-three years, and it hurt, it hurt like — and I looked at him. With my eyes. And he was — " The boy stopped. He stopped flying. The yoke sat in his hands and he did not correct the wander. "He looked like her. I'd never seen it. I'd fought him twice and I'd never seen him. I said some things. I told him to tell his sister something."

Obi-Wan thought he could guess what he had said. He did not know how he could guess. He had heard the boy say something like it once, to a dead man's image in a dark room, when he had thought no one was listening, and he had not let himself know what he had heard; it came back now and sat down beside the word *Ben* and would not go.

"You told him he was right," Obi-Wan said. "About his father. That there was something in you to be right about."

"I'm not going to say it again. I said it once. Once was enough." The boy's shoulders went down. "He said I'd be with him. I was. And then I wasn't. There was a light, and it was — it wasn't bad. I'd thought it would be bad." He turned round again, all the way, and looked at Obi-Wan with those eyes in that face, and said, in the boy's own register, as if it had slipped out of him: "I thought you'd be there. In it. I thought you'd be there and you weren't, and then I woke up in my bed with my own hands, and you were in the next room making tea."

* * *

It was Obi-Wan's turn.

He had thought, when the word came, that he would find it hard. He found that he had been telling it for four years to no one, in the second column, in the dark, and that it was all there and in order and only wanted a listener, and that the listener had to be the one person who had the right not to hear it.

"You know I was on the ridge," he said. "You don't know what it was."

"Tell me what it was."

"It was nineteen years." His side ached. He found a way to sit that let him breathe. "I took him to Owen and Beru when he was three days old and Owen took him from my hands and told me to go. I went. I found a hut in the Wastes that a hermit had died in, and I buried the hermit, and I kept his name off the door and put mine on it, the one you heard. I had nothing to do, Anakin. Do you understand that? I had been Obi-Wan Kenobi, High Council, General, I had held a system in one hand and a treaty in the other, and I had nothing to do. So I went up the ridge every morning. There was a shelf of rock. You could see the domes and the vaporators and the sand-crawler road. And I watched your son learn to walk."

The boy had turned round. He did not turn back.

"I watched him learn to walk and to fall and to stand at a vaporator with a hydrospanner in both hands because one was not enough. I watched Owen teach him to drive the landspeeder. I watched him fight with Owen in the yard at nine and at twelve and at fifteen, the same fight, you and Qui-Gon's fight, *I want to go and you cannot go*. I watched Beru put a loaf on a rock at the edge of the farm once a tenday and never once look up at where I was. Nineteen years she did that. I never thanked her. There was no way to thank her that did not put the boy at risk, and I had decided, you see, that nothing was worth that."

"The Inquisitors came."

"Twice. Once early on, and once in the tenth year, the hunt that came nearest to finding me. They asked in Mos Eisley and in Anchorhead for a man with a beard, and I let the beard go white and I let my name go to Ben, and I put the saber in a chest under the floor and did not take it out for a decade. I bought water from a Jawa and I fixed vaporators for farmers who did not like me and I made a noise at night that Tuskens were afraid of. That is what your master did with his late thirties, his forties, his fifties, Anakin, the second time round. He hid from you."

"I know. I ran them. I'd have — " The boy stopped. "I'd have killed you. If they'd found you. I'd have come myself."

"Yes." Obi-Wan looked at him. "That is what I believed you were. I want to tell you that now, so that we do not have to come back to it. I believed that the thing I left on that bank had got up, and that nothing of you was in it. I had evidence. I had Padmé's face in the medcentre. I had a Temple with the children in it. I had a dark world, ten years in, where I went to get Leia out of a cell, and the thing in black was waiting for me, and its helmet had a crack across the eye, and the eye in the crack was yours. I said your name to it. I said, *Anakin*, and it said, *Anakin is gone. I am what remains.* And I said, *Then my friend is truly dead. Goodbye, Darth.* And I went. I took her and I went and I did not look back, because I had looked back once and it had cost me what it cost me." He drew a breath that hurt. "And then nine years later I walked into a hangar on a moon-sized gun and you were at the end of the corridor, and I put the blade up, and I thought, *the boy is at the ship, he is nearly aboard*, and I let it fall. I let you have it. Not because I had forgiven you. Because the boy was nearly aboard, and the one thing I had learnt in nineteen years was what I would spend to buy him three seconds."

The boy's hands had left the yoke. They were in his lap, two flesh hands, turned up.

"You said something. Before. When the blade came down. I've thought about it for — I've thought about it."

"I said what I believed. I believed it." Obi-Wan held his eye. "I was wrong. You have just told me I was wrong. I am not going to pretend that is easy."

"No."

"Good. Then we understand each other."

The blue went by. The courier drifted and the boy did not correct it, and after a moment Obi-Wan said, "The ship," and the boy turned and put her back on the line and said, over his shoulder, "The four years."

"Yes."

"Why you didn't tell anyone. I've had it from the other side, you know. I've had five months of watching you not tell anyone."

"I watched the Council fail," Obi-Wan said. "That is the whole of it. I watched them fail at Naboo and at Geonosis and at Kamino and at the end, and I watched them fail you most particularly, because they never once in thirteen years decided to trust you, and you knew it, and a man who is not trusted goes where he is. I woke up with four years' lead and a boy of eleven in the next room, and I asked myself what a Council that knew would do with that boy, and the answer was: watch him. Like a bomb. And a watched boy becomes the bomb. So I told no one. I told Yoda nothing. I told Qui-Gon, if he was listening, and he was not. I decided, on the first morning, over the tea, that the only thing I had was you, and that I would raise you differently, and that the differences would have to be small, because large ones would be noticed."

"The letters."

"The letters. You were nine and you wrote to your mother every tenday in your head, I could feel you doing it, and the Order's answer was that attachment is forbidden. So I went to the Council and argued that a wound in the open heals, and they let me have it because they thought it a harmless eccentricity in a new master, and your mother wrote back, and you stopped dreaming about her four nights in five. I counted."

"The Chancellor."

"I have never once got you out of that room." Obi-Wan's voice was flat. "More than a hundred times. I stood in Monument Plaza more than a hundred times and could not think of a single reason to give the Council that did not begin with *the Chancellor is a Sith Lord*, and I had no proof, and I will not have any for four years, and in four years it will be a war. So I waited for you in the plaza and bought you a sweet on the way home and watched the thing he had done to your face wear off, and I did that for four years. If you are looking for the worst of it, that was the worst of it."

"The Archives. Kamino."

"A hopeless thing, done because I could not do the other. I searched for a planet that had been cut from the records by the man who cut it, because I thought if I could find the clones, I could — I don't know what I thought. Stop them. Warn them. Buy them." He closed his eyes briefly. "I know they turn. I know they turn on an order and I do not know how an order makes a man do that to a man he has fought beside for three years, and I have thought about it for twenty, and I still do not. I put it down on the list under *not understood*."

"The list," the boy said. He was turned round again. "You have a list."

"I have a list." Obi-Wan did not say where. "Every Jedi who dies and where. Forty-one so far; it is not finished; I write them as they come back to me. It begins with a Temple."

"I know what it begins with."

"I know you do."

"Is my name on it?"

"No," said Obi-Wan, and found that this was true, and that he had never once asked himself why. "No. You're not on it. I don't know why you're not on it."

* * *

Then there was a long time when neither of them said anything, and the courier drove on through the blue, and Obi-Wan sat against the bulkhead with the sling across his chest and listened to his own blood going round, slow and insufficient, and watched a fifteen-year-old fly a ship with his back to him. He had thought there would be more. There was more; there was twenty years of more; but the shape of it had been said, and what was left was the one thing under the shape, the thing they had never said to each other, because one of them had been in a mask and the other on a ridge and there had been no occasion.

He knew the boy was thinking of it, because the boy had stopped turning round.

"Anakin."

"I'm here."

"We have ten hours. I would rather say it now than carry it in."

The boy's hands came off the yoke. He set the course lock. He turned the seat all the way round, so that he was facing aft with his back to the stars, a boy in a pilot's chair with his feet not quite flat on the deck, and he looked at Obi-Wan down the length of the small cabin, and for a moment his face was the face on the bank, the one that had been burning.

"You left me to burn."

The drive hummed. Obi-Wan did not look away. He had not looked away on the bank either; that was a thing he could say for himself, and he had said it, in the night, in the second column, more than once. He had watched. He had watched it take the hair and the face and then he had picked up the saber and gone.

"Yes."

The boy's hands closed. Opened.

"I hated you for twenty years."

It had come across the lava. It had come across the lava in a voice that was already going, and he had heard it with the saber in his hand and Padmé on the ground behind him, and he had carried it up the ridge every morning for nineteen years and set it on the rock beside him and looked at the domes. He did not need the boy to tell him. He told him anyway, because the boy had the right to hear it heard.

"I know. I heard you."

A long silence. The blue went by. Somewhere in the aft bulkhead a coolant line ticked as it cooled, and the sound was very loud.

"I'm sorry," Anakin said.

Obi-Wan had thought, in the hut, on the ridge, in the corridor, that if the boy ever said it he would know what to do with it. He had rehearsed what he would do. He found that he had nothing. There was a thing he had said to him across the river, with the lava going by and the boy's face coming apart, and it was true, and it was the truest thing he had ever said, and he did not say it now; it was not a thing that could be said twice; the boy had heard it. What he had was two words, and he gave them.

"So am I."

That was all. He had thought there would be more. There was not more. The boy sat in the chair and he sat against the bulkhead and nothing in the cabin had changed: the wound was where it was; the Temple had still burnt; the children; Padmé; the ridge; the corridor; Luke hanging under a city calling a name. None of it was mended. He did not know that any of it could be. But the thing had been said, both halves of it, in the right order, with the right silence between, and he felt, sitting there with his side on fire and his Padawan's eyes on him, that something had been set down on a table between them that had been carried, separately, in two directions, for a very long time; and that neither of them was going to pick it up again alone.

The boy turned the chair back to the port.

"There's someone else," he said, after a while, to the stars. "At the Temple. You need to meet them. Not now. When you can stand."

"Someone else," Obi-Wan said, and the Tano child's blue eyes came up in him out of nowhere and he put them down again. "I see. Yes. I rather thought there might be."

"You're not going to ask."

"I have ten hours and one lung, my friend. I am going to ask you everything. I am simply going to do it in order."

The boy laughed. It was a boy's laugh, high and surprised, and it stopped at once, as if he had heard it and not known whose it was.

* * *

Coruscant came up at the end of it the way it always did, a hard white line and then the whole glittering disc, the lanes thick as veins, the Temple's five spires a dark shape against the dawn side; and the courier dropped out of the blue with a lurch that went through Obi-Wan's side like a second bolt, and he made a sound, and the boy was out of the chair and across the cabin and on his knees with his hand on the dressing before the stars had finished becoming stars.

"It's holding."

"I know it is holding. I can feel it holding. Fly the ship."

"She's on approach. Temple beacon. Thane's crew chief will have us down in nine minutes." The boy did not get up. "They'll want to know."

"Yes."

"How I got there. Who sent me. Where the ship came from. Why you've got a Pyke bolt through you on a routine escort and eleven surveyors telling anyone who'll listen about a torn door and the dead on the pad and a boy walking out with a man floating beside him. And a Mandalorian saw all of it, Obi-Wan. He had a helmet on. They record."

"I saw him."

"Then you know where that goes."

"I know where it goes." Obi-Wan looked past the boy's shoulder at the Temple coming up in the port, the Processional Way, the gate, the thousand windows with the children behind them, and he thought of the Council in its circle, and of Plo Koon's long mask, and of Master Yoda who said little and the right thing, and of all that he had kept from them for four years, and of how much easier it had been to keep it when he had been the only one keeping it.

"We will tell them the truth," he said, "as far as it goes. The Pykes held the surveyors. The Mandalorian was theirs. You came after me because I opened a private channel and summoned you when the negotiation turned, within two hours of my landing, and if the Council asks why I did not call them instead we shall have to see what can be done about his memory. The droids were old. I was lucky. You were a boy who did a brave and foolish thing, and I will be properly cross about it in front of them." He shifted, and the side bit, and he let it. "Everything else, we will lie about. The name. The fight. What you are. What I am. The son. Each of us has been lying to that Council alone for years, Anakin, and done it rather well, and now there are two of us, and the lies will have to match."

The boy's hand was still on the dressing. The Temple filled the port. Below them the hangar doors were opening, and there would be a hangar officer at the foot of the ramp with a face like a slapped tally-sheet, and a stretcher, and healers, and questions, and behind the healers, somewhere, small, in a sand-coloured tunic, a child with blue eyes who had sent this boy a ship.

"Then we had better," said Anakin, "agree on what we did with the six hours."

## Chapter 11: The Chancellor Takes Tea

The message came by the ordinary channel and said the ordinary things, and Obi-Wan Kenobi read it twice, standing in the common room with one boot on, because of the single word in it that was not ordinary. The Supreme Chancellor would be grateful for an hour of Master Kenobi's time at the fourth hour, in his office, on a matter of no urgency. He would be grateful if Master Kenobi came alone.

Anakin was already gone. He went to Drallig's hall before first bell now, every morning, to show the battlemaster a stroke that the battlemaster had not taught him, and he came back with his tunic dark at the collar and said nothing about it, and Obi-Wan asked nothing, which was the arrangement they had arrived at without ever discussing it. The teapot on the table was warm. The boy had made it and left it for him. Obi-Wan put his hand flat on the side of it and stood for a moment like that, and then he put his other boot on.

He walked. It was the day before the *Crucible* left and the Temple was full of the particular noise of children being made ready for something: lists read out in corridors, a Rodian girl crying in a stairwell because her boots had been taken away to be re-soled, Master Sen's voice saying *Hawkbat, with me* in the tone of a woman who had said it ten thousand times. Six of them were going. He had read the list the way he read everything that crossed the Council's antechamber, quickly and entirely, and he could have recited it, and he did not, because the sixth name on it was one he found he did not like to say even inside his own head. Anakin was to go as escort. Master Yoda had asked for him by name. Obi-Wan had been in the room when he did it and had said *Of course* with his whole face.

The Senate District at the fourth hour was all glass and the kind of light that showed every mark on a robe. He crossed Monument Plaza by the long diagonal, past the bench where he had sat every tenth day for six years waiting for a boy to come down the steps, and did not look at it.

* * *

The outer office was cool and grey and contained, as it always did, the Umbaran woman, who looked up at him with her pale eyes and said nothing, and two Senate Guards in blue who did not look up at all. The inner door opened without anyone touching it.

"Master Kenobi. How very good of you."

The Chancellor came round the desk to meet him. He was a man of middle height in dark, excellent cloth, with a face that had been kindly for so long it had set that way, and he took Obi-Wan's hand in both of his and held it a moment longer than a handshake needed. The office was red. The window behind the desk was the width of the room, and Coruscant lay in it from edge to edge, with the Temple a small brown shape far off to the left like something left on a shelf.

"Chancellor."

"Sit, sit. I have had them bring tea. I know the Order's position on most pleasures but I have never known a Jedi refuse tea, and I have known a good many Jedi." He poured it himself, from a pot that was too fine for an office, into cups that were finer.
Obi-Wan sat. The chair was good and had been placed, he noticed, a little nearer the desk than a visitor's chair usually was, so that the man behind the desk did not have to lean to speak softly. The cup was put in front of him. Steam came off it. He looked at it and found that his hand was in his lap and intended to stay there.

"You'll forgive the word *alone*," the Chancellor said, settling. "I did not want you to think the boy was in any trouble. He is not. I simply find that when a master and a Padawan sit before me together, I learn a great deal about the master and nothing about the boy, and when the boy sits alone, he tells me what he thinks I want to hear, and when the master sits alone"—a smile—"I may at last ask a plain question and have a plain answer. I hope."

"I will do my best, Chancellor."

"I am sure you will." He drank. "He seemed withdrawn, when I saw him last. I say *seemed*; I am an old man who reads faces for a living and I may read too much into a boy's. But he was quieter than I have known him. In six years I have not known him quiet. He has always been so open with me."

"He is fifteen," Obi-Wan said. "Boys that age go in and out like the tide. One learns not to set a clock by them."

"Just so. Just so. And he had a bad night, I understand. The night before the last audience. Or was it the night after?" The Chancellor frowned at his own memory and let the frown go. "The Temple's healers were kept busy, I'm told. Thirty-one children with one dream between them. It must have been very distressing for the little ones."

Obi-Wan let his face do what a Jedi Master's face did when a politician repeated Temple gossip, which was nothing at all, and said, "You are remarkably well informed about our nurseries, Chancellor."

"I hear things." The Chancellor spread a hand, deprecating. "It is most of what the office is, Master Kenobi. People bring me things to see what my face does. It is a sieve, your Temple, like every great house." A pause, perfectly placed. "I only wondered whether the boy had been among the ones who dreamed."

"The healers have been thorough."

"I'm sure." The old face was all concern. "And then there was the business with the senior Padawan. Bellas, is it? A bout that ended with a blade at a throat. I hear it with sympathy, I assure you. I was a boy once. I was called things. One does not always choose what the hand does."

"A training saber," Obi-Wan said. "A boy's temper, and a provocation I would not have tolerated from a Padawan of mine. He has been set a penance."

"By Battlemaster Drallig. Yes." The Chancellor turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, and looked at it, and then up. "Forgive me. But a boy who can put a blade a finger's width from an older boy's throat and stop it there, at fifteen—I confess my first thought was not *penance*. My first thought was, is he being given enough to do?"

"He is given a great deal to do."

"Classes. Yes." He leant forward, and the chair that had been set a little too near the desk became exactly the right distance. "Master Kenobi, I will come to it. In some weeks the Naboo delegation will mark the anniversary of the battle. There will be a reception; the Queen sends people, the Senator attends, I attend. The boy was at that battle. He was nine years old and he was there, and he did a thing that is still spoken of on my homeworld in tones I will not embarrass you by repeating. It would please the Naboo to see him. It would please me a great deal. I should like him to accompany me. For the evening, nothing more. I would see him home myself."

The tea had stopped steaming. Obi-Wan looked at the man across the desk and felt, as he always felt in this room, the thing he did not have a name for and would not look for one: a cold that was not in the air, a weight on the back of the neck like a hand that had not yet been laid there. He had sat in this office perhaps forty times. He had never once picked up the cup.

"He'll be on Ilum," he said.

"Ah."

"The Gathering leaves tomorrow. Master Yoda has asked for him as escort. It is a considerable honour for a Padawan, and the first time it has been asked of him, and I don't think I could, in conscience, take him off the *Crucible* for a reception."

"Nor would I ask it. The Gathering takes, what, a week?"

"The crossing, the caves, and then the children build their sabers, which is not a thing that can be hurried, and then the crossing home. And Master Yoda," Obi-Wan said, "is not a man one hurries either."

"No." The Chancellor smiled. It was a very good smile; it reached the eyes and stayed there. "I said *some weeks*, Master Kenobi. I did not say next week. I have a Senate to keep in order and the Naboo are not people who hold a reception without a month of argument about the flowers. He will be home from Ilum, and rested, and—I should think—rather pleased with himself. And I should like to put him in a room with people who will tell him so."

"I'll put it to the Council."

"Of course." The smile did not change. The space after the word did. "The Council."

It was a small silence and it lasted exactly as long as it needed to. In it he could hear the air system and, far off, the speeder lanes, and under both the Chancellor deciding not to say something, and then deciding to say something else.

"You have never once let me have him for a whole day," the Chancellor said, quite gently. "In six years. You know, I think, that I have noticed."

"I'm sure you have, Chancellor."

"I find I rather admire it." He sat back. "You have refused me forty times with the most beautiful manners I have ever seen, and I have never once been able to be angry about it, and I have tried. I only wonder, as I wondered about the penance, whether it is the right thing for *him*. A boy that gifted. A boy that—" he searched for the word, and found it, and laid it on the desk between them like a card—"*alone*."

"He is not alone."

"No. He has you." The Chancellor looked at the untouched cup and then at the man. "Your tea, Master Kenobi. It will be quite cold."

"I'm afraid I've had rather a lot already this morning."

"Then I shall not press you." He rose; the hour was done, to the minute, and he had not once looked at the chrono. He came round the desk again and laid his hand on Obi-Wan's shoulder, lightly, the way a man does who is used to his hand being welcome. "Give him my regards. Tell him the Chancellor was asking after him. It pleases him to be asked after; he has told me so. And come and see me when you are back from—no. You are not going. I keep forgetting. You are staying behind."

"I am staying behind."

"Then come and see me while he is gone," the Chancellor said, "and we shall worry about him together."

* * *

In the plaza the light had moved round and the bench was in shadow. He did not look back at the window. He was quite sure that the man was standing at it, a dark shape against red, watching a Jedi cross a square with his hands in his sleeves; he had never seen the Chancellor at that window after an audience, not once, and he was quite sure, and he did not ask himself how, because the question had a bottom to it and he did not want to see what was lying there.

His hand was not steady. He put it in his sleeve, where it was nobody's business, and took the long way back.

* * *

The Archives in the early afternoon were a church. The light came down through the high lamps onto the long blue aisles and the busts of the dead, and a dozen initiates sat at terminals with the stillness of children who had been told that this was the one room in the Temple in which they would be killed for running. Obi-Wan found Madame Nu where she was always found, which was wherever one happened to be looking for her.

"Master Kenobi."

"Madame Nu. I'm not interrupting."

"You are, but it is a thing I have been meaning to put down." She set the datapad on a lectern and folded her hands over it. She was a small, upright woman with white hair pulled back hard and a face that had looked at eight hundred years of catalogue and found most of it wanting. "What do you need?"

"Nothing very much. A question about the logs." He said it lightly, in the voice one used for a thing one had been meaning to ask for weeks and had kept forgetting. "I wondered who had been reading them lately. The access logs. The record of who asks the Archive for what. I had a reason to think they'd been looked at."

Jocasta Nu regarded him for a moment with an expression he could not quite read.

"You are the second Kenobi to come to me about the Archive's records in a tenday," she said.

"The second."

"I'm aware he is a Skywalker. He is a Kenobi in every way that matters to a librarian. He asks for a thing by asking for the thing next to it." She unfolded her hands and folded them the other way. "Your Padawan came to me yesterday and asked, very politely, for exactly one thing: his master's Soresu training-access record. It was a proper request and I gave it to him. And I told him, which he had not asked, that the general query log was not for Padawans. Initiates and Padawans do not read who-read-what; they would do nothing else. I cannot tell you why I said it. He thanked me and went away."

"Did he."

"That same morning the general query log was opened from terminal nine, for most of an hour, under a maintenance account belonging to a librarian who has been dead nine hundred years." She let that stand. "The Archive is old, Master Kenobi, and it was built by people who assumed everyone in it would be honest, which is a charming assumption and a poor one. But it traces. It is slow, and it is ugly, and it took less than a day, and it points at terminal nine, and at the hour, and at the only person in this building I had told that log was closed. He found a way. I imagine he would say he was told no, and not told *how* no."

Obi-Wan said, "I'm sorry."

"Don't be. I have logged it. Reporting is for people with more time than I have, and I have not yet decided whether this is a thing for Master Yoda or a thing for you, and so I am telling you, because you are his master, and because you asked, and because I have a feeling you would rather hear it from me than from Master Windu." A pause. "What was he looking for?"

"I don't know."

"No." She picked the datapad up again. "I should tell you, since you asked so lightly, that the unfilled queries for the last five years are not a long document. Most are initiates misspelling things. The rest, one could read in a minute." She looked at him over the pad, and the look was not unkind. "Including yours, Master Kenobi. The one, at least: Kamino is there, unfilled. The other was filled, and so it is not on that list. He will have read the one. I thought you should know that before you decided what to say to him."

"Thank you, Madame Nu."

"Thank me by teaching him to ask properly. I should hate to have to change the locks on a building this size."

* * *

He walked out through the hall of busts without looking at the one he did not look at, and down the long stair, and found that he had stopped on the landing with his hand on the rail and the children's voices coming up from below like water.

Why would Anakin look for a planet that does not exist?

The sentence arrived whole and he turned it over and could not make it mean one thing. There was no such planet. There was no such entry. Two years ago he had sat at terminal four and typed a name into the Archive and watched it give him back nothing, for forty minutes, the way it gave nothing for a misspelling, and then he had gone away and come back four days later and typed another name, a dead Jedi's, and read what there was. He had never been able to say, even to himself, which of the two sentences he was telling himself when he thought about that afternoon: that the place was not there, or that the place was not *there*. He was not able to say it now. The boy had read the log. The boy had read a name that was nothing, tagged with his master's initials, and the boy had not come to him, and had sat across a supper table instead and said another name, a woman's, and watched his face.

Obi-Wan took his hand off the rail. He did not know what the boy was looking for. He was sure of that. It was the only thing he had said to Madame Nu that had been entirely true, and he found he was holding it the way one held a railing, and he let go of it, and went down.

* * *

CoCo Town at the eighth hour smelt of fry-oil and hot ferrocrete and the faint sweetness that came off the recycling stacks when the wind was wrong. The diner sat on its corner like a thing that had been there before the corner and intended to outlast it, all chrome and red seats and a window that had not been clean in Obi-Wan's lifetime. The droid at the counter swivelled her head at him and shouted into the kitchen before he had reached a stool.

"Obi-Wan!"

Dexter Jettster came out of the back wiping two of his hands on an apron and holding the other two wide, and Obi-Wan allowed himself to be embraced by something the size of a speeder with the smell of a griddle on it. The Besalisk held him off and looked at him and said, "You're thin," which he had said at every meeting for nine years, and steered him to the corner booth and lowered himself into the far side of it with the sound of a building settling.

"Tea," Dex told the droid, without asking. "The real stuff, not the sweepings. And pie. He'll say no. Bring it anyway." He folded his upper arms on the table and his lower ones under it. "So. How's the kid?"

"Growing."

"They do that. Still flying everything he can get his hands on?"

"Everything with a key in it, and several things without."

Dex's small bright eyes went over him in the way they had, the way of a man who had bought and sold things on forty worlds and could price a face. "You didn't come for pie."

"I came for the pie."

"You came for the pie *and*." The tea arrived. Obi-Wan held the cup. It was hot and thick and tasted of the bottom of a pot, and he drank it, and Dex watched him drink it and waited.

"Two years ago," Obi-Wan said, "I asked you about the Kaminoans."

Dex's face did not change, exactly. It arranged itself. "You did."

"I wondered whether anything had changed."

"Nothing's changed." He said it without hesitation and without heat, as a man says a thing he has already checked. "I told you then and I'll tell you now, and it's the same telling. Tall. Pale. Long necks, like something that's been stretched. Eyes like a fish that's been left out. Cloners, and the best there are, if you can find 'em and if you can pay, and most can't do either. They're out past the Rishi Maze, south of everything, so far off the charts the charts don't bother. And nobody—nobody, Obi-Wan—has seen one in this part of the galaxy in decades. I asked around after you asked me. Quiet. People who'd know. Not a whisper." He turned his tea cup with one thick finger. "Same as before. Word for word, near enough."

It was. Obi-Wan had known it would be; he had known the shape of the sentence before Dex got to it, *past the Rishi Maze*, and had felt his own mouth want to make the words a half-beat ahead, and had kept it still. He had not come for the answer. He had the answer. He had come to hear a man who was not a Jedi and not a ghost say the thing out loud in a room with grease on the windows, and to see whether it was still true that nobody was looking, and it was. Nobody was looking. Nobody but a boy with a dead librarian's codes and his master's initials in front of him.

"Thank you, Dex."

"Don't thank me, tell me." The Besalisk leant in, and the booth creaked. "Two years ago you asked, and I told you, and you went. Fine. But you've come back with the same question. Not *what else have you heard*. The same question, like you're checking I've not changed my story." He did not look offended. He looked worried, which was worse. "Why does a Jedi keep asking about a people nobody's seen?"

"A loose thread," Obi-Wan said. "I dislike loose threads. It is a failing in a man of my training; Qui-Gon used to say so."

"Two years is a long time to pull on one."

"I am a patient man."

"You're a lot of things." Dex sat back. "And you came in here looking like a man who's been to a funeral. You want to tell me whose, I'm here. You don't, fine. Eat the pie."

Obi-Wan ate the pie. When he stood to go, Dex took his hand in one of his and his elbow in another, which was as near as a Besalisk came to saying something gently.

"Bring the kid next time. Before he's taller than you."

"That ship," said Obi-Wan, "has sailed."

* * *

The quarters were dark when he came in and there was a light under Anakin's door, and then, as he stood in the common room with his cloak half off, there was not. He waited. He felt for the bond at the back of his mind the way one feels for a tooth with the tongue, and it was where it had been for a tenday: thin, slack, a thread where a rope had been, letting through the faint grey of a boy pretending to sleep or sleeping. He stood with his hand on the back of a chair and did not go to the door. He had sat on the boy's bed once in the night, ten days ago, and had been seen doing it.

The Chancellor was asking after you, he thought at the door. It pleases you to be asked after. You have told him so.

He went into his own room and did not open the drawer. There was a list in the drawer, in a hand he had taught himself so that it would not be his, and he had not added to it in a month, and he did not add to it now. He lay down in his clothes. One day. A ship, and a cold world, and six children with their boots re-soled walking into a cave at sunrise, the sixth of them a girl who held her saber the wrong way round and who, across the Room of a Thousand Fountains, had looked away first, and he had felt, absurdly, that he had lost. And Anakin at Yoda's shoulder. And Obi-Wan here, in a Temple full of people hunting a shadow, with an invitation to take tea.

He slept, eventually, and the dream came, the one that came every few months and never changed.

*A ridge of pale rock, and the sun over it so white there was no colour anywhere. Below, a long way below, a farm: a ring of tall machines standing in the sand like the spars of a wreck, a low dome with a door in it, a pit of a courtyard sunk into the ground against the heat. He was on the ridge. He was always on the ridge. A boy came up out of the courtyard with something in his hand, a tool, a toy, it was too far to see; a small boy in grey with hair the sun had bleached to nothing, and he stood in the glare and shaded his eyes and looked at the horizon, not at the ridge, never at the ridge, as if he was waiting for something to come over it. A woman's voice called from the dome. The boy did not go in at once. Obi-Wan did not go down. There was a rule, and the rule was that he did not go down, and he had made the rule himself for reasons he could not, in the dream, remember, only their shape, like a wall under his hand in the dark. The wind came across the sand and moved the boy's hair and brought no sound up the ridge at all. The boy went in. The dome's door closed. The machines in the sand ticked in the heat, one after another, a sound like a clock that had been set to the wrong day.*

He woke. The room was black and the Temple was silent in the way it was only silent in the hour before the first bell, and his face was wet, and he lay and let it be wet and did not touch it. On the table by the bed was the pad he kept for the things that came to him at night, and the stylus beside it, and his hand went out towards them in the dark and stopped.

There were things he wrote down, and there were things he did not. He had kept the two columns for four years with great care. He put his hand back on his chest and lay in the dark and listened to the boy breathing through the wall, and did not write it down.

## Chapter 21: Not This Time

The box was full. Anakin knew it by the weight of the lid before he had it open.

He crouched on the ledge behind the third waterfall with the spray coming through his tunic and read the two lines by a glow-stick held in his fist so that only a thumb's width of light showed. The alphabet was seven back. He had stopped needing to shift it after the second note; the cipher had begun to look like plain text and the plain text like cipher, which was a thing that happened to a man who had spent twenty-three years reading encrypted traffic in the dark.

*Oba Diah. Pykes. It's a trap. Who asked for him by name?*

*S.*

He read it twice. Then he held the glow-stick to the corner of the flimsi until it caught, let it burn down to his fingers, and dropped the last of it into the water.

She was quick. He had known since the evening before and had spent the whole of today trying to decide what could be put in a box for a ten-year-old, and she had worked it out from a clan-mother's gossip and a mission roster she had no business seeing, and had got to the right question before he had.

Who asked for him by name.

He did not know. That was the truth, and he disliked it. The request had come down through the Chancellor's office, as a third of the Council's missions did, stamped and courteous: a senator with constituents on a survey team, a mineral ridge on Oba Diah, eleven Republic citizens held by the Pyke Syndicate pending the settlement of a fee. The senator's name had not been on the flimsi Obi-Wan had left on the table, and Obi-Wan had not said it, and Anakin had not asked, because asking was a thing a boy did not do and he had been careful for five months to be a boy.

It did not matter whose name was on it. He knew whose hand.

He knew Oba Diah. Not from this life. He had sat in the cockpit of a shuttle with Obi-Wan beside him, both of them grown, both of them tired, the war two years old, and listened to his master tell it in the dry voice he kept for the things that frightened him: a Master called Sifo-Dyas, lost before the war; a message from the Rishi Maze; a Pyke who had kept a dead man's lightsaber in a box for twelve years because he was more afraid of the client than of the Jedi. And the client's name, which the Pyke had given up in the end, in that other life, with the client himself standing in the room. *Tyranus.*

So. The Pykes still had the saber. The Count still had the Pykes. And a Jedi Master was being sent to the one place in the galaxy where the Sith had a body buried, at the request of a Chancellor who had every reason to want that Master alone and far from Coruscant for ten days.

And Obi-Wan, if Obi-Wan was what Anakin had spent five months half-believing and half-refusing to believe, knew every word of it. Knew the Pyke's name. Knew what was in the box. And was packing anyway.

He had the stylus out and the flimsi on his knee before he had decided what to write.

*Don't know the name. Chancellor's office carried it; a senator with surveyors. I know the place. He told me about it once, somewhere else. If he is what we think, he knows it too. He's going anyway. Can't stop him without telling him how I know.*

He looked at it. A boy's hand, because he had made himself write like a boy for five months and now could not do otherwise without thinking about it. He added a line.

*Stay away from the hangar.*

*S.*

He put the flimsi in the box and the box under the moss and went back along the ledge, and in the dark gallery he stood a moment with the water loud behind him and the thread at the back of his mind as thin as he could hold it, and tried to feel what his master was feeling two floors above. He felt what he had felt for months: a door that was neither closed nor open, and nothing beyond it he could name.

* * *

He asked the next evening, because waiting had stopped being patience and become something else.

Obi-Wan was packing. He packed the way he did everything, with a list, and the list was on the table beside the tea, and the tea was going cold because he had been reading the list instead of drinking it. Two tunics, folded. The second comlink. The small medical kit he had carried since before Naboo. The common room was the size it had always been, two doors off it and a window on the city, and it had never felt small to Anakin until this body, which had grown four centimetres since the spring and did not yet know where to put its elbows.

He stood in the door of his room with his shoulder against the frame, because his hands wanted to do something and he had learned not to let them.

"Why did you look for Kamino in the Archives?"

Obi-Wan folded the second tunic. He did it properly, sleeves in, the way he had once spent a year trying to teach a nine-year-old to do it and given up. He did not look up.

"Who told you I did?"

"The logs."

A pause the length of a breath. Then Obi-Wan set the tunic in the case and sat down across the table from the cold tea and looked at him, and his face was the face Anakin had known for thirty-six years: courteous, attentive, giving nothing away and not pretending otherwise.

"Sit down, my friend. It is not a short story, and you have taken to looming."

He sat. The chair was too low for him now, or he was too long for it.

"Sifo-Dyas," Obi-Wan said. "You will not know the name. There is no reason you should. He was a Master, on the Council for a time, and a friend of Qui-Gon's from when they were both initiates. And a friend of Dooku's, which is the only reason I know anything about him at all." He turned the cup a quarter-turn on the table and left it. "I met the Count four times while Qui-Gon lived. On one of them, in the corridor outside the Archives, he asked Qui-Gon whether he had heard from Sifo-Dyas lately. Qui-Gon said no. The Count did not care for the answer. I was twenty-three and I thought it was nothing, the way one does."

"And after Naboo."

"After Naboo." Obi-Wan's mouth moved, not quite a smile. "You will remember I was not a great deal of use to anyone for a while. Somebody had to go through my master's effects, because the Archives wanted his notes and nobody else would do it. In a treatise on the living Force, folded into the pages where no one would ever look, there was a flimsi in Qui-Gon's hand. It said Sifo-Dyas had come to him with a dream he did not trust. A war. He had asked Qui-Gon whether a man should act on a vision, and Qui-Gon had written down the question and not the answer, which was very like him." He stopped. "Clipped to it was a transit docket. A freight house on Ord Mantell, passage for one, outbound toward the Rishi Maze. The Rishi Maze, Anakin. There is nothing out there. I assumed Sifo-Dyas had left it with him the way a man leaves a thing with a friend when he is going somewhere he does not expect to come back from."

"And there was a word on it."

"There was a word on it I did not know. A destination, or a company, or a captain's joke; the docket did not say. I went down to the Archives and put the word into a terminal, and the Archives had never heard of it. Not a system, not a station, not a ship. Nothing. I sat there for a long while thinking that could not be right, and then I thought: it is a misspelling, or a smuggler's name for a rock, and I have a Padawan who has set fire to the refectory." A quarter-turn of the cup. "Four days later it occurred to me to look up the man instead of the place. The Order's word for him was *lost*, which is what the Order says about a Master it would rather not discuss. I read eleven minutes of nothing and closed the terminal and let it go. I have not thought about it from that day to this." He looked at Anakin. "That is the story. It is a dull one. I am sorry it is not better."

It was not dull. That was the trouble with it.

Anakin sat with his too-long hands on his knees and took it apart the way he had taken apart intercepted transmissions on the bridge of a ship that did not yet exist, and every piece fitted. Kamino first, forty minutes, null: a man looking up a word. Sifo-Dyas four days later, eleven minutes: a man looking up a name, finding nothing, going away. The Count asking in a corridor; the Count *had* asked, somewhere in the other life Obi-Wan had told him that too, a passing thing, a corridor. Qui-Gon's habit of writing down the question and not the answer. Ord Mantell. The Rishi Maze. The refectory, which was true, and which Anakin had forgotten until this moment: the smell of burnt sugar and a Master's hand on the back of his neck. Even the way it was told was right: the dryness, the apology at the end, the thing Obi-Wan did with a cup when he was deciding how much of himself to spend.

He could not tell.

He had lived inside Sidious's mind for twenty-three years and could hear a lie in that voice from across a room. He had been lied to by Dooku, by Tarkin, by every governor in the Outer Rim, and had known it every time. He sat across a table from the man who had raised him and could not tell whether he had just been told the truth or the best lie he had ever heard, and understood with a cold, clear feeling under the breastbone that the two were not different from the inside. A man who had come back would tell exactly that story. So would a man who had not.

Obi-Wan watched him work it through and let him.

He did not ask how a Padawan came to have two-year-old Archive logs. He did not ask whose terminal, or whose account, or what the Chief Librarian would say. He did not ask the one question any Master in the Temple would have asked first, and the not-asking sat in the room between them like a third chair, and Anakin looked at it and knew that Obi-Wan knew he was looking.

"All right," Anakin said, and heard his own voice come out of a boy's chest, light, a little sullen, exactly right. "It was just strange. Seeing your name on it."

"I expect it was." Obi-Wan got up and went back to the case. "While you are sitting down. The Council has given me Oba Diah."

"I know. When do we leave?"

"I leave the day after tomorrow, on the morning courier. You stay."

Anakin did not move. His hands did not move. It took everything.

"Say that again."

"You stay. It is a negotiation with a crime syndicate over eleven surveyors and a fee the Republic will not admit to paying. A tenday there and back. A Padawan is not needed for a negotiation, and the Council agrees, and before you say it, yes, I asked them to." The medical kit went into the case. "You have the Drallig class on the fourth day and Master Vos-Kerrick's seminar, and you have the audience, which I am told the Chancellor would be very disappointed to miss."

"You've never left me."

"No."

"Six years. Carnelion. The *Ventris* thing. The time I had a fever and you carried me onto the ship because you said you'd rather have me sick in a corridor than well on Coruscant without you."

"I remember what I said."

"Then why?"

Obi-Wan closed the case. He did it gently, the latches one at a time, and stood with his hand flat on the lid and did not turn round, and the back of his neck above the collar was the back of a man of thirty-one, not grey yet, and Anakin looked at it and saw, as he had on the first morning and every morning since, how young it was.

"Because it is a negotiation," Obi-Wan said, "and you negotiate with your hands."

It was a good answer. It was the answer Obi-Wan would have given at thirty-one in the other life, dry and affectionate and final. It was not the reason, and they both knew it was not, and the reason stood in the room beside the third chair and neither of them looked at it.

Anakin could have said *it is a trap*. He could have said *the Pykes have a dead Master's saber in a box, and the man who paid for the death is a friend of yours, and you are walking into it with two tunics and a comlink*. Every sentence of it was true and not one of them could be said by a fifteen-year-old who had never left the Core without his master, and he sat there with the truth in his mouth like a stone and swallowed it, and across the room his master stood with his hand on a closed case and swallowed, Anakin was almost sure, the same stone.

"Fine," he said.

"Anakin."

"I said fine." He stood up, too fast, caught his hip on the table; the cold tea went over and ran across the list. Neither of them moved to stop it. "Ten days. I'll try not to burn anything down."

He went into his room and shut the door, which he had not done in anger since he was eleven, and stood with his back against it in the dark and breathed, and the breath came, as it always did now, without a machine, and he hated that he noticed.

* * *

The anger was old. He knew exactly how old.

He lay on the narrow bed with his boots on and let it come, because fighting it would spill, and a spill tonight would have Masters at the door. It came up through the body like heat through a deck plate, and it was not fifteen years old. It had a voice, and the voice was deep and slow and came through a grille.

*The Council left me behind.*

They had. A hundred times. They had put him on a Council seat and refused him the rank, and sent him to guard a senator while the war was decided elsewhere, and in the end they had sent Obi-Wan to Utapau and kept the boy at home like a dog they did not trust off the lead, and the boy had gone to the opera instead, and the Chancellor had been waiting, as the Chancellor was always waiting, with a story about a Sith lord who could save people from dying.

*Obi-Wan left me.*

He had. On a black bank above a river of fire, with three limbs gone and the rest of him burning, he had stood with Anakin's saber in his hand and said the thing about brothers and walked away, and the walking away had taken twenty-three years to stop hearing. Vader had hated him for it with a hatred that had an architecture, floors and stairs and rooms, and Anakin had lived in every room of it.

He lay on the bed and felt all of it, the whole building, go up in a chest that was not big enough to hold it. A forty-five-year-old grudge in a boy's ribs. His hands shook; he let them. He knew it was borrowed. He knew it was lifted whole out of another life and laid over this one like a stencil, that this Obi-Wan had never walked away from anything, that this Council had voted exactly as any Council would vote on a tenday's negotiation with a Padawan on the roster. He knew the Chancellor's office had carried the request precisely so that the master would be ten days gone when the tenth day came and the boy sat alone in the red room with the wide window.

He knew it, and felt it anyway, and the feeling did not care what he knew.

After a while, when his hands had stopped, he took out the datapad and opened the list in the cipher keyed to a serial number no one in this galaxy would ever stamp on anything, and under *Things that are different* he wrote, in a hand that was not a boy's because there was no one to see it:

*He is leaving me. He has never left me. He is doing it on purpose and he will not say why, and I cannot say why I know, and we sat in a room and lied to each other for love and both of us knew. If he is who I think he is, he is walking into Oba Diah with his eyes open. If he is not, I have let him go blind.*

*I do not know which is worse.*

He closed the pad. Through the wall, faint and steady, the bond held its door neither open nor shut, and after a long time he slept, shielded, and no child in the Temple woke.

* * *

The morning courier left from the north bay at the seventh hour, and the north bay at the seventh hour was Knight Sabo Thane's, the way a ship was its captain's.

Thane was a big, unhurried human with a hangar officer's eye and a hangar officer's manners, which meant he looked at every person who came through his doors as a potential fire. He had looked at Anakin that way since the speeder day and had never quite stopped. He was at the foot of the courier's ramp with a manifest when they came in, and he nodded to Obi-Wan and gave Anakin the look and went back to his manifest, and the maintenance crew around the courier went on doing what maintenance crews did, which was to be loud and slow and in the way.

The courier was a long-nosed Temple ship with Republic markings, a six-seater with a hold and no guns, the kind of thing that carried diplomats and letters and, once every three months or so, a Padawan's envelope to Tatooine. Its engines were already warm. The bay smelled of coolant and hot durasteel and the city coming in through the open doors, and the light out there was the flat grey light of a Coruscant morning before the traffic had properly woken.

Anakin had carried the case. He had insisted, in a boy's voice, with a boy's shrug, and Obi-Wan had let him, and the case was heavier than two tunics and a comlink and a medical kit had any right to be, and he did not ask what else was in it.

They stopped at the ramp.

He had thought all night about what he would say and had got nowhere. Everything true was unsayable and everything sayable was small. He stood on the deck plates in the coolant smell with his master's case in his hand and his Padawan braid against his neck and could not find a single sentence.

Obi-Wan found one for him.

"You have not asked me what I am carrying," he said. "And I have not asked you where you got the logs. I think we may call that even, for now." He took the case. He did not look at it; he looked at Anakin, and the look was the one from the first morning, the counting look, the one that went over a face as if checking it against a list. "Whatever you are hiding, I would still have you at my back. But not this time."

The deck did not move. Anakin felt it move.

*Not this time.*

He had heard the words before. He had heard them in a voice that was not Obi-Wan's, in a place that was not a hangar, through the ruined left lens of a mask with a crack running down it, so that the world came in halved and bright on one side and dark on the other, and on the bright side a woman's face, white-marked, blue-eyed, older than he had ever seen it and still so young. The temple going down around them in slabs. The Force screaming. Her voice saying she would not leave him. Saying it would not be like the last time; saying those three words, exactly those, as if she had chosen them to cut. And the thing he had answered her with, the sentence he would have given the rest of his hands to unsay, that he had said in a voice that was not his and had meant every word of.

*She had been wrong. She had left him. He had made her.*

The hangar came back. Coolant, grey light, Thane's manifest, a maintenance droid whining somewhere under the courier's belly. It had been less than a second. Obi-Wan was still looking at him, and if anything had crossed Anakin's face in that second, Obi-Wan's gave no sign of having seen it, which was itself a sign, or was nothing, and he could not tell, he could not tell, he would never be able to tell.

"I'm not hiding anything," he said. The lie came out in the boy's register, thin and sulky and perfect, and he hated it.

"Of course you are not." Obi-Wan put his free hand on the back of Anakin's neck, the way he had on the night of the refectory, the way he had on a hundred ramps, a hand on a boy's neck, warm, brief. "Ten days. Do your forms. Go to your seminar. Eat something that is not from a machine. If the Chancellor offers you a seat on the Council, decline politely and send me a message." A pause. "I will come back, my friend."

"You'd better," Anakin said, and did not know which of them had said it in which life.

Then the hand was gone and Obi-Wan was up the ramp with the case, and Thane was signing the manifest and stepping back, and the ramp came up with a hydraulic sigh that Anakin had heard ten thousand times and had never, until this body, heard with ears that were his own.

The courier lifted. It did it badly, the way Temple couriers always did, a lurch and a correction, somebody's Padawan at the controls who had learned on simulators. Anakin's hands twitched to fix it. It cleared the bay doors, found its lift, and went up into the grey, into the first thin stream of the morning lanes, a long-nosed shape turning north over the Senate District with its running lights still on, and then a smaller shape, and then one of a thousand.

He stood in the hangar and watched the place where it had been.

He had watched ships leave before. He had watched them from the bridge of a star destroyer with his hands behind his back; he had watched a freighter go up from a landing pad on a cloud city with a boy's blood on its ramp; he had watched the last transport off a burning Temple with a saber in his hand and no one left to use it on. He had never, in any life, stood on a hangar deck with two flesh hands and a braid behind his ear and watched Obi-Wan Kenobi go somewhere without him. It was the first time. It had taken forty-five years and two lives to arrive, and now that it was here it felt like nothing so much as being nine, on a landing platform, watching a tall man with a ponytail walk away towards a city with a boy's whole future in his pocket.

Sabo Thane cleared his throat, which was Thane for *you are standing in my hangar*.

Anakin did not move yet.

The maintenance crew had gone back to their noise. Someone was running a diagnostic on a shuttle two bays over, a steady electronic chirp. Someone had dropped a hydrospanner. And twenty metres off, at the edge of the crew's tool cart, with a flimsi permission slip in one small fist that she was holding up at an angle so that anyone who wanted to could read the crew chief's signature on it, a ten-year-old Togruta in a sand-coloured tunic with a brown belt and lekku barely past her shoulders was looking very carefully at a coil of fuel line, and not at him, and not at the sky where the ship had been, and not at him.

He had told her to stay away.

He stood with his boy's hands empty at his sides and the city coming in grey through the doors and did not look at her either.

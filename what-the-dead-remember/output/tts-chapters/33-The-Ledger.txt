Chapter Thirty-Three. The Ledger.


The training sabers were kept in a rack by the east door of the third dojo, hilts up, twenty of them, and Anakin Skywalker handed them out one at a time because Drallig had told him, four years ago in another life and a month ago in this one, that a child who takes a weapon from a rack takes it like a thing; a child who is given one takes it like a trust.

So he stood by the rack in the morning light and gave them out. Hawkbat Clan came through the door in the order they always came, which was not an order anyone had set. Tam Dorreth first, because Tam Dorreth was first through every door in the Temple and would be until something taught him otherwise, loud already, telling Brannick Sel about a speeder he had seen from the refectory window. Brannick, whose head-tails were not yet long enough to tie back and kept swinging into his eyes. Kessa Ruun, green and solemn, who said "Thank you, Padawan Skywalker" with the whole of her ten years behind it. Ilyo, the Rodian girl, whose wrist was too thin for the hilt and who gripped it the way a child grips a pole on a moving tram. Wix, the Ithorian boy, both mouths humming under his breath, a sound like a distant generator. Fourteen more. He knew every name and every hand.

And at the back, last, the Tano child, who took her saber from him without looking up and turned it in her small fist as she walked away so that the emitter pointed behind her, down along her forearm, the way it should not be held and the way she had held one since the first week he had been here. There was a hilt on her belt now, beside the training saber's empty clip, that had not been there a month ago: plain, a little long for her, the metal still new enough to show the file marks where Huyang had let her finish it herself. She did not touch it. Nobody had told her not to wear it to initiates' class and so she wore it.

Drallig watched from the far wall with his arms folded. He had watched from the far wall every class since Oba Diah. He had never said why and Anakin had never asked.

"Lines," the battlemaster said.

They made their lines. Four rows of five, the children at arm's length from one another so that a stumble would not take a neighbour's eye, boots squeaking on the mat, Tam Dorreth still talking until Drallig looked at him and the talking stopped as if a tap had been shut. Anakin took the front, where the assistant stood, facing them. It put the whole clan in front of him at once. Twenty children in sand-coloured tunics with the hems let down twice and the belts tied by someone else that morning. Twenty unlit hilts.

"First position," he said.

They lifted them.

He had done this perhaps twenty mornings now, in this body. The first time, on the first morning, he had seen the hall on fire behind them and had put his hand on the rack to keep standing. The second time he had seen it. The tenth. The twentieth, with his voice not shaking, which was the worst of it. He had learned to let it come and to teach through it. He had thought that was the most that could be done.

Twenty children raised their sabers and he saw twenty children raising their sabers.

He did not notice it at once. He corrected Ilyo's wrist. He told Brannick to get his hair out of his eyes or he would tie it back himself, and Brannick grinned, and the Tano child's mouth moved at the corner in the back row. He walked the first line looking at feet. Then he came to the end of the line and turned to walk the second and something in him, some long-trained part that stood always at the door of the next moment waiting for the fire, said: where is it? And he realised that for the length of a walked line there had been nothing in the corner of his eye but the dojo wall, and the grey Coruscant morning in the high windows, and Drallig.

That was when it came. Not the whole of it. One line, cutting in the way it always did, the way a blade comes through a door.

The boy's hands up, both of them, the way they were taught, and the blue light on his face, and his mouth open to ask.

He stood with it. Tam Dorreth's saber drifted out of position and he said, "Tam," and Tam corrected it. His own hands were cold. He breathed, which still, after seven months, startled him: the air going in on its own, and out, no valve, no hiss. He breathed again. The dojo came back around the edges of the thing and then over it, as water comes back over a stone when the wave that bared it has gone, and the stone was still there under the water, and would be, and the water was over it.

It passed.

He had not known it could do that. He had thought the choice was between seeing it and not seeing it, and that he would see it until he died. He had not understood that there was a third thing, which was to see it and have it pass, and to find at the end of it twenty children with their sabers up and the next thing to do being to tell Wix to stop humming.

"Wix," he said. "Stop humming."

Wix stopped humming. The children laughed, which they were not supposed to do in Drallig's hall, and Drallig let them, which he had never done.

They went through the first six forms. He showed the parry before the stroke, because a child who learns to hit before he learns to be hit will hit everything, and he put them in pairs and let them sting each other. Tam Dorreth yelped. Kessa Ruun took a bruise across the knuckles from Ilyo and did not yelp, and looked at her hand as if it had surprised her, and he knelt and showed her where her guard had been and where it should have been, and she nodded with her lips pressed together and went back and did it right. Children were children. They tired by the third pairing and he could see it in their shoulders, the sabers sinking, the feet coming together, the thing that made Drallig say lines in that voice. He gave them water. He was fifteen and his own shoulders ached from holding a training saber at arm's length to demonstrate, which a man could have done all morning and a boy could not.

The Tano child was paired with Kessa, as always. She let Kessa win twice and then, in the third pass, forgot herself, or chose not to remember, and turned the reversed saber under Kessa's guard with a motion that nobody in the room had taught her and nobody in the room could have, and touched Kessa very lightly on the hip and stepped back and looked, briefly, at the floor.

Drallig saw it. Anakin watched Drallig see it. The battlemaster's face did not change, except that something went into it and stayed, the way a figure goes into a ledger.

"Enough," Drallig said. "Rack them."

They racked them. They went out in the order they had come in, Tam first, loud again the moment he reached the corridor, Wix humming, Ilyo rubbing her wrist. The Tano child went last and did not look at him and he did not look at her, and the thin thread between them, the one they had made on the nineteenth night and kept faint enough that nobody would ever feel it who was not inside it, carried for a moment something that was not a word. He let it go by.

Drallig came off the wall.

He was a square, grey man with a swordsman's stillness and a face that gave nothing to anyone, and he had been the second person to die under Vader's blade in the Temple and the only one who had made him work for it. He stood now by the rack and counted the hilts with his eyes, twenty, as he did after every class, and then looked at the Padawan beside him who had put them there.

"You watch their feet," he said.

"Yes, Master."

"Most Padawans who assist in this hall watch the blade. The blade is what they were praised for." He paused. "You correct the feet, and the feet are what will keep them alive. I do not know where you learned to do that. I did not teach it to you. I taught it to your master, and I am not certain he took it in."

Anakin said nothing. There was nothing safe to say.

"I looked at a cut on Oba Diah," Drallig said, "and I told Master Windu that I did not know anyone alive who could have made it. That was true." He looked at the rack. "I will tell you something else that is true, which I have not told Master Windu, because he did not ask. In thirty years I have had perhaps four assistants in this hall who could teach. It is not the same skill as the other. It is nearly the opposite, and I have come to think that is because a man who can do the first thing has very little reason to learn patience." He turned to go. "Whoever you are, Padawan, you have had a reason. Same time in three days."

He went out. Anakin stood by the rack with the morning on the mat and the smell of twenty children's sweat going out of the air, and for a while was only there, which was new.


He wrote the letter at the common-room table before the midday meal, because the courier went at the second hour and Obi-Wan was paying for it out of a stipend that did not have much left in it after the Council's penalty.

He wrote it by hand. He had always written them by hand; Obi-Wan had said at the beginning that a thing written by hand could be read only by someone who cared enough to decipher it. The hand that held the stylus was fifteen and still unsure of its own size. The letters came out large and a little uneven, a boy's letters, and he let them.

Mother,

We are back. The journey was fine and Obi-Wan's arm is out of the sling, though he still holds it as if it were in one when he thinks nobody is looking, and I have told him so.

I am teaching the little ones again. Hawkbat Clan, the same twenty. There is a boy who talks through everything and a girl who hums, except she is a boy, it is hard to tell with Ithorians, and a girl called Kessa who got hit on the hand today and did not cry, and I thought of you, because you would not have cried either, and then I thought you would have told me not to say so in a letter.

I am well. I am sleeping. That is true. I know you looked at me in the kitchen as if it was not true and I am telling you it is.

I have not got the hydrospanner back. I am not going to ask for it. You said I would want it and so I would come, and that is right, and I would rather it stayed where it is until I do. Tell Owen the compressor is fine and the fault was where I said it was, and that if the dome alarm goes before dawn it is the far north row and not the house, and not to get up for it, and to get up for it anyway.

The initials are mine. I scratched them with a file when I was eleven. The A is bad.

I will write again in ten days. Obi-Wan says to send his regards and to say that the tea was very good, and he means it, I have seen him with bad tea.

Ani.

He read it through once. Every line in it was true that could be true. The ones that could not be were not in it, and the space where they would have gone was the shape of a man of forty-five sitting in a kitchen on Tatooine with his mother's hand on his wrist and the thing she had seen in his face, which she had not asked about and he could not have answered. The letter went around that space the way the vaporator rows went around the rock on the north line. It was a boy's letter. He folded it and put it in the courier sleeve and sealed it with his thumb, and did not read it again, because he knew that if he did he would see the space and not the letter.


Obi-Wan gave him the list after supper.

They had eaten in the quarters, which they did more often now, the two small rooms and the common room between them that had been home for four years and were, this month, something more exact than home. Obi-Wan had made tea. The sling was off and the left shoulder still did not lift properly and he had stopped pretending it did, in here, and had let Anakin take the pot from him without a word about it. The lamp was on. The city went on outside the window with its lights and its traffic lanes stacked up into the dark, and the Senate dome was out there somewhere to the north with its own light on, and neither of them looked at it.

Obi-Wan put the cup down and went into his room and came out with a notebook.

It was flimsi. A plain cheap Temple-issue notebook of the kind the Archives gave out by the box for taking notes in a reading room, grey cover, the corners gone soft. He had seen it before, on the third night after Oba Diah when the three of them had laid their lists out one beside another and nobody had read anyone else's, and face down on the shelf by Obi-Wan's bed every morning since, with a cup on it. He had never opened it.

Obi-Wan put it on the table between the cups and sat down and did not push it across.

"I began it on the third night," he said. "After I woke. You know that. I did not know what else to do with the nights. I wrote down everyone I could remember and where, and when I remembered another one I wrote that one down, and when I was told one by someone, later, in the years after, I wrote that down too, with the date I was told. There are forty-one. I have read it at night, only at night, and never in the day, for four years." He stopped. He had a way of stopping before the thing he meant to say, as if to give it room. "I kept it so I would not forget what we are preventing."

Anakin pulled it across and opened it.

The handwriting was Obi-Wan's and it was four years old. That was the first thing. The hand on the first page was the hand of a man who had been awake for three days in a body he did not recognise, pressing too hard, the letters leaning, a tremor in the downstrokes that Obi-Wan would have been ashamed of in a report. By the third page it had steadied. By the tenth it was the hand Anakin knew, neat, upright, a little impatient at the ends of words, the hand that wrote on the backs of mission orders and on the margins of his Padawan's astrogation homework. The list was in no order that he could see. It was in the order of remembering.

Cin Drallig. The Temple.

That was the first line. He had known it would be and it still went in under the ribs like a parry that was not there.

Jurokk. The Temple, the gate.

Depa Billaba. Kaller. Her Padawan survived.

Plo Koon. Over Cato Neimoidia. His own men.

Ki-Adi-Mundi. Mygeeto.

Aayla Secura. Felucia.

Luminara Unduli. Taken alive; Kashyyyk. I was told the rest later and do not know if it is true.

They went on. Some had places and some did not. Some had a date beside them in a different ink, the date Obi-Wan had learned of it, nineteen years before the Death Star or ten or two, a date that was now meaningless and that he had written anyway because he was a man who put dates on things. Some had a word after them, a single word, clones or Temple or unknown, and one had me, and he read that one twice before he understood it was Obi-Wan's note against his own name on the page of a man whose death he had been told of and could not have prevented and had counted himself responsible for anyway.

He went through to the end. Forty-one. He went through again, more slowly, because he was looking for something, and did not find it.

"I'm not in it," he said.

"No."

"Why not?"

Obi-Wan did not look at the notebook. He looked at the window. "I tried," he said. "Every night for the first month. I would get to the end and sit with the stylus and I could not find the page it belonged on. There was a page for the ones who died and a page I did not keep for the one who did it and I could not put you on either. So I did not put you anywhere." A pause. "I am aware that this is not the same as leaving you out."

Anakin closed the notebook and then opened it again at the last written page, where there was most of a side of flimsi still blank under the forty-first name, and held out his hand.

Obi-Wan gave him the stylus.

"These are the ones you don't have," he said. "The ones after. You wouldn't have had them. You were on the ridge." He bent over the page and his hand, writing, was not the hand that had written the letter at midday. It was smaller and quicker and it did not make a boy's letters. "Jocasta Nu. Coruscant, outside the Temple. She came back for a holocron and he found her." He wrote it. "Kirak Infil'a. Al'doleem. The first. He beat the thing that came for him nearly to death, and then it came back." He wrote it. "A Padawan on the Archive stairs. The night itself. I don't know her name. I never did. She had a blue blade and she stood on the fourth step so that she would have the height." He wrote Padawan, Archive stairs, the night and under it, without a name, drew a short line where the name would be. "Mon Cala. Four. Cabarria, one; I never learned it either. Chandrila, two, together. A Knight on Dathomir who had gone to hide among the Nightsisters, and the Nightsisters. Vuvur."

He wrote until the page was full. He did not count them. Obi-Wan did not ask him to. When he put the stylus down his hand was steady and his face was not, and he let it not be, in here, where the only person who could see it had seen the other thing.

"There are more," he said. "There were more than I ever knew. The Inquisitors took their own and did not always report. I have only the ones I saw." He turned the notebook round so that it faced Obi-Wan and pushed it back across the table. "Those are mine."

Obi-Wan looked at the page for a long time. He did not touch it. Then he said, "There should be three," and Anakin, who had been thinking it and had not wanted to be the one to say it, reached inward along the thin thread, the one the length of the Temple, and tugged.


She came in twenty minutes, by the service corridor, at an hour when a youngling should have been asleep in the Hawkbat dormitory; a Temple Guard who saw a child in a sand tunic in that corridor would assume an errand for a Master, because what else would she be doing. She knocked twice and once. Obi-Wan opened the door and she came past him into the lamplight and looked at the table and understood it before anyone spoke.

She was ten. He never stopped seeing it and never stopped being unable to see past it: she came to his shoulder, and he was not tall; her lekku were barely past her collar; she had to climb up onto the chair, not sit on it, and she did, and set her grey flimsi on the table beside the notebook without being asked, and the green hilt knocked against the chair-leg as she settled, and she put her hand over it the way a child puts a hand over a thing that she has been told not to bring and has brought.

"All three," she said.

"All three," Obi-Wan said.

She pulled the notebook over and read it, as he had, straight through. She read faster. He watched her get to Plo Koon and stop, and go on. He watched her get to Depa Billaba. Kaller. Her Padawan survived and put her finger on the line and hold it there, and not say Caleb's name, and not say the other one either, and take her finger away. She read his page. She did not say anything about his page.

"Mine are mostly not Jedi," she said.

"Then write them."

She took the stylus. Her hand was the smallest of the three and the most certain, because she had had seven years to learn this body and had learned it the way she learned everything, completely. She wrote in Basic, not Togruti, because the list was to be read.

Jesse. Mandalore. He was chipped, and he tried to kill me, and he died in the crash. I put his helmet on a pole with the others.

The Venator. All of them. The 332nd. I buried as many as I could find. I did not count. I should have.

Maul's list is on my flimsi and I am not copying it in, because they are his.

She stopped. She looked at Obi-Wan's forty-one and at the one line among them she had held her finger on, and wrote nothing beside it, because it was already written. Then she bent over the page again and wrote, in letters that pressed harder than the rest:

Rex. Alive. He got out with me. He is on Kamino now and he is six years old and he is on no list that anyone has made and I am putting him on this one.

"This is a list of the dead," Anakin said. He said it gently. He was not arguing.

"Then it needs one who isn't," she said, "or it's only a list." She put the stylus down. "He's the reason I know the ones above him. Jesse would have shot me if Rex hadn't got between us first. I'm not writing Jesse without writing Rex. You can take it out when I'm gone."

"Nobody is taking anything out," Obi-Wan said.

They copied it in. It took the better part of an hour. Anakin's datapad, the one with the cipher on it, keyed to a serial number from a machine that did not yet exist. He opened a new file beside the cipher and did not encode it, because this one was going to be read by three people who already knew everything in it, and he keyed in Obi-Wan's forty-one from the flimsi while Obi-Wan read them out, and then his own page, and then Ahsoka's, and then she gave him the grey flimsi and he keyed in her eight Togruti lines beside a column where she wrote what each one was in Basic, and she did it without hesitating, which told him she had decided in the corridor. Then Obi-Wan held out his hand and Anakin, after a moment, opened the cipher and gave him the key, and Obi-Wan read Things that are different in silence, forty-some entries now, the last of which was in a different hand and said I do not know which is worse, and did not ask whose hand, and copied it in.

Three lists. One datapad. The flimsi notebook lay beside it with the stylus on top, and when the copying was done Obi-Wan turned it face down, out of four years' habit, and then after a moment turned it face up again and left it.

"Will you tell Yoda?" Anakin said.

Obi-Wan looked at the notebook.

"When he can hear it," he said.

Ahsoka climbed down off the chair. She put her hand on the datapad once, flat, the way she had put it on the hilt, and took it away. She did not look at Anakin and he did not look at her and the thread between them said what it said. At the door she turned and said to Obi-Wan, in the careful child's voice she used in corridors, "Thank you, Master Kenobi," for the Guard who might be listening, and then in her own voice, very low, "He'd have put himself on it. Rex. If he'd made one. That's why I did," and went.

Obi-Wan sat a moment looking at the door, and then he reached into his sleeve and laid something beside the notebook that he had evidently been carrying all evening. It was a letter, flimsi folded in three and gone soft at the creases, the seal never broken, and the years were in it the way they were in the notebook's corners. On the outside, in a hand Anakin knew and had not seen in a very long time, it said Padawan Skywalker, care of Master Kenobi. "I found it in my own files the year I came," Obi-Wan said, in the tone he kept for the condition of a hyperdrive. "The man I was before had put it away unopened, and I have not known what to do with it since, which is not a thing I admit often." Anakin turned it over once, with the seal under his thumb, and thought of a woman saying I never heard back, and put it inside his tunic unopened.


The lamp had been on a long time. The tea was cold. Obi-Wan did not get up to make more, which was how Anakin knew that he had understood there was a thing still on the table that was not the notebook.

He had been sitting with it for seven months, and for twenty-three years before that in a body that could not sit, and he had once thought that if he ever had a chance to say it he would say it somewhere with the sky in it. He had not thought it would be a common room with two cold cups and a flimsi notebook. He found that this was better. Nothing in the room could burn.

"You said you loved me," he said. "On the bank."

Obi-Wan did not move. The shoulder that did not lift stayed where it was.

"I did."

Anakin looked at him. He was fifteen and it was in his face and his hands and the way his knees did not reach the floor properly from the chair, and under it he was the other thing, and he let both of them be there, because there was nobody in the room who did not know. His voice when it came was not a boy's and not the one he used for the Chancellor. It was the one from the bank, the one that had said the other thing, which he had never taken back because there had not been a mouth to take it back with.

"Say it where I can hear it."

Obi-Wan put his good hand flat on the table, beside the notebook, not on it.

He had never been a man who said things twice. He had said them once, exactly, and let them stand, and had spent twenty years on a ridge learning what it cost to have said one of them to a man who was on fire and could not have heard it over the sound of himself. Anakin had heard it. He had always heard it. That was the thing Obi-Wan did not know and was not going to be told tonight, because tonight was not for what Anakin had heard.

"You were my brother, Anakin," Obi-Wan said. "I loved you."

The city went on outside the window.

He did not say it the way it had been said on the bank. There was no lava in it and no turning away. He said it looking at the boy across the table, and he said it in the tense it had been said in, because it was the thing that had been asked for and he was a precise man, and then, after a moment in which neither of them breathed, he said, more quietly, in a voice he had heard once, in another life, "I have not stopped," and took his hand off the table.

Anakin did not answer. There was no line for it. He sat in the lamplight with the air going in and out of him on its own, which still startled him, and let it.

After a while Obi-Wan reached over and turned the lamp down, not off, and neither of them moved to go to bed, and the notebook lay face up between the cups with the stylus across it, and the datapad beside it dark, and the Temple around them full of children asleep.

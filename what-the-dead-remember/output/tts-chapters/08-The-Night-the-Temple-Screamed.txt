Chapter Eight. The Night the Temple Screamed.


Ahsoka was inside it before she knew she was asleep.

That was the first wrong thing. Her own dreams came to her as dreams, soft at the edges, so that even under them some part of her knew they would end. This one had no edges. There was a stair, and she was on it, and she knew the stair. She knew the height of its risers because she had once been smaller than they were. She knew the band of paler stone at the turn where the mortar had been repointed in the wrong colour, and the way the light from the high windows fell across the eleventh step and not the twelfth. She knew the stair. She would not say whose stair it was.

Then there was fire. Not the clean fire of a brazier or a forge. Fire in a place that had never been meant to burn, licking up the inside of a hall taller than ten of her, so that the great hanging lamps turned and swung in the heat and the shadows swung with them. Smoke lay along the ceiling like a second ceiling. Beneath it, in lines, in step, came the white. Rows of it, shoulder to shoulder, the colour of a thing scoured clean, and the sound its boots made on the floor was a sound she had known in her sleep for seven years and had never once let herself hear while awake.

She did not name it. In the dream she turned her face from it as a person turns from a lamp that is too bright, and she would not give it a word.

At the end of the hall was a blue light, and at the end of the blue light was a boy's face.

He was looking up. The blade was a hand's breadth from him, lighting him from beneath, so that his eyes and the hollows of his cheeks were the colour of the blade and the rest of him was dark. He was not afraid yet. That was the worst of it. He was asking something. His mouth was open on a question and the question had a name in it, and she knew the name, and the dream ended on it the way a cable ends when it is cut.


She woke on the floor between the bunks.

She did not remember falling. She had been in the lower bunk nearest the door, as she had been for three years, and now she was lying on her side on the cold tile between her bunk and Kessa's with her cheek against the leg of the frame and her hands fisted under her chin. The dormitory was dark. The dormitory was full of noise.

Every child in Hawkbat Clan was crying.

She lay still and let herself hear it, because she needed to hear it to know where she was. Twenty children. Tam Dorreth, who was eleven and loud in all things, was loud in this; he was sitting up and sobbing in great wet gulps and saying the boots, the boots. And above her, a hand's reach away, Kessa Ruun was crying the way Kessa did everything, carefully and without wanting to be a bother, into the crook of her own elbow.

Ahsoka did not cry.

She got up. Her legs were a child's legs and they did not quite hold her the first time, so she put a hand on the bunk frame and tried again, and then she was standing in the dark in a tunic that came to her knees with her lekku cold against her neck and her heart going like something trying to get out. She walked to the refresher at the end of the dormitory. She did not run. She closed the door and put the light on low and knelt at the basin, and she was sick, in silence, with her hands flat on the cold rim and her breath counted between, the way a grown person is sick when there are others on the far side of a door.

When it was done she rinsed her mouth. Then she went out and climbed into Kessa's bunk and held her.

Kessa turned into her at once, the way she would have turned into anyone. She was a Mirialan girl of ten with a fringe of dark hair and the diamond marks of her people on her cheeks, and she was shaking so hard that her teeth clicked. "There was a stair," she said into Ahsoka's shoulder. "Soka. There was a stair and the lights were all. And there was water. There was water going somewhere and I couldn't."

"I know," Ahsoka said.

"Did you have it?"

"Yes."

"Did you have the chairs? There were chairs in a circle and nobody was, "

"Yes," she said. "Hush. It's gone now. It's gone."

She held the girl and looked over her head at the dark room and the children in it, and she thought: a circle of chairs. They had been given the stair and the gate and the water and the blue light and the chairs. They had been given the house of it. They had not been given the hall. They had not been given the white, or the boy's face with the question in it. That had been hers. She did not know what that meant and she did not want to, and the not-wanting was so strong that it felt like a hand on her mouth.

The corridor door opened and the lights came up to half, and Master Imari Sen came in with her sleeves pushed back and her grey hair down, which none of them had ever seen, and behind her two healers in their pale tunics, and behind them, carried and led and stumbling, the smallest children of Bear Clan.


They were brought to the common room because the healers wanted the clans where they could be counted. Hawkbat sat on the floor in two uneven rows with blankets round their shoulders. Bear were put on the benches, where their feet did not reach the floor, and a healer went along them with a datapad and a light and asked each one the same three questions in the same gentle voice. Did you dream? Was there a stair? Was there a blue light?

Thirty-one, across every clan in the Temple. Ahsoka heard the number because she was listening for it. The healer said it low to Master Sen at the door, and Master Sen put her hand over her mouth, and the healer said something else that had four of the older ones in it and holding it, and then looked at the children and stopped.

Ahsoka was ten years old and sitting cross-legged on a floor with a blanket over her head like a hood, and she looked at the healer's hands, not the healer's face, and she thought about the four older ones and what it was they had been holding. She stopped thinking about it. She had got good at that. It was like putting a cup down.

A small weight arrived against her side.

Caleb Dume was seven. He was a Bear Clan boy with hair that would not lie flat and a way of looking at things slightly too long for comfort, and for a year he had followed her about the Temple the way a tooka follows a person who once fed it. He had a blanket too, and under it he had found her sleeve, and he had closed his whole fist on the cloth of it as a child holds a handrail on a ship, and he did not say anything at all.

"Hello, Caleb," she said.

He looked up at her. His eyes were green and open and perfectly whole, and they went over her face, checking it, and then he put his forehead against her arm and kept it there. He had not been crying. He had the particular pale stillness of a child who has gone past it.

She did not take her sleeve back. She found that she had shifted, without deciding to, so that her shoulder was square to his shoulder and her arm was where his hand could find it without looking, as if he could not have found it with his eyes; and she felt herself do that and did not stop it, and it was another thing she did not think about.

The children stopped crying one by one, the way rain stops. The healers took four away to the Halls, Tam among them, still saying the boots. The rest were told to sleep where they were. Ahsoka sat with Kessa's head in her lap and Caleb's fist in her sleeve and did not sleep, and watched the window at the end of the room go from black to grey.


Master Plo came at the second hour of the morning.

He was very large and the door was not, and he ducked through it in the way she had loved since before she could remember loving anything, and the clan, which had been pretending to be asleep, stopped pretending. He went along the rows. He did not say much. He put his gloved hand on each head in turn, Hawkbat and Bear alike, and he said each name, and the children went quiet under his hand as they always did, as if the mask and the breather and the great gentle bulk of him were a thing that had been put in the galaxy for that purpose.

He came to her last. She had known he would.

"Little 'Soka."

"Master Plo."

He sat on the end of the bench, which creaked, and the Bear Clan children on it slid gently toward him. Caleb had been collected an hour before by a clan-master with a tired face; her sleeve was still creased where his hand had been. Kessa was asleep against her at last, properly, with her mouth open. Plo looked at the sleeping girl and then at Ahsoka, and she could feel him looking through the lenses, and she made her face a child's face and kept it there.

"You did not go to the Halls," he said.

"I wasn't crying."

"No," he said. "You were not." He let that sit for a moment. "The healers tell me thirty-one younglings in the Temple shared it. Most of your clan, and the Bear children over four among them. They tell me it was one dream, in all of them, with the same rooms in it."

"The same rooms," she agreed.

"Did you have the same rooms?"

"Yes, Master."

He waited. He was very good at waiting. She had learned a good deal of what she knew about waiting from him and she used it now against him, which was a thing she had never once let herself feel badly about, and did.

"Master Plo," she said. "Can I ask you something? Not about me."

"You may ask me anything, little one. Whether I can answer it is a different matter."

She looked at her hands. They were small and the knuckles were skinned from the training floor and they lay in her lap like somebody else's. "If a youngling had a dream," she said. "Not like this one. One of her own. And it was true. Not true like a thing that might happen, that the Force shows you and then it changes. True like, " She stopped and chose the word the way one chooses a foothold. "True like a thing that had happened. Somewhere. And she knew it had. Would the Council believe her?"

She felt him go still. It was not a thing she could have seen; the mask gave nothing and the hands did not move. It was in the Force, where he lived to her as a warmth the size of a room, and the warmth did not go cold but it stopped, for a breath, the way a person stops on a stair.

"Would they believe her," he repeated.

"Or would they say she was ten."

"Ah." He turned his head, and she saw the window in his lenses, grey. "You ask a Master's question, and I will give you a Master's answer, and you will not like it, and I am sorry for that in advance." The breather hissed. "The Council does not count years, Ahsoka. The Force does not count them either, and the Council tries, with varying success, to listen to the Force. If a child of ten came before them and said I have seen a thing that is true, they would not laugh at her. They would listen. They would be kind."

"But."

"But," he said, "a dream that is true and a dream that is only very much wished for, or very much feared, feel the same from inside. I have had both. There is no way to tell them apart from the inside of one's own head. That is what Masters are for. That is why a youngling has a Master, and a Master has a Council, and the Council has the Force, and even the Force", he tipped his head, "is sometimes in motion. The Council would believe that she believed it. That is not nothing. It is also not what you asked."

"No," she said.

"No." He was quiet. "You asked me something like this once, at five, in the Fountains. I have reminded you of it before."

"I don't remember," she said.

It was the one lie she told him that morning, and she had told it before, and it went in exactly as it had the first time. She watched it go in. She was not sure that it had.

He looked at her for a long time.

She had been looked at by him for seven years, and she knew most of his looks, and this was not one of them. It was not suspicion. It was something slower, something that went round her like a man walking round a sculpture he has been told is one thing and is beginning to think may be another. It lasted long enough that Kessa stirred against her and settled, and Ahsoka kept her face where it was, and then it was over, and he put his hand on her head as he had on all the others.

"Sleep, little 'Soka," he said. "If you can. If you cannot, come and find me, and we will not sleep together, which is a thing I am quite good at."

She said yes, Master. He went out, ducking. She did not sleep.


The Gathering was in ten days.

She had known it was coming for a month, the way all the clan had known it. Master Yoda would take a group to Ilum aboard the Crucible: initiates of about ten and over who were ready, and readiness was a thing the clan-mother drafted and Master Yoda approved and nobody else discussed. Ahsoka was ten by four months. She was good; she knew exactly how good she was, which was far too good, and she had been shaving it for three years, letting Tam take her blade in demonstrations, falling where a child would fall. She had thought she would go to Ilum when it was her turn, and had not much cared when that was.

She cared now. She had cared since the moment, two days after the dream, when Master Sen said in the refectory, in the carrying voice she used for things that were already decided, that Master Yoda had asked for a senior Padawan to accompany the group, as was sometimes done, and that it would be Master Kenobi's Padawan, who already assisted in the initiates' classes and knew the children.

Ahsoka had kept her spoon moving. The soup was the thin green soup that came on the third day of every tenday and she ate all of it.

Then she set about it.

She did it the way she did everything now, which was in pieces too small to be seen. In Drallig's hall, that tenday, she held her saber the right way round for the whole of the first form and reversed it only at the end, when a girl of ten who was tired and showing off might, because a girl who never did anything wrong was a girl somebody would eventually look at.

On the third day she found Master Plo in the lower gardens, where he walked when the Council had been long, and she fell into step beside him with the hem of her tunic catching on the gravel and said, in the voice of a child who has been working up to something all the way down the stairs, "Master Plo, am I ready for Ilum?"

He walked a few steps. "Do you think you are?"

"I don't know," she said, which was the right answer and also, she found, true. "Kessa's on the list. She'll be frightened in the caves. She's frightened of the dark on the stair to the dormitory." She looked up at him, which she had to do, which she always had to do. "I thought if I was there."

"That is a kind reason."

"It's not the only one."

"No," he said. "I did not suppose it was." But he did not ask what the other was, and she had been counting on that, and was, in the part of her that was not counting anything, a little ashamed. "Master Sen drafts the list. I may say a word to her. I will say only that you have asked, and that I think you are able. Whether able is the same as ready she will decide."

"Thank you, Master."

"Do not thank me yet," he said. "The caves are very cold."

She said a word to Master Sen herself the same evening, carefully, about Kessa, about the dark on the stair. The clan-mother listened with her hands in her sleeves and her grey head on one side, and said that the list was the list, and that it was not a thing children lobbied for, and that she would think about it. Her name was on it the next morning, sixth of six: Tano, A., Hawkbat. Ahsoka read it on the board outside the refectory with Kessa jumping up and down beside her and did not jump, and let Kessa hug her, and looked at the name on the board as if it belonged to somebody small.

In the corridor beyond the refectory a Pau'an Temple Guard went by in his mask and yellow robes, unhurried, and Ahsoka stepped back against the wall before she knew she had moved, her shoulders gone up around her neck. She could not have said what she was afraid of, and she was ashamed of it, and she made her face smooth again before he had passed. Kessa, still bouncing, saw nothing, and neither did the Guard.


The clan went to the Room of a Thousand Fountains on the fifteenth day, all of them together, because the healers had said water and green things and because Master Sen did not argue with healers.

It was too large to be a room. The waterfalls at the far end made a sound that got into the bone, and the air was wet and warm and smelled of soil and moss. The paths went in loops and over small bridges, and the clan went along them in a straggle, and she walked in the middle with Kessa's hand in hers and the bright noise of the water filling the place where thought would have been.

She felt him before she saw him. She had been feeling him for fifteen days, the hard-shielded thing in the Temple that she had fixed, rightly or wrongly, on the Padawan with the braid; it was there at the back of her mind the way a tooth is there when it is going bad, and she had got used to carrying it and never once stopped knowing where it was. Now it was ahead of her, and to the left, and close.

They were on the bridge over the second pool. Master Kenobi and his Padawan, standing, not walking, the Master with his hands behind his back and his beard and his brown robe and the boy beside him in the sand-coloured tunic that fitted him badly because he was growing too fast for it, with his hands in his sleeves and his face turned to the water. They were not talking. They had the look of people who had been talking and had stopped.

Her clan went past them on the lower path, below the bridge, in a straggle of ten-year-olds, and she went with it, and she looked up because she could not have done anything else.

The Padawan did not look down. He was looking at the water with the particular blankness she had seen on him in Drallig's hall, the face of a boy who was somewhere else and keeping it off his face. She saw his hand, the right one, go once to his left wrist and close there and open, and she filed that, as she filed everything about him, on the list that was not written anywhere.

Master Kenobi looked down.

He looked down at the line of children and his eyes went along it as a man's eyes go along a shelf, and they came to her, and they stopped.

It was not long. It was a beat too long. It was the length of time it takes a person to recognise a face in a crowd and then remember that they could not possibly, and in the whole of that length his face did not change at all, which was itself a thing. He was a Jedi Master and she was a youngling of ten with wet moss on the hem of her tunic and a Mirialan girl's hand sweating in hers, and he looked at her as if she were a word he had been trying all week to remember, and she looked back.

She looked away first.

She did not decide to. Her eyes simply went to the path, to the gravel and the moss and her own small boots, and she found that her hand had tightened on Kessa's hard enough that Kessa said ow, Soka, and she said sorry, and the bridge was behind them, and the sound of the waterfalls came up and took everything.

She did not look back.


That night she went to the dojo.

It was the fifth night and so it was permitted, by the rules she had made for herself and kept as if somebody else had made them. The third-level dojo was small and square and badly lit; the fault ticket on its lighting was three years old and the strips along one wall came on at half and the strips along the other did not come on at all, so that the room was always divided into a lit half and a dim one, and she worked in the dim.

She used the two short wooden rods the youngest children used for footwork, because they were the right length for her wrists, which the sabers were not, and because wood made no sound and no light. She held them reversed.

She worked for an hour. Two blades, the body low, the left rod guarding the line the right one opened. She did not think about the stair or the hall or the white. She did not think about the boy on the bridge with his hand on his wrist, or the Master with his long look. She moved until her arms shook and her tunic was dark at the back and her breath was the loudest thing in the room, and then she stopped and stood in the middle of the floor with the rods hanging from her hands and let the dim half of the dojo be quiet round her.

Then she looked up at the gallery.

She did not know why. She had looked up at it on the first night, eleven nights ago, when something that might have been nothing had sat in the dark up there and watched her, and she had not been able to prove it and had told herself so. She had not looked up since. She looked now, and there was nothing there, only the rail and the dark and the shut door at the gallery's end.

She went up anyway.

The stair to the gallery was steep and made for grown legs and she went up it on her hands as much as her feet, with the rods pushed through her belt. The gallery was a narrow walk along two sides of the room with a wooden rail at hip height, a grown person's hip, her chest. Nobody used it. The dust on the rail was the thick soft kind that comes to things nobody uses, and she could see it in the half-light from below, an even grey along the whole length of the wood, like a skin.

Except where it was not.

Near the corner, where the gallery turned and a person sitting on the floor could put their back to one wall and look down at the dim half of the room, the dust on the rail was gone in a patch the width of two hands. Not wiped. Worn, as a thing is worn when somebody rests their forearms on it and leans and looks and does not move for a long time. The wood under it was dark with old oil and showed through. Below, on the boards of the gallery floor, the dust was flattened in a shape she looked at for some time before she let herself call it what it was, which was the shape of someone who had sat there with their knees up.

She put out her hand, and did not touch it, and took her hand back.

It had not been there eleven nights ago. She would have sworn to that. She did not know for how long, or how many nights, or whether it was one person or several; a Knight checking a fault ticket, a Guard on a round, a child from another clan come to watch the strange Togruta girl who held her sticks the wrong way round. She did not know whether it was him. She did not know. She stood in the dark on a gallery with the rail at the height of her chest and the dust of it on her sleeve and she made herself say it in her head, in the plain words, which was the discipline: I do not know who sat here.

Below her, the lit half of the dojo hummed in its strips. The dim half was empty. She had left the rods on the floor down there, the two of them, lying crossed where she had let them fall, and from above they looked very small.

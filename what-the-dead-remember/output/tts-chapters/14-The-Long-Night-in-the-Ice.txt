Chapter Fourteen. The Long Night in the Ice.


Ahsoka Tano had been ten years old for four months, and in that time she had learned nothing new about being ten; she had learned it all already, twice over. What she had not learned, in seven years, was how to stand in a cave with her back to a wall of ice and look up at a boy who had once been her master and once been the thing that killed him, and know which one she was looking at.

He had taken his hands off her shoulders. That was the first thing. He had taken them off as if the cloth had burned him and stepped back, and now there were three paces of blue-lit floor between them and she could see all of him: the tunic short in the wrist, the thin braid behind the ear, the mouth open a little on a breath that showed white, the eyes. Fifteen. He was taller than her by a head and a half and he had a lightsaber hilt in his right hand, not lit, the thumb resting on the switch the way a boy's thumb would rest if he had been taught to keep it there, and the way a man's would if he had used it every day of his life.

She had a crystal. It was in her fist, and the fist was in front of her at the height of her own chest, which was the height of his belt, and the green of it came through her fingers so that the small bones showed like a lamp held behind a leaf. It was the only thing in the chamber she owned.

"Say something," he said.

"I did."

Her voice came out of her the way it always did, high and light, a girl's instrument. She had hated it for seven years. She hated it now, because she wanted to sound like what she was, and what she was had died in a temple a great deal darker than this one, and the sound that came out of her was a child's.

He did not move. Behind him, in the ice where she had seen it, the black shape had gone; the wall was only a wall again, lit faintly from within as all the walls here were, and her own reflection stood in it, small, with the lekku barely to her shoulders and the hem of her tunic touching the tops of her boots. Her breath went up in front of her. The cold was in her feet already. On Ilum the cold came in through the soles first and then up the shins and then, if you let it, into the places where you kept things.

She held the wall. She held it the way he had taught her, on a transport in the dark, with the noise over the top and the plain girl's fears laid out beneath the noise for anyone to find, and the true thing under that. He had gone up it. She had felt him go up it and stop. He had recognised the seams; she had felt that too, a jolt down the whole length of him, like a man who has put his hand on his own handwriting in a stranger's house.

Which proved nothing. The thing in the mask had known the seams as well. The thing in the mask had known everything.


She had felt him come three weeks and two days ago, above and to the west, like a struck bell, and she had lain in the Hawkbat dormitory with Kessa breathing in the next bunk and her two hands pressed flat on her stomach to keep herself from sitting up, because a child who sat up in the dark at the fourth hour and said he's here would be in the Halls of Healing by first bell with a note in her file. She already had one. She had been five, and the dream had been of a hall and fire and white ranks and a boy's face, and she had asked a healer whether a dream could be a memory, and understood from the healer's face that she must never ask anything like that again.

So she had let the bell ring through her and not sat up.

Seven years. She had come awake on Shili at three years old in a body that could not run without falling, with her mother's voice in the next room saying a name she had not heard in thirty years, and she had understood, in about the time it took to fall down, what had happened and that there was no one in the galaxy to tell. Days later a Kel Dor Jedi had crouched in the grass in front of her and held out a hand, and she had taken it with a hand so small it closed round two of his fingers, and she had thought, Master Plo, and said nothing, and let him carry her.

The Temple had been worse. On Shili she had at least been able to be strange. In the Temple strangeness was recorded. She had learned to lose a bout to a boy she could have disarmed with her eyes shut, and to look as if losing it hurt; she had learned when children were expected to cry, and to do it then, and not when she wanted to; she had learned that when the other younglings wept at night she must not go to them. She had learned to be intense rather than strange. Intense was a word they had for children. It went in a file and nothing came of it.

She had a list. She had made it at seven, on grey flimsi stolen from the Archives, in Togruti, which no one in Hawkbat read, and she kept it under her mattress. Eight lines. Names, mostly, and one date, and one word that was not a name but a point on a compass. No one had seen it. On Shili a green bird had sat on the roof of her mother's house for four days and watched her learn to walk again, and she had told no one about that either.

And she had waited. For a boy with a braid to turn up in the initiates' saber class each tenday and correct her grip with a bored face, and for her to look at his hands and feel nothing, because there was nothing there yet to feel. For seven years there had been nothing. She had watched him at the back of a hundred rooms and he had been a boy and that was all, and she had been relieved, every time, and sick with it.

Three weeks ago the bell. And then he had walked into Drallig's hall on the ninth day and taken a stance she had not seen in twenty years, and she had held her training saber backwards in front of him on purpose, to see what his face did, and his face had done nothing at all, which was the most frightening thing it could have done.

She had been trying ever since to get close enough to be sure. She had not thought about what being sure would mean. She had not let herself. Being sure meant one of two things, and one of them was good and she did not believe in it, and the other was standing three paces away from her now with its thumb on a switch.


"I'm going to sit down," he said. "Over there. Against the wall. You can keep the width of the chamber if you want it."

"Put the saber on the floor."

He looked at the hilt as if he had forgotten it. Then he crouched, slowly, the way you crouched in front of an animal, and set the hilt on the ice and stood up, and the hilt lay there between them and did not roll. She had not expected that. She did not let her face show that she had not expected it.

"I can call it," he said. "You know that."

"I know that."

"So it doesn't help you."

"It helps me," she said, "to see if you'd do it."

He went to the far wall and sat down with his back to it and his knees up, and the tunic rode up his shins, and he pulled it down with a boy's unthinking tug and then stopped, mid-tug, and looked at his own hand doing it. She filed that.

Her feet had gone past aching into something else. She put the crystal in her left hand and the right hand under her arm, and after a while she sat too, because a soldier did not stand for nine hours to make a point. She sat cross-legged on the ice with the width of the chamber between them, and the blue light came up through the floor, and their breath went up, and nobody said anything for the length of time it took her to count to four hundred.

"Where did you come from?" he said.

"You first."

"No." He said it gently. "You've got a crystal and I've got nothing. You first. Then you'll know what I say doesn't come from what you said."

That was his voice. Not the pitch; the shape of the sentence. The way he would lay a thing down in front of you so that you had to pick it up. She had been fourteen and he had done that to her every day for three years and she had not once seen it coming.

She told him.

She told it the way she would have told it to a debrief: flat, in order, nothing in the voice. The temple, which was a different kind of temple from this one. The stairs that went down. Kanan, who was Caleb Dume, seven years old, Bear Clan, a boy who followed her about with a mouth full of questions; she had watched him have his sight taken, red through the eyes, by a man both of them had thought was dead. The thing at the top of the stairs. She told him what it had looked like, from below, with the ice of this chamber making the shape of it for her an hour ago as if it had been in the wall all along, waiting.

"It said, 'Anakin Skywalker was weak. I destroyed him.' I said I would avenge his death. It said revenge was not the Jedi way. I said I was no Jedi." She looked at him across the floor. "I cut the mask. I got in under the side of it and cut up and the mask broke, and there was an eye. It said my name. In your voice. Not the mask's. Yours." She heard her own small voice go thin and did not stop. "I said, 'I won't leave you. Not this time.' And it said, 'Then you will die.'"

He had not moved. His hands were on his knees. The knuckles had gone white, which she would not have known from a mask.

"And then the temple came down," she said, "and that's the last thing. That's where I'm from. A floor, and a red light coming through the floor, and the thing I'd said it to coming up the steps. I don't know what happened after. I don't think anything did." She stopped. "So you'll understand," she said, "if I ask what's wearing your face."


He told her Endor.

She had never heard the word. It was a moon, he said, green, forested, with a Death Star over it, and that was a word she did know and did not want to; a second one, he said. He told it as flatly as she had told hers, as if they had agreed on the register. An Emperor in a throne room. A boy, twenty-three, blond, with a green blade. Luke. His son. He said the name as if it had cost him something to keep it in his mouth so long. The boy had refused to kill him. The Emperor had put lightning into the boy and the boy had screamed, and he had stood and watched, and then he had not. He had picked the old man up and thrown him down a shaft. He had died on the floor of a hangar with his son's hands on the mask, with the mask off, with air on his face for the first time in twenty-three years, and the last thing he had seen was the boy's face, and the boy's face had been like Padmé's.

"And then I woke up in a bunk in the Temple," he said, "with two hands. Twenty-two days ago. That's where I'm from."

She listened to it to the end. Then she said, "No."

"All right."

"That isn't a thing that happened. That's a thing you want to have happened." She heard the child in her voice and pressed on through it. "You know what I am. You know what I'd need to hear. You just said it to me in order, with the right names in it. It knew my name too. It said it in your voice."

"I know."

"Vader says what it needs to say."

"Yes," he said. "He did."

He did not insist. That was the thing she had not been ready for; she had been ready for him to insist. He sat with his back against the ice and his breath going up and let her not believe him, and after a while he said, "You've got the whole night. Ask me things."


"Mortis."

"The Father, the Son, the Daughter. A place that wasn't a place. You died." He said it without emphasis. "The Son touched you and you turned, and then you died. Obi-Wan and I carried you to the Daughter's altar. The Daughter was dying. She gave what was left of her into you through my hands, and you woke up and looked at me and said, 'Hey, Skyguy,' as if it had been an ordinary morning." He looked at the floor. "I've never told anyone that. There was no one to tell."

Her lips were cold. She made them move. "What did I say after?"

"You asked why I was crying. I said I wasn't."

She had asked him that. She remembered the altar and his face above her and the salt on it, and the lie.

"Rex."

"Captain. CT-seven thousand five hundred and sixty-seven. Blond, the hair cut short on the sides. Carried two pistols and a dead man's jaig eyes on his helmet. Said sir to me in a way that meant you idiot about half the time." A thin breath that might have been a laugh in someone warmer. "He'd have followed you into a sun. He did, more or less."

"What did he call me?"

"Commander. Kid, when he forgot."

"And you? What did you call me?"

"Snips." Only that. It went across the floor between them and lay down next to his saber.

She wanted to pick it up. She sat on her hands, which was a child's gesture, and she let it be one.

"Fives," she said.

He looked up at that. She had known he would; it was why she had kept it.

"You were gone," he said.

"I know I was gone. Tell me."

So he told her. A clone who had taken his own chip out of his head on Kamino, and gone to the Chancellor with what he had found, and been made to look mad there; who had been hunted across the undercity, and got a message to Rex, and Rex to him; who had been waiting in a warehouse on Level one thousand three hundred and twenty-five with Fox's men at the door, raving, in a way she would have recognised and he had not. Fives had said the chips and the Chancellor and the nightmares, and General Skywalker had told him to calm down, had believed none of it, and Fox had come through the door and shot him, and Fives had died with his head in Rex's lap and the last thing he said was that the nightmares were over. "That's how he died," he said. "I was two paces away. I thought he'd lost his mind. I told the Council he'd lost his mind. I told the Chancellor." He looked at his hands on his knees. "I told the Chancellor."

She had known that Fives was dead. Rex had told her, years after, in as few words as he could find, and she had not asked for more because his face had closed. She had not known the floor, or the lap, or the last words. It was the first thing he had given her that she had not already held. Everything else she had been able to lay against what she knew and check, and matching proved only that whatever sat across from her had Anakin's memories, which the thing on Malachor had also had. Fives she could not check. She sat on the ice with her hands under her and found that she had taken it, without deciding, while he was talking, the way you take a hand in the dark.

She did not tell him that.

"The chips," she said instead.

"Go on."

So she told him hers, because he had told her his. The Siege of Mandalore; Maul in a chair in the hold of a Venator, talking; the ship turning for Coruscant. Rex coming into the brig with his helmet on and his hands shaking, and reading her the order as if it were a thing in another man's mouth, and the pistol coming up, and find him, find Fives, forced out of him sideways through whatever the chip had left of him. The medical bay. Three droids and a scanner and her own two hands holding him down on the table while they cut the thing out of his head, and the whole ship trying to get in through the door. "It was a chip," she said. "In all of them. They didn't choose. Rex didn't choose. I've known for sixteen years that they didn't choose. I thought I was the only one who, "

"I know," he said.

She stopped.

"I knew," he said. "Not then. I found it out after. The whole of it: the order, the Kaminoans, where the chips were, who'd paid for them. I knew what had been in their heads when they turned round on the Temple steps and on every world. I knew it for twenty years." He did not look away from her while he said it. "I had the 501st under me on Mustafar. The same men. I knew they hadn't chosen and I used them anyway. Then I had the clones retired and the Empire took on men who'd volunteer. I knew about the chips the way I knew about everything. There's nothing you've carried for sixteen years that I didn't know, Ahsoka."

The cold had got into the places where she kept things.

She had thought it was hers. She had thought the chip was the one fact that she had and he had not, carried out of the war for the men who could not carry it themselves. And it had been in the mask the whole time. The thing at the top of the stairs had known the men who turned on her had had no choice, and had never said so, and had let the galaxy believe for twenty years that the clones had chosen it. Her list under the mattress had Rex's name on it. He would have been on no list of Vader's. He would have been retired.

"You're very quiet," he said.

"I'm thinking about what you knew."

"Yes," he said. "I thought you might."


The night went on. She got up once and walked the perimeter of the chamber to get the blood back into her feet, and he watched her and did not get up, and when she sat down again she sat, without choosing to, a little nearer. The saber lay where he had put it.

"Luke," she said. "You said Luke. Your son."

He did not answer at once.

"Padmé," she said. Her mouth did not want to make the name. "I knew. About you and her. Everyone with eyes knew. Rex knew." A breath. "She died. She died, I heard it on a transport two months after, from a man who'd read it on a screen. The war ended and the Senator from Naboo died, and no one said what of." The crystal was in her hand again. She did not remember moving it. "I didn't know she'd been carrying."

"Twins," he said.

Something happened in her chest.

"A boy and a girl. She didn't die on Coruscant. She died somewhere else. I found out where, years later. I went and stood in the room. I was told she died and the child with her, and I believed it for nineteen years, because he told me. Then a boy from Tatooine put a torpedo into the Death Star." He said the word as if it belonged to someone else. "Skywalker. Luke Skywalker. Obi-Wan had put him with Owen Lars on the farm. Obi-Wan sat in the desert for nineteen years and watched him from a ridge." A pause in which she could hear the cold. "And Leia. Bail Organa took the girl. Princess of Alderaan. I had her in a cell on the Death Star and I didn't know. I stood over her and I didn't know. She's got Padmé's face, Ahsoka. She stood in front of me in a cell and lied to me with Padmé's face, and I didn't, "

She did not hear the rest. She had put her hand over her mouth.

Padmé's children had lived.

She had carried the Senator for sixteen years as a closed door, a woman who had been kind to her at fourteen and had died with the Republic, and she had never once let herself think the word child, because if there had been one it would have died too, with everything else, in the first year. Dead was dead. And the whole time there had been a boy on a moisture farm learning to fly, and a girl in a palace learning to lie, and Obi-Wan on a ridge, and she had been Fulcrum, she had passed within a parsec of Alderaan a dozen times, and nobody had told her, and she had never known to ask.

Her eyes were full. She felt it happen with a kind of distant surprise, as if it were happening to a child a long way off, and then it was not far off at all; it was her, on the ice, ten years old, with a crystal in her fist, and the thing she had kept behind the wall for seven years had come out through the front of her face and she could not get it back in. She had cried on schedule for seven years, at the pitch and length a child of that age should, and watched herself do it from a long way back and felt nothing. This was the other thing. She had not done this since before Malachor; she could not remember when. It came out of her in the voice she hated and she could not stop it and she did not try.

He did not touch her. He sat where he was with his hands on his knees and let her, and that was right; that was the only right thing he could have done, and she hated that he knew it. The chamber took the sound and gave it back off the ice, small and high.

Then he got up. She heard him cross the floor, past the saber, and felt him sit down next to her, close, the cold off the tunic and the warmth under it, and his arm came round her shoulders, a boy's arm, thin at the wrist, and she turned her face into the sand-coloured cloth at his ribs and that was the end of her for a while. He smelled of the ship and of cold. He was fifteen years old and all bone. She was ten and she fitted under his arm the way she had never fitted at fourteen, and somewhere in the part of her that was still working she thought, this is not forgiveness, this is only the cold, and she let herself have it anyway, because the night was long and she had been alone for seven years and there was no one else in the galaxy who knew the names.


When she could speak she said, into his ribs, "Her face."

"Padmé's. And Luke's got mine. The old one. Before."

"You said the mask came off."

"Yes."

"I'm still not sure I believe that."

"I know."

She sat up. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, both sides, the way a child did, and he looked away while she did it, which was the first gentle thing he had done all night that she could not have predicted. She was shaking. It was the cold; some of it was the cold.

"Rules," she said. Her voice came back hoarse and small and she made it work. "We need rules."

"Go on."

"Nobody knows. Not the Council, not Master Plo, not Master Kenobi. No one. Not for anything."

"Agreed."

"If they ask what happened in here, the door froze and we waited and I was frightened. I'll be frightened. I'm good at it."

"I've seen."

"You stay a Padawan who's good with a saber. I stay a youngling who's intense." She looked at him. "That stroke. In Drallig's hall. Stop doing that."

"I'm doing penance for it every morning."

"Do more."

The corner of his mouth went. It was not a smile. "Anything else?"

"Obi-Wan." She said the name and watched his face and saw it close, not a wall, only a door pulled to. "Is he back?"

"I don't know."

"You've been living with him for three weeks."

"He's different." Carefully. "He's warmer. He says my friend to me. He never did that; not at this age. He let me write to my mother. He won't leave me alone with the Chancellor. He looks at me as if he's counting." He turned his hands over. "It could be a different Obi-Wan. It could be my memory. I had twenty-three years in a suit to get him wrong in."

"He isn't back," she said. "I'd know."

"How?"

She opened her mouth and nothing was in it. It was true; she would know; she had known it with her whole body before he asked and had not the faintest idea why. Master Kenobi had looked at her once, across a hall, with his eyes older than his face, and she had felt him flinch from her, the way she flinched from the Pau'an on the Guard, and she had put it down to a child's imagination.

"I don't know how," she said. "I'd know."

"There's an Archive log."

He told her. Two years ago, twenty-eight B B Y, terminal four, a search under Kenobi's own name for a planet that did not exist in the records: forty minutes on it, a null return, no reason given. Four days later, from the same terminal, Sifo-Dyas, eleven minutes, public record only. He had found it by slicing a dead librarian's account; Jocasta Nu had found him finding it.

"Kamino," she said.

"Yes."

"He searched for Kamino. Two years ago."

"Forty minutes."

The Jedi did not know Kamino existed. She knew that; she had read the whole wretched business after the war in a prison transcript, Dooku, the erasure, Sifo-Dyas's codes; the entry had been gone from the Archives since before this Obi-Wan was knighted. No Knight of thirty-one could have had that name. Nobody in this Temple could have had it. Not until a tall man with a lightsaber dart walked into Dex's and asked.

She went quiet. She felt herself go, from the inside, like a room being switched off, and she did not come back out of it, and he let her stay.

"I don't know what it means," he said eventually.

"Neither do I."

"We could ask him."

"No."

"No," he agreed. "We couldn't."


Towards the end of the night he said, "We'll need a bond. A thin one. If we're going to do this."

"A Padawan bonded to an initiate would show."

"So thin it doesn't. You know how."

"You taught me how." She heard herself say it, and the ice gave it back. "Not now. After. On the ship. Not here. It may have to wait until the Temple, though; Yoda will be aboard."

"After," he said.

They sat. He had moved back to the far wall when she had stopped shaking, without being asked; she had let him go and not said anything, and he had understood that too. The saber lay where it had been all night. She had not touched it and neither had he.

There was a thing she wanted to say and she turned it over for an hour and did not. It was the name. He had given her hers and she had not given him his, and she knew what it would do to his face, and she was not ready to be the person who did that; the thing at the top of the stairs still stood in the ice behind her eyes with its red blade, and she still did not know what had got into a bunk in the Temple twenty-two days ago with two hands. She knew that it had known Fives's last words. She knew that it had let her cry. She knew that it had not insisted.

She had been sure, for sixteen years, that Anakin Skywalker was gone for good. She was less sure than she had been at sunset. That was all. It was a long way from the thing she could see he wanted, and she would not pretend it was nearer.

She said it once. Not aloud, at first; and then, because the light in the walls had begun to change, and because she was ten and cold and her face was stiff with salt and she would be a child again in an hour and might not have the nerve, aloud, very quietly, across the floor.

"Skyguy."

He put his head back against the ice and shut his eyes, and did not answer, and she did not need him to.


The door went the way it had come: not all at once. First the blue in the walls thinned to grey, and then the grey had a line of white in it, low down, where the ice met the floor, and then the sound, which she had forgotten, a long creaking tick like a hull in atmosphere, and the whole face of the door began to sweat. Water ran down it in sheets. The ice went to glass and the glass went to nothing, and the sun of Ilum came in low over the snow and hit her in the eyes, and she put her hand up and the green of the crystal went through her fingers again, brighter now, with the real light behind it.

She had not thought to look at it all night. She looked at it now: a shard the length of her smallest finger, green, with a flaw in it like a thread. Green. It had been green the last time too. She did not know what she had expected. She closed her hand on it.

He picked up his saber on the way to the door and hung it on his belt without looking at it, and that was a thing she filed as well.

They came out through the arch together, the Padawan a step ahead and the youngling behind him with her hand in her sleeve, which was how it had to look, and the cold outside was a different cold, with wind in it. The snow went off white to the horizon. Across the open ground in front of the temple the Crucible sat on its struts with its ramp down, and between the ship and the arch, in a line, in their sand-coloured coats, stood five children, and a tall bronze droid, and in front of them, small, in a hood, with both hands on his stick, Master Yoda.

Kessa was crying. Ahsoka saw that first, because it was the thing she had learned to see first. Tam Dorreth had his arm round her, which he had never done before and would be unbearable about later. Brannick was looking at the Padawan, not at her, and Ilyo was looking at the ground.

She did what she had learned to do. She let her face go, let the lip tremble, let the night and the cold come up through the small body and show, and it was not even hard, because there was so much in her this morning that was true that a little of it got out without her choosing which. She ran the last part, to Kessa, who grabbed her, and over Kessa's shoulder she watched Yoda watch the boy.

He had stopped a few paces short of the Master, hands at his sides. He looked, with the night on him, exactly fifteen: grey under the eyes, hair flattened where he had leaned against the ice, the wrists out of the tunic, a boy who had sat up all night in a cave with a frightened child. He had made that face. She had watched him build it, in the last minute before the door opened, the way you watched a man put on a coat.

Yoda's ears were down. He looked at the boy for a long time, in that way he had, which she had been afraid of at fourteen and was afraid of now in a different way, because she knew what it had been looking for in all the years after.

"Found her, you did," Yoda said.

The boy inclined his head. The braid swung. "She was in the deep chamber, Master. She'd lost the light. We waited for the door."

A pause. Snow came off the roof of the temple behind them in a soft fall and nobody turned to look at it.

Yoda's eyes moved. They came off the boy and crossed the open ground to where Ahsoka stood in Kessa's arms with her face wet and her hand in her sleeve closed on a green stone, and they rested on her, and she gave him the child, all of the child, every lesson of seven years, and felt him look through it the way you look through a window at weather.

"Found you, she did," he said.

He did not say it to her. He did not say it to the boy either. He said it, she thought, to the air between them, where the thin thing they had agreed to make on the ship did not yet exist, and he turned, and put his stick down in the snow, and went towards the ramp.

"Come," he said. "Cold, it is. Crystals, you have. Build, you will."

They went, the children in a straggle behind him and the droid behind the children, and the Padawan last, and when Ahsoka looked back from the top of the ramp he was standing in the snow with his hands in his sleeves, looking up at the sky over the ice, at the place where a bird might have been if there had been a bird.

Her crystal was green. She held it so hard it hurt, all the way up.

Part One. The Returned


Chapter One. The Weight of Breath.


The deck was tilting. That was the first thing Anakin understood and the last thing that made any sense: that the floor of the hangar bay was going over under him like the deck of a sea-ship, and that it was not the floor at all but the suit, the whole black tonnage of him, listing in the boy's arms while the station shook itself to pieces around them.

He had never known how heavy he was. He had carried it for twenty-three years and never once felt it, because the servos did the carrying; now the servos were dying one by one down the length of his legs and the weight came back to him joint by joint, as if his body were being handed to him in instalments. His son had him under the arms. His son was dragging him. The boy's boots skidded on the plating, found purchase, skidded again. Somewhere behind them a conduit let go with a sound like tearing cloth and the air went hot and then cold.

The respirator was failing. He heard it before he felt it: the in-breath coming a half-beat late, the out-breath ragged at the end, the machine in his chest hunting for a rhythm it could no longer find. Lightning had done that. It had gone through the suit's circuitry the way water goes through sand, and the lights on the chest-plate were guttering green, amber, nothing. Each breath was now a thing the machine did badly for him, and he could feel, under the doing, the old ruined lungs trying to help and not being able to.

Luke got him to the foot of the ramp and could get him no further. The boy knelt. His face was very close, and it was still red; everything was still red, the red of the lenses, twenty-three years of a world the colour of a wound.

Anakin asked for the mask.

He did not have the breath to say it well. He said it in pieces, and the boy argued, as he had known the boy would argue, because the boy was Padmé's and had her way of standing in front of a thing that was going to happen and telling it no. And then the boy stopped arguing, because he was also Anakin's, and understood a lost cause when he was holding one.

The seals went one at a time. There was a pressure in the ears, a sound like a held breath let out, and then the boy lifted the helmet away and the air of the hangar came onto his face.

It was cold. That was all, at first. He had forgotten cold as a thing that could touch skin. It smelled of ozone and burnt insulation and the sweat of the boy who was holding him, and it moved; air moved; he had not felt air move on his face since a river of fire. His eyes watered. He thought it was the air and then knew it was not.

And there was the boy's face. Not red. Not through a lens. The colour of it, the plain colour of a human face in bad light, tired and dirty and twenty-three years old, with his mother's mouth and a cut above one eyebrow that had bled and dried. Blue eyes. He had not known the boy's eyes were blue. He had seen them through the mask a dozen times, across the carbon-freezing chamber, across a gantry at Bespin, across the throne room an hour ago, and the mask had made them black. They were blue. They were wet. The boy was looking at him as though he were something worth looking at.

He had so much to say and no breath to say it with. He had the boy's hand; he had taken that hand once, on a gantry, with a blade. He tried to say that he knew that. He tried to say the boy's mother's name. What came out was smaller and truer, and it was all he could carry.

"You were right about me," he said. "Tell your sister... you were right."

The boy said something. Anakin did not hear it. The hangar was going white at the edges, and the white was not the lights, which were failing, and not the fires, which were orange. It came from no direction. It came up through the deck and down through the ruined ceiling and in through the boy's hand, and it was warm, and it did not hurt, and for the first time since Mustafar he was not afraid of anything.

He took a breath. The machine did not help him with it. It was his own.

He let it out.


He was breathing.

In, and out. The in-breath and the out-breath, and nothing doing them for him.

For a long time that was the only fact. He lay in the dark and listened for the machine and the machine did not come. There was no hiss at the top of the breath, no click at the bottom, no faint whine of the pump that had been the floor of every silence he had known since he was twenty-three. There was only air going into him and air coming out, and the sound it made was a soft, wet, animal sound, the sound a sleeping creature makes, and it was coming from his own mouth.

Something was wrong with the respirator. He reached for the control on his chest-plate.

His fingers closed on cloth.

Cloth, warm, a little damp. Under the cloth a chest that rose and fell. Under the chest, a heart going fast. He pressed his hand flat against it and felt the ribs, each one, and felt the hand doing the pressing, and lay very still.

Fingers. He had fingers. He lifted the hand in the dark and could not see it, but he could feel it: the five of them, the joints, the small pull of tendon when he closed them. He closed them. He opened them. He brought the hand to his face and touched his mouth, and it was a mouth, lips, dry; and above it a nose, and the skin of a cheek, and no seam anywhere, no edge where the mask bit, no ridge of grafted hide. He touched his own eyelids. They were soft. He had not touched his own face in twenty-three years and he did it now the way a blind man reads, slowly, by the fingertips, and when he came to the hair at his temple it was hair, short and warm and sticking up with sleep.

The other hand. He was afraid of the other hand, and he lifted it anyway, and it was a hand.

He lay with both hands held up in the dark above his face, as a child does, and the breath went in and out of him.

He thought: I am dead. This is what comes after. He had been promised nothing and had expected less, and if what came after was a dark room and two hands and no pain, then it was more than he had any right to, and he would lie here and be grateful and not ask.

But a dead man did not need to piss. His mouth was dry in the specific way of a mouth that has slept with its lips parted, and there was an itch at the back of his neck where something fine and plaited lay against the skin, and the body he was in had a bladder and a dry tongue and an itch, and the body was not negotiable.

He put a hand to the back of his neck and found the braid.

Thin. Tight-plaited. Running from behind the right ear down over the collarbone, with a bead in it that his fingers knew before he did. He held it. He had cut it off himself once, in a Council chamber, and handed it to Obi-Wan; and before that he had worn it for ten years and hated it for most of them and pulled at it when he was thinking, exactly as his fingers were pulling at it now.

The breath went in. The breath went out.

He made himself do the rest of the catalogue, because he had been a soldier and a soldier inventories the body before he moves it. Legs: he sent the thought down and the legs were there. Two of them, long, lying under a thin blanket, and when he told the toes to move the toes moved, and he felt the blanket against the skin of his shins. Skin. The shins had skin. He put a hand down under the blanket and ran it along one thigh and it was a thigh, bony, a little hairy, cool at the knee, and nowhere on it was there a port or a socket or a ridge of scar. He ran the hand up over the belly, which was flat and hollow with hunger, over the ribs, over the chest again, and nowhere was there the hard plastoid shell, and nowhere was there a tube.

Lungs. He drew the deepest breath he could, down to the bottom, past the place where for twenty-three years the breath had stopped against a wall of ruined tissue. There was no wall. The air went all the way down and the ribs spread to take it and nothing tore, and he held it, and let it go, and the sound it made going was a sob, and he let that go too.

There was no pain.

That was a sensation in itself. He had not known it could be. For twenty-three years pain had been the room he lived in: the burns that never finished healing under the suit, the grind of the prosthetics in the stumps, the ache in the chest from the pump, the headaches from the lenses; a constant low noise, so constant that he had stopped hearing it the way one stops hearing a ventilator. Now the noise had stopped, and the silence where it had been was so loud that he lay rigid in it, listening, waiting for it to come back.

It did not come back.

He turned his head. The pillow was cool against his cheek, which was a thing a pillow had not been for a very long time, and there was a smell to it, and the smell undid him more than the hands had.

Stone. Old, dry, faintly warm stone, the kind that has been breathed on by ten thousand years of people sitting still. Under it the recycled air of a vast building, with its mineral tang, and under that, so faint he would not have been sure of it if he had not known it with his whole childhood, a green note, damp, of growing things and moving water, carried through a kilometre of ductwork from the Room of a Thousand Fountains. Incense, days old, soaked into the plaster. Starch from a tunic folded over a chair. And his own sweat, which was the sweat of a boy, sharp and thin and nothing like the sweat that had cooked inside the suit.

The Jedi Temple.

He had burned it. He had walked in through the front gate with a legion at his back and gone up the Processional Way with his blade already lit, and the stone had smelled of this, and then of smoke, and then of other things. He knew exactly what this building smelled like when it was dying. It was not dying now. It was asleep, and he was in it, and he was in a bed.

The bed was narrow. He stretched and his feet found the end of it and his head the other, and that was the measure of him: he fitted. There was a ceiling close above, a slit of window to his left with the city's light coming through it in a dull orange bar, and in the bar of light he could just make out a chest, a chair, a tunic over the chair, the shape of a datapad on a shelf. Two small rooms off a common room. The Padawan's room was the one nearer the door.

He knew where he was now. He did not yet dare to know when.


He felt Obi-Wan before he thought of him.

It was at the back of his mind, where it had always been, where there had been nothing for so long that he had forgotten the shape of the absence: a presence, a mood, a weather. Asleep. Thirty paces away, through two walls, lying on his side as he always lay, dreaming something mild and orderly. There was no thought in it. There never had been; it had never carried words. It carried warmth, the way a wall carries the warmth of a room on the other side, and it was carrying warmth now, into the dark, into him, the way it had every night of the ten years he had been a boy in this building, so that he had never once in those years lain awake and been alone.

Obi-Wan.

Alive, and asleep, and thirty paces away.

The last time he had felt him through anything was on a bank of black rock, with the river roaring, when the bond between them had not broken so much as burned, and in the burning he had felt Obi-Wan feel it, had felt the exact moment Obi-Wan turned away. After that, nothing, for nineteen years. Then one flicker, on a corridor of a station the size of a moon, as an old man lowered his blade and smiled at him and went out like a lamp.

Now this. Warmth. Sleep. A man dreaming of nothing in particular, thirty paces off, who did not know he was being felt.

Anakin slammed the shield down.

He did not decide to. The decision would have taken a thought, and there was no thought; there was only the reflex of twenty-three years, the thing Sidious had taught him first and best, before the saber work, before the choke. Close the mind. Close it the way a blast door closes, all at once, from the top, and let nothing through, not a mood, not a colour, not the fact of being awake; because anything that got through was a thing the Master could use. It came down now as it had come down ten thousand times, black and total, a wall of old stone with the scars of old sieges on it, and the warmth at the back of his mind stopped as though a hand had been laid across a candle.

He lay in the silence he had made.

Then he understood what he had done, and his whole body went cold.

Obi-Wan would have felt that. Even asleep. Especially asleep, when a master's mind lies open to the bond because the bond is how a master knows his Padawan has not fallen out of a window in the night. For six years that bond had been the one thing in the Temple that Anakin had never been able to hide from, and he had just shut it in the man's face. Not thinned. Not dimmed, as a boy does when he is sulking or hiding a cut lip. Shut. With a discipline that did not exist in this building, that no one in this building had ever felt, that any Jedi who had ever stood near a Sith would know in his teeth for what it was.

The first thing he had done in this body was give himself away.

He made himself breathe. In; out. The wall held. He could not take it down now; taking it down would be worse, would be a boy's mind with a man's grief in it spilling out across the Temple at the hour when the younglings slept lightest. He had to leave it up and pay for it. He had been paying for things his whole life. He would find out what this one cost in the morning.

He got up.

The legs did not want him. He had forgotten that a body has to be asked: that one does not simply decide to stand, with a flesh body, but negotiates it, and that the knees have opinions. These knees were a fifteen-year-old's. They had grown four centimetres in a year and had not yet been told, and when he put his weight on them they wobbled, and he put out a hand to the wall and found it closer than it should have been because his arm was shorter than the arm he had reached with. He stood against the wall until the floor agreed to be a floor. The flagstones were cold under his bare feet. He had forgotten feet. He had forgotten cold under them, the specific cold of stone at the fourth hour, and he stood on it and let it hurt him in that small clean way, because it was the first thing since the white light that had hurt at all.

Three steps to the shelf. He caught his shin on the corner of the chest, hard, and swore in Huttese in a voice that cracked on the second syllable.

He stopped.

That voice. High, and then not, and then high again: a voice in the middle of changing, a voice that had not finished deciding what it was. He had not heard it in thirty years. He had forgotten he had ever had it. He put his hand over his mouth, as though he could catch the sound and put it back, and stood there in the dark with a boy's hand over a boy's mouth and his shin throbbing, and did not laugh, and did not weep, and did neither for long enough that he could move again.

The datapad on the shelf woke when he touched it. Its glow was blue and small and showed him a chrono in the corner, and he looked at the chrono for a long time before he let himself read it.

Then he read it.

He read the year. He read it again. He did the arithmetic that a man does when he has been told the date and cannot make it fit, counting on from the only fixed point he had, which was the morning of Geonosis, which he would never not be able to find; and the count came out the same every time he did it, and it was four years.

Four years before the war.

He put the datapad down with a care that was almost violence, because if he did not put it down carefully he would throw it, and went to the window.

Coruscant. The slit gave him a narrow slice of it, a vertical strip of the city from the Temple's flank, and he put his face to the transparisteel and looked. The Senate dome, lit from beneath, pale and swollen. The lanes of traffic above it, moving at the thinned pace of the fourth hour. The towers of five hundred Republica, with their rich windows dark. He looked for the things he knew and they were not there: no orbital batteries picking out the sky in a lattice; no grey wedge of a warship parked above the Senate like a thumb over a mouth; no searchlights. The air above the city was clear of everything but weather and traffic and the ordinary haze of fifty billion people breathing. It was a city before. It did not know yet. Nothing in it knew.

Four years.

The Chancellor was in his office, six years into his term, with a window that looked across at this spire, and he had been in this boy's head every tenth day for six years and would expect to be in it again. Dooku was at Serenno with a curved blade in a drawer. On Kamino, under the rain, Rex was six years old and looked twelve, and had a thing the size of a grain of sand behind his eye. His mother was alive. His mother was alive on Tatooine, under a different name, with a letter to him half-written on a kitchen table, and would be alive for four more years.

Everyone was alive. Everyone in this building, every one of them, was asleep and alive, and he had killed them, and they did not know.

He stood with his forehead against the cold glass and his breath fogging it and his hands, both of them, flat against the wall on either side.


The knock, when it came, was soft. Two knuckles, twice.

He had heard the man wake. He had not been able to help it; even through the wall he had built, there was the small physical sound of a body turning over in the next room, the pause, the bare feet on stone. He had heard him stop outside the door and stand there, and he had known what the man was doing, because he had done it himself a thousand times outside a hundred doors: deciding whether to be the kind of person who knocks.

"Anakin?"

He turned from the window. He did not trust himself to speak yet. He said, "Yes," and it came out in the boy's voice, and it was enough.

The door slid back. The light from the common room was low, one lamp left burning as it was always left burning, and the man in the doorway was backlit, and then he stepped in and was not.

Obi-Wan Kenobi. Thirty-one years old. His hair flattened on the side he had slept on, his beard short and uncombed, his sleep-tunic creased, one hand still on the frame of the door. His face without the lines the war would cut into it. His eyes without the thing that would be in them on the bank of the river, that would still be in them nineteen years later in a corridor of a battle station, that Anakin had carried like a stone in the chest and would have given everything he had, twice over, to have never seen. There was none of it. The man had not seen anything yet. He was simply tired, and a little rumpled, and concerned, in the ordinary way of a man who has been woken at the fourth hour by a feeling he could not name.

"Nightmare?" Obi-Wan said.

The word was gentle. It was offered the way one offers a hand to someone who has stumbled: not insisting, not grabbing, just there. He said it as though he had said it before, in this doorway, more than once, and as though the answer had sometimes been yes.

Anakin looked at him. He had the boy's body between himself and the man, and he was grateful for it, because it meant Obi-Wan was looking at a narrow fifteen-year-old in a sleep-tunic with his hair on end and his shin bruised, and not at what was behind the eyes.

He could have said: I died. I died an hour ago, in my son's arms, and I had just stopped being the thing I was, and I was so glad, Master, I was so glad, and then I woke up here and you were asleep and I could feel you, and I shut you out because the Emperor taught me to, because I spent twenty-three years as his dog, and I have killed every person in this building, and I have killed you, and I am sorry, and I do not know why I am here.

He said, "Yes, Master."

Obi-Wan's hand stayed on the doorframe.

"You shut the door rather hard," he said. Mildly. He did not say the bond; he had never liked to speak of it, it had always embarrassed him a little, as though it were a form of eavesdropping. "I thought you'd fallen off something."

"I'm all right."

"Mm." The man looked at him. It was a longer look than Anakin had expected. It went over his face and down to his hands, which he realised he was holding clasped in front of him, the way a soldier holds his hands when he has nothing to do with them, and he unclasped them and did not know what to do with them instead. Obi-Wan's eyes came back to his face. "Do you want to tell me about it?"

"No." Too fast. He heard it. He softened it, in the boy's voice, with a boy's shrug. "I don't really remember it. It's gone. It was just... I woke up and I didn't know where I was. For a second."

That part, at least, was true.

And here was the thing he had not reckoned on, standing at the window doing his arithmetic: that the man would believe him. That Obi-Wan would take the lie the way he had taken a hundred lies from a boy in this room, with a slight tilt of the head and a slight narrowing of the eyes and then a nod, because the boy had the right to his own nightmares and a master who pried would get less, not more. He saw Obi-Wan decide to believe him. He saw it cost the man nothing. It was small; it was the smallest lie he had ever told anyone; he had told lies that burned planets. And it went into him like a splinter and he knew at once that he would be feeling it for days, for the simple reason that the last time Obi-Wan had asked him a question and he had answered, he had answered with a blade, and this time the man had asked him gently in a doorway at the fourth hour, and he had lied to him again, and the man had said all right.

"All right," Obi-Wan said.

He did not go. He stood a moment longer with his hand on the frame, and the look he gave the room was, Anakin thought, a strange one for a man to give a Padawan's room at night: a look that took in the window, and the datapad, still faintly lit on the shelf, and the chest, and the boy, and seemed to be counting them. Then it was gone, and the man was only tired.

"There's tea in the pot if you want it. It'll be stewed." A pause. "I'm just there. You know that."

"I know, Master."

"Try to sleep, Anakin."

The door slid to. The feet went away across the common room. The pause, the turning-over, the settling. And then, through the wall, through the black wall he had built, nothing: the man lying awake, as Anakin was lying awake, and neither of them able to feel the other. For the first time in six years. He had done that.

He sat down on the floor with his back against the bed.


He did not sleep. He had not expected to. He sat on the cold flagstones with his knees up and his arms around them, folded small as the body wanted to be folded, and watched the bar of light from the window crawl across the floor as the city turned, and thought.

He thought first about whether it was a trick.

It would be like the Master. It would be exactly like him: to let the boy die, and let him think he had died well, and then to put him here, in the one place and the one time where he could be made to do it all again, and to sit in his office across the city and watch. There were old stories about Sith who had done such things. Anakin had read them, in the years when he had read everything, in the archives that the Emperor had opened to him as one opens a cage to a trained animal. None of the stories had been true. He did not think this one was either. He had felt Sidious go into the reactor shaft. He had felt him go the way one feels a tooth go when it is pulled: a wrench, a hole, and then an absence that the tongue keeps finding. The absence was still there. He put his mind to it carefully, like a tongue, and it was still a hole.

So: not the Master. Or not in any way he could see, which with the Master had never meant much.

Something else, then. He did not know what. He had been offered no explanation and expected none. The Force did not explain itself; it had never explained itself to him, not on Mortis, not on the lava bank, not in the throne room. It had shown him a thing and let him choose. If it had shown him this, then it was showing him something and he did not yet know what, and until he knew he would be a fool to act.

So he would not act.

He turned it over, because it went against everything in him; it always had. He wanted to go now, tonight, barefoot, across the city to the Senate Office Building and up the lift and in through the red door and put his hands around the old man's throat while he slept, and he could do it, he knew exactly how it was done, where the guards stood and which lift the staff used and what the man's mind felt like when it was asleep. He wanted to go to the Archives and find the hole where Kamino had been and shout about it until the Council listened. He wanted to go to Obi-Wan's room, thirty paces away, and wake him, and tell him everything, and be held.

He could not do any of it. He went through each one, sitting on the floor, and took it apart.

The old man was not asleep; the old man was never asleep, and a fifteen-year-old who went across the city at night and died in a red office would change nothing except the date of the next boy's apprenticeship. The Council would listen to a Padawan who talked of an army on a planet that was not in the Archives for exactly as long as it took to call a healer. And Obi-Wan would believe him. That was the worst of it. Obi-Wan would believe him, because Obi-Wan loved him and did not yet have any reason not to, and then Obi-Wan would look at him and know what he was. He would get the look. He would get it ten years early, from a man who had not yet earned the right to give it, and he found that he could survive almost anything in this body, the breathing and the hands and the smell of the stone, except that.

Hide, then. All of it. The shield would stay up, thinned to something that could pass for a boy's sulk, and he would learn by morning how to hold it at that thickness, because he had held it at every thickness for twenty-three years for an audience that forgave nothing. The body would learn to fight like a boy. The mouth would learn to say Master and mean the right man. He would go to his classes and stand in a line of children and hold a training saber that did not weigh enough and let them hit him with it. He would sit in the Chancellor's office every tenth day and let that hand touch his mind and give it a boy to touch. He had been a mask for twenty-three years. He could be a face.

Change nothing. Not yet. Not until he understood why he was here, and whether he was alone.

That was the thought he came to last, as the bar of light reached the far wall and began to pale, and it was the one that sat with him longest. He did not know why it came. Perhaps it was the look Obi-Wan had given the room. Perhaps it was only that a man who has been a monster learns to assume that where there is one, there are others. He sat with it and could not make it into anything. He was here, alone, in a boy's body, in a sleeping building, and no one in the galaxy knew what he was.

He had just got that far when he felt it.

Far off. Not in the room; not in the quarters; not, he thought, on this level of the Temple at all, but somewhere below him and to the east, through many floors of stone. Something rang.

It was not a sound. It was the thing a sound is before it reaches the ear: a struck bell, a single note, clear and sudden and enormous for its size, as though a very small thing had been hit very hard. It went through the Force the way a bell's note goes through water. It had no words in it and no direction and it was not aimed at him; it was, he thought, not aimed at anything. It was simply the sound something makes when it wakes all at once.

Then it was gone, and the building was asleep again, and the grey of the window had become the grey of dawn.

He sat very still, with his arms around his knees, and did not know what it was. He thought it might be the Temple itself, which was old and had heard things. He thought it might be himself: some echo of his own arrival, going round the walls of this place and coming back to him. He thought, for one cold moment, that it might be the Master, somewhere across the city, opening his eyes.

He did not think it was a child.

He had no reason to. He was fifteen, and alone, and dead, and the morning was coming, and somewhere below him in the dark a bell had been struck, and he put it away with everything else, in the place where he kept the things he would have to understand later, and got up off the floor to find his boots.

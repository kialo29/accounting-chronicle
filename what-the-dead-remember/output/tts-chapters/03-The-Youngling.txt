Chapter Three. The Youngling.


Ahsoka Tano had been awake since the bell.

Not the Temple's bell; the Temple had no bell, only the chimes that went through the dormitories at the sixth hour like someone running a fingernail along a comb. This had been the other kind. It had come up through the floor and the bunk frame and the thin mattress and the ten-year-old body lying on it, somewhere past the middle of the night, and it had rung in the Force like a struck bell, one note, enormous, and gone. She had sat up with her heart going so hard she could feel it in her montrals. She had put her hand flat against the wall, because the wall was cold and real. She had not got out of the bunk.

That was the discipline. You did not get out of the bunk.

Around her, nineteen children slept. Hawkbat Clan: twenty bunks in two rows, a door at each end, a night-glow strip along the skirting that made everything the colour of weak tea. Tam Dorreth snored. Ilyo breathed through her mouth with a tiny click. Kessa Ruun, in the next bunk, had thrown an arm over her face the way she always did, as if sleep were a bright light. None of them had stirred. Whatever it was had not been loud; it had been deep, the way a thing is deep when it is very far down and very large.

Above and to the west, she thought. The Padawan floors.

She lay down again and put her hands on her stomach and counted her breaths, which was a thing a youngling was taught at four and most of them had forgotten by six. She did not forget things. She counted to a hundred and started again, and her heart did not slow, and somewhere around the third hundred she stopped pretending that it would.

The note had been gone a long time by then. What stayed was the shape it had left behind, like a dent in a cushion where someone has sat. She lay and looked at the shape and did not name it. There was a feeling that went with it, and she did not name that either, because the feeling was far too big for a girl in a bunk in a dormitory, and if she let it have a name it would have her.

Her feet did not reach the end of the mattress. She noticed that, lying there: the gap of cold sheet past her toes. She had noticed it most nights for seven years and it had never once stopped being strange.

Under the mattress, between the slats and the foam, was a strip of flimsi folded in four. She did not reach for it. She knew what was on it; she had written it.

When the chimes came she sat up with the rest of them and rubbed her eyes with her fists, which was what a child did.


"You look awful," said Kessa, which was what Kessa said most mornings and meant kindly.

"Didn't sleep."

"Bad dream?"

"No." Ahsoka pulled her tunic over her head. It was the sand-coloured one every initiate wore, belted brown, and it had fitted her at the start of the year and did not now; the hem came to the middle of her thighs and the sleeves stopped short of her wrists by the width of two fingers. The quartermaster droid issued new ones at the turn of each quarter, and the turn was still nine days off, and in the meantime she walked about the Temple with her wrists showing like a girl who had been let out of somewhere. "Just awake."

"You're always just awake." Kessa was Mirialan, green-skinned, the black diamonds on her cheeks not yet complete; she was ten, and loud in the mornings, and she had decided two years ago that Ahsoka was her friend, and Ahsoka had let her. It was easier with a friend. A girl alone was watched; a girl with a friend was a pair, and a pair was ordinary. "Sit. Your left one's twisted."

Ahsoka sat on the edge of the bunk and let Kessa straighten the silka bead on the end of her left lek, which had turned in the night. Kessa's fingers were quick and careless. Ahsoka kept her face still. She had learned, early, that she did not like to be touched there by anyone she did not trust, and that a child who flinched from her clanmates was a child who went into a file. So she did not flinch. She looked at the wall above Tam Dorreth's bunk, where someone years ago had scratched a hawkbat into the plaster with a stylus, and she thought about nothing.

"There," Kessa said. "Now you're only half awful."

"Thanks."

"Saber day."

"I know."

"Drallig'll have the Padawan again. The Skywalker one."

"I know," Ahsoka said, and bent to do her boots, so that she was looking at the floor.


The Bear Clan boy was waiting in the corridor outside the refectory, as he was most mornings, standing against the wall with his hands behind his back the way the very small ones stood when they had been told not to run.

Caleb Dume was seven. He was too thin for his tunic the way she was too big for hers, and he had a cowlick that no amount of water would flatten, and a way of looking up at her that she could not bear and could not stop looking at. He had attached himself to her the previous winter, for no reason either of them could have given; he had simply started being wherever she was, a few paces behind, like a thing she had dropped. The clan-masters thought it was sweet. Kessa thought it was funny. Ahsoka thought nothing about it at all, very carefully, every single day.

"Hawkbat has sabers," he informed her. He knew all the timetables. "Bear has Master Yoda."

"Lucky Bear."

"He makes us sit still."

"That's the point of him."

"Is the Padawan coming? The tall one?"

"I don't know," she said, which was a lie, and put her hand on the top of his head to steer him round a pillar he had not seen, because he was looking up at her and not where he was going. His hair was warm. She took her hand away a half-second later than she should have and felt the half-second go through her like a draught through a door.

He walked beside her to the refectory, talking about Master Yoda's stick. She let him. In the doorway she stopped, and he bumped into the back of her legs, and she stood for a moment looking at the long room: the long tables, the sand-coloured ranks of younglings with their bowls, three Knights at the far end with their tea, the tall windows going grey with the morning. She looked at all of it. She did that in doorways. Then she went in and got her bowl and sat beside Kessa and ate, and when a girl from the clan above them, Mirialan too, older, quiet, with an earnest braid down her back, passed behind their bench, Ahsoka looked into her porridge until she had gone by.

"Was that Offee?" Kessa said. "She's going to Ilum next quarter, I heard."

"Probably," said Ahsoka, and ate.


Master Plo found her in the colonnade before the class, as he did perhaps once a tenday, and she let him see her light up because it was not a performance. It was the only thing in the Temple that was not.

"Little 'Soka."

"Master Plo."

He was very tall. Everyone was very tall, but Master Plo was tall in a way that made her feel small on purpose, and she liked it; it was like standing in the shade. His breath mask hissed softly. His tusks, behind it, made the shape of what passed for a smile. He had found her, once, on a world she did not remember and did remember, and had carried her up the ramp of a ship, and she had been three years old and had put her arms round his neck and known, with a certainty that a three-year-old could not have had, exactly who he was. He did not know that. She intended that he never should.

"You are tired," he said.

"A bit."

"The night was long?"

"I woke up." She looked at the Processional Way through the columns, the first speeder traffic going by at the level of the cornices. "Did you feel anything, Master? Last night? Late."

She had not meant to ask it. She asked anyway. It was the way she asked all her questions of him, as a child asks: idle, looking elsewhere, one foot scuffing.

Plo Koon was quiet. The goggles turned towards her and stayed. "Feel what, little one?"

"I don't know. Something. Like something being dropped. A long way off." She made a small gesture with the hand that did not reach the end of her sleeve. "It woke me up. I thought maybe someone..." She let it go. "Nothing. I was dreaming, I expect."

"The Temple was quiet," he said, slowly. "I felt nothing. But I was in meditation, and in meditation I am not always listening." The mask hissed. "Does it trouble you, that I did not hear it?"

"No, Master."

"It troubles you that you did."

She did not answer that. He put his hand, briefly, on the top of her montrals; it was the only touch there she did not have to make herself bear.

"You asked me once," he said, "whether a dream could be a true thing. You were five. Do you remember?"

"I remember."

"I told you that the future is always in motion. I have thought since that it was not a kind answer. It was the Council's answer." He looked down at her. "Was it a dream, 'Soka?"

"I don't know what it was," she said, which was true, and was the first true thing she had said that morning, and she felt it sit between them like a stone she had set down and would have to pick up again later. "I'll be late for Master Drallig."

"Go, then." And as she went: "Hold the thing the right way round, child, for the sake of my ears. He complains of you."

"Yes, Master Plo," she said, and did not look back, because her face would have told him something.


The hall was the long one on the second level, with the high gallery and the sprung floor, and it smelled, as it always did, of twenty children and the ozone of the training sabers. Drallig had them in four ranks before the chimes had finished. Ahsoka took the back corner, as she always did, left-hand end, where the light from the clerestory did not quite reach and where a girl could hold a thing however she pleased for a full minute before anyone with authority noticed.

The training saber was too long for her. They all were; they were made for a twelve-year-old's wrist, and the grip was fat in her hand, and the weight was wrong, and she had stopped minding years ago. She held it reversed, blade down along her forearm, the way she had held it since she was seven, since the first morning they had put one into her hand and her hand had turned it round before she could stop it. Drallig had had words. Her clan-master had had words. Master Plo had had the gentlest words of all, and she had listened to every one of them and turned it back round the next morning, and they had put it down, eventually, to a Togruta thing, or a stubborn thing, or a thing she would grow out of, and she had let them.

The Padawan was late.

She felt him before the door. That was the thing she would not think about afterwards: that she had been standing in a corner with her eyes on Drallig's boots, and her whole body had turned, as a plant turns, half a second before the door at the end of the hall opened, and she had known.

He came in white. That was the first thing. He was a tall boy, fifteen, narrow in the shoulders, with the thin braid behind his right ear and the short sandy hair, and she had seen him across the refectory and along corridors and a dozen times in this hall for the better part of two years, and he had always come in like a boy: late, loose, apologising to Drallig with a grin that said he was not sorry. He did not come in like that. He came in as if the door were heavy. He stood just inside it and he looked at them, the four ranks, the twenty sand-coloured children with their sabers, and his eyes went along the first rank and the second and the third, one by one, not fast, and she thought: he is counting us.

She did not know why she thought it. She thought it the way one knows a smell.

Then his eyes reached the fourth rank and the corner and she looked down at the floor, hard, at the grain of the sprung wood, and her heart did the thing it had done in the night.

"Padawan," said Drallig. "Good of you to join us."

"Master. Sorry, Master."

A boy's voice. A boy's voice with something gone out of it.

The class went on. Drallig called the first form and the hall filled with the hum of twenty low blades and the squeak of boots, and Ahsoka moved through it with the rest of them, the sixth cut and the seventh, her reversed blade making the same shape the right way up would have made and nobody in the back rank caring. She kept her eyes on her own hands. She did not watch the Padawan. She felt where he was the whole time, the way one feels the sun on one side of one's face with one's eyes shut, and she did not watch him.

She watched him kneel to Ilyo.

She had not meant to. He had come down the third rank correcting grips, a word here, a hand there; he had passed Kessa with a word and come to Ilyo, the Rodian girl, who held hers like a spoon, and he had gone onto one knee to her without being asked. He had put his hands over hers on the hilt. He had said something too low for Ahsoka to hear, and Ilyo had laughed, and he had smiled at her, and the smile was the wrong smile: not a Padawan's smile for a youngling, careless and brief, but the smile of someone who had done this before, a great many times, and had done it once too many. He stayed down there with his hands over hers for longer than the grip needed. Then he stood, and his hand went to Ilyo's shoulder, light, as if to check that she was still there, and he moved on.

Ahsoka looked at the floor.

Stone falling. A red light moving. Someone saying her name as if it cost him to say it.

"Tano," said Drallig.

He said it as he always said it: a name and a sigh, from the front of the hall, over the heads of the ranks. Her grip. Of course. She did not turn it round. She stood with the blade down along her forearm and waited for the Padawan, who was already in the fourth rank, three children away, two, one, and she was looking at her hands, and then he was in front of her and she was looking at his.

He went down on one knee. He was still too tall for her, kneeling; she would have had to look up a little. She did not look up. She looked at his right hand as it came out and closed round her wrist.

It was shaking.

Not much. Not so a child would see it. A tremor in the fingers, in the tendons on the back of the hand, the kind a hand gets after it has carried something too heavy for too long and been told to put it down. He held her wrist and the shaking went into her through the bones, and she stood very still, the way one stands when an animal one has not expected has put its mouth round one's hand and has not yet decided to bite.

He turned the saber in her grip. Blade up. The right way. His fingers did it gently and they did it well, and then they stopped, and he did not let go.

He looked at it. Her hand, the hilt, the way her fingers had been arranged. He looked for a long time. The hall went on around them, Drallig calling the ninth cut, the hum of the blades; it went on a long way off. She could hear him breathing. She did not look at his face. She thought that if she looked at his face something would happen that she could not take back, and so she looked at his hand on her wrist, and the shaking in it, and she thought, with a clarity that was almost cold: you are not. You cannot be. He is a boy.

"You hold it like this," he said. Barely a voice at all. "Reversed. Always?"

"Yes," she said.

It was all she said. One word, in a child's flat voice, to a boy's hand. He let it go.

He let her wrist go and he stayed on his knee a second longer looking at the saber, the right way up now, in her small hand, and then he got up, too fast, the way a boy gets up when his knees have been on the floor too long, and he moved on down the rank to Wix. He did not look back. He did not correct her again.

She turned the blade back round.

She did it slowly, so that he would feel it if he was feeling, and he was feeling; she saw the line of his back go still for the width of a breath, three children away, and then go on. Drallig saw it too. Drallig saw everything. "Was there something, Padawan?" he said, from the front, and the Padawan said, "No, Master," and the ninth cut went on, and Ahsoka stood in the corner with her saber reversed along her arm and her wrist burning where he had held it, and told herself nothing.

That was the discipline. She told herself nothing, the whole of the rest of the hour. She did the forms. She let Tam Dorreth beat her at the paired drill, twice, because Tam was loud and a loud boy who won was a boy who did not look at you. She bowed to Drallig at the end and she filed out with the rest and she did not look for the Padawan, who had gone out of the far door before the chimes, white, with one hand against the frame.


She wrote the line at midday, when the dormitory was empty and the others were at history.

The flimsi was the kind that came out of the mechanics workshop, grey, thin, the stuff the droids wrapped spare parts in. She had taken a sheet of it the first year and folded it in four and kept it between the slats and the mattress ever since, and she wrote on it in Togruti, in the small square script that nobody in Hawkbat could read and that Master Plo could read a little, which was a risk she had decided was worth running, since there was no one else in the Temple she would rather be caught by.

She took it out. She unfolded it on her knee. It was not long. Six lines, seven, in a hand that had got steadier as the years went by; the first line had been written by a child of four who could barely hold a stylus and had wanted very badly to put a thing down before it went. She read them, all of them, quickly, the way one checks that a locked door is still locked.

Then she took the stylus and wrote the eighth line, small, at the bottom. It took her longer than it should have. She looked at it when it was done and did not like how it looked, and did not change it, and folded the flimsi in four and put it back under the mattress and smoothed the blanket over it, and sat for a moment with her hands between her knees.

Her hem came to the middle of her thighs. Her wrists showed. She was ten years old.

She went to history.


The small dojo on the third level was never used after the ninth hour. She had learned that in her second year, and she had been going there, perhaps one night in five, ever since: down the dormitory corridor in her socks with her boots in her hand, past the night-glow strips, up the service stair that the droids used, along the upper passage, and in.

It was a square room with a sprung floor and a rack of training sabers on the wall and a gallery along one side, high, railed, where a master could stand to watch a bout without being in it. Nobody stood there at night. The lights came up to a quarter when the door opened and no further. She had asked a maintenance droid about it once, carelessly, as a child asks, and it had told her the dojo's lighting was on a fault ticket that had been open for three years. She had thanked it. She had not told anyone.

She took two sabers off the rack.

They were too long for her. Both of them. The grips were fat in her hands and the balance was high and wrong, and when she thumbed them on the low blue hum of them filled the room and she felt the familiar thing go through her: not pleasure, exactly. Rightness. The feeling of a key going into a lock it was cut for. She turned them both in her hands so the blades ran down along her forearms, points towards the floor, and she stood for a moment in the quarter-light in her socks with her wrists showing and her tunic hem at the middle of her thighs, a small girl holding two sabers backwards in a room where nobody could see her, and she let her breath out.

Then she moved.

It was not the forms. The forms were for the hall, the right way up, in a rank. This was something else, something that had no name in any class she had sat in, a thing of turns and crossed blades and the body going low and coming up from the other side, both hands at once, the blades making shapes in the air that no one had ever taught her and that her hands knew the way they knew the stylus. Her arms were too short for it. Her wrists tired. The sabers were a half-hand too long and she felt it in every turn: the end of the blade going where she had not meant it, the weight dragging the stroke a fraction wide. She corrected. She went on. She was strong for ten, everyone said so, and she was not strong enough for this, and she did it anyway, every fifth night, in a room with a fault ticket, because her body did not know how to do it yet and some other part of her could not bear that it did not.

She did not think about the hall. She did not think about a hand shaking on her wrist, or a boy who counted children, or the wrong smile going down to Ilyo on one knee. She thought about the blades. The left came up and the right came round and she turned, low, and came up from the other side, and sweat ran down between her montrals and she thought about nothing at all for the first time all day.

She stopped because of the gallery.

It was not a sound. There was no sound. It was the feeling that comes on the back of the neck when a room one has believed empty has stopped being empty: a change in the air, a weight. She stood with both blades down and her chest going and she looked up.

The gallery was dark. The quarter-light did not reach it. There was the rail, and the black above the rail, and nothing moved there, and nothing breathed that she could hear over her own breathing. She stood and looked at it for a long time. She reached out, carefully, the way one puts a hand into cold water, and there was nothing: no presence, no mind, no shape of anyone, only the Temple's great slow hum and, far above, the particular quiet of a place where a thousand people were asleep.

And something else. Not in the gallery. Not anywhere she could point to. Something at the edge of what she could feel that had not been there two days ago, the way a new piece of furniture is not there and then is, and the whole room is arranged around it. Something that was shielded very hard, and very well, and by someone who had done it for a long time. Something that was, she thought, holding on.

She thumbed the sabers off. The hum died. She stood in the quarter-light in a room that was empty, that she was sure was empty, that she could not make herself believe was empty, and she put the two training sabers back on the rack, exactly where they had been, and picked up her boots.

In the doorway she looked back once at the gallery. There was nothing there. She told herself nothing.

She went down the service stair in her socks, and along the dormitory corridor with the night-glow making everything the colour of weak tea, and in past nineteen sleeping children, and she put her boots under her bunk and got in and lay with her feet not reaching the end of the mattress and her hands flat on her stomach and her heart going, exactly as it had gone the night before, exactly as it had gone in the hall with a shaking hand round her wrist.

She did not sleep.

She knew what she knew, and it was this, and it was only this, and she would let it be nothing else: a bell had rung, and something had come into the Temple that night, and it was wearing Skywalker's face.

Chapter Thirty. Supper.


The card came on the sixth morning after Obi-Wan left the tank for good, and Anakin knew what it was before the courier droid had finished reading the address, because it was cream and thick and carried the residence crest in the corner instead of the office seal; the seal had summoned him near twenty times and nothing good had ever come of it, and the residence card had never come at all, which was worse.

He took it from the droid at the door of their quarters with a boy's hands and turned it over. The hand was Sly Moore's; he had seen it on near twenty cards of the other kind. The Chancellor would be honoured. In recognition of his conduct on Oba Diah. The Padawan alone, as his master is understood to be convalescent. The evening of the twelfth. That was tomorrow.

Obi-Wan read it twice, sitting at the table with his left arm in the sling and his tea going cold, and then put it face down on the table the way he put his list face down, and looked at the window.

"Well," he said.

"I have to go."

"Yes."

"If I don't go, he knows. Near twenty times I've gone. I've gone with a fever. The one time I've got something to hide, I come down with a headache." Anakin heard his own voice: a boy's register, fast, a little high. He made it slow down. "He wrote it so it can't be refused. He wrote alone so that it can't be refused and we'd know he'd done it."

"I am aware of what he wrote." Obi-Wan turned the card over again and read the line about convalescence, and something in his mouth went dry and private. "I shall write to the office, of course. I shall say that my health is quite recovered and that I would be glad to accompany my Padawan, as is customary."

"They'll say no."

"They will say no beautifully. I should like to have it in writing."


The Council had been informed as a courtesy, which meant that the Council had not been asked, and Mace Windu came down to the Padawan corridor himself at the sixth hour to make it understood that he had noticed. He stood in the doorway of their common room and did not sit. He looked at the card on the table without touching it.

"You'll go," he said to Anakin. Not a question.

"Yes, Master."

"Your master is in a sling and the Chancellor of the Republic wants you for supper at his private residence, alone, a fortnight after you brought back a Syndicate's hostages in a stolen courier." Windu's voice was clipped and level. "The office says in recognition. The holonet says heroism. This Council said theft, and we meant it, and I'd like to know which word the Chancellor uses when the door is shut."

"I'll tell you, Master."

"Will you." Windu looked at him for a while. The look was the one from the window, the one Anakin had carried for twenty-three years on the inside of a mask and had thought he would never be able to stand again, and here he was standing it in a tunic that was short at the wrists. "I have been wrong about you before, Skywalker. I still don't know which way. Go and eat his supper. Come back and tell me what he said. All of it."

"Yes, Master."

Windu looked at Obi-Wan, and something passed between them that Anakin could not read, and then he went.

The reply from the Chancellor's office came within the hour, under Moore's hand. The Chancellor was overjoyed to hear of Master Kenobi's recovery. The Chancellor would not dream, however, of taxing it; the hour was late, the residence was a long way from the Temple, and the Chancellor had been assured by the Order's own healers that rest was the one prescription Master Kenobi was disinclined to follow. It was perfectly polite. Obi-Wan read it and laughed once, without any amusement at all, and said, "He has read Che's notes. Of course he has," and filed it.


They went behind the waterfall after the evening meal, the three of them, and sat on the stone with the water making its wall of noise between them and the Temple, and tried to prepare him. None of it helped, and all three of them knew that it would not before they started, and they did it anyway, because the alternative was to sit there and say nothing.

"He will not touch you in the first hour," Obi-Wan said. He had his back to the rock and his good hand flat on his knee, and he spoke in the voice he used for briefings, the voice Anakin had heard in a hundred tents. "He has never needed to. He will feed you and praise you and tell you what the Council thinks of you, and he will tell you what I think of you, which he will get slightly wrong on purpose so that you will correct him, and in correcting him you will tell him something. Don't correct him. Let him be wrong."

"I know how he talks."

"You know how he talks to a man. You have not sat across a table from him in that body since you came back, not for a whole evening, not alone." Obi-Wan looked at him. "He has had six years of walking in and out of you like a garden, and then one night the gate was locked, and he has been very patient. He is not going to be patient tomorrow. Tomorrow he will put his hand on the door."

"And I'll let him in."

"You will let him into what you built. The boy. All of it, as deep as he likes; he has had that boy before and he will want to see that he still has him. The question is what he does when he reaches the back of the boy and finds the wall."

"He's found the wall. Months ago."

"He has touched the wall. He has walked past it, politely, near twenty times, and noted it, and gone away. He has not tried it." Obi-Wan's voice did not change. "Tomorrow he will try it. And you will have to decide, in the second that he does, how strong a wall a fifteen-year-old can have."

Anakin said nothing. The water went on. Somewhere beyond it a bell rang the hour and a class of younglings was called in from the gardens, their voices going up in the long room and then stopping as the door shut.

"Let it hold," Obi-Wan said. "Let it be old and scarred and let him think what he thinks about who built it. Dooku, or the one before. He has already thought it; let him go on thinking it. Whatever you do, do not show him who built it."

"I know."

"I know you know. I am saying it so that it has been said."

Ahsoka had been quiet. She sat with her knees up and her arms around them, small against the rock, her lekku barely past her shoulders and the white of her markings showing in the dark; a girl of ten with a woman's eyes who had sat in a great many briefings too, on the other side of the table, and knew what they were worth. She waited until Obi-Wan had finished.

"Three things," she said. "Don't kill him."

Anakin looked at her.

"I mean it. I know you can. I saw you on the recording in my head every night this tenday whether I wanted to or not, and I know you can put him through that window before the guards have their pikes up. Don't. We talked about it. We haven't agreed, and until we have, you don't."

"I'm not going to kill the Chancellor at supper."

"Second. Don't let him see that you want to." She said it flat. "You've got a face. I know it, Obi-Wan knows it; you think you're sitting there like a stone and the whole room can see you want to put your hand through the table. He has seen that face across a desk since you were nine. If it comes up, look at the food."

"And the third."

"Eat something." Her mouth moved. It was not a smile. "He's made it late so you'll be hungry, you're always hungry, and you'll sit there with a full plate and your stomach making a noise and you won't eat because you're waiting, and he'll watch you not eat. That's a thing he can read. Put something in your mouth. Chew it. It's only food."

"Snips."

"It's only food," she said again, and looked away at the water.

None of it helped. He had known it would not when they climbed the steps, and they had known it; the thing waiting at the end of tomorrow was not a thing that could be briefed. But he sat with the two of them until the hour was very late and the Temple had gone quiet beyond the water, and when Obi-Wan got up, slowly, favouring the side, Ahsoka put her shoulder under his good arm without being asked, and Anakin walked behind them down the long steps and thought, this is what I have, and was not sure whether that was a comfort.

The towers of it went up so far that the air traffic lanes threaded between them at three levels, and the upper storeys sat above the lanes in a quiet that money bought. He had been here before, in the other life, in a cloak, at night, by the private lift from the sub-levels. He had never come in through the front.

The front was a lobby of pale stone and a Senate Guard at a desk, blue robes, blue helmet, a face he did not know. The guard read the card and did not look at him and said, "Padawan Skywalker. The Chancellor is expecting you. The lift on the left." There were two more guards at the lift, and two at the top of it when the doors opened on a corridor that was hushed and wide and smelt faintly of a wood that grew on Naboo and nowhere else, and at the end of the corridor there was a door, and in front of the door there was Sly Moore.

She was exactly as he remembered her, which he had stopped finding strange about anything by now and found strange again, standing in front of her. Tall. Pale as the inside of a shell. The white eyes that did not seem to focus on anything, and the long still hands folded at her waist, and the hood back. She had stood at the Emperor's shoulder for years and he had never known what she thought about any of it, or whether she thought.

"Padawan Skywalker," she said. It was barely sound.

"Aide Moore."

She looked at him, or in his direction, a beat longer than she needed to; the attention of a person used to looking at people on another's behalf. Then she turned and opened the door for him and did not come in.

The room was long. That was the first thing: it went away from him further than any room in the Temple quarters, panelled in the pale wood, lit low, and the far end of it was glass, floor to ceiling, and the glass was full of the city at night, and in the middle of the city, placed in the exact centre of the window as if the whole of Coruscant had been built as a frame for it, was the Temple. Five spires. The lights on along the Processional Way. The Council tower with a single window lit near the top.

He stopped in the doorway. He had not meant to.

"Ah," said the Chancellor. "You see I have arranged a view for you. I confess it is my favourite in the apartment. One can always find it, whatever else the city does." He was standing by the table with his hands clasped in front of him, in a long grey robe of the kind he wore in the evenings, which Anakin knew, and his face was lined and kindly and lit from the side by the candles on the table, which he also knew, and his voice was the voice. "Come in, come in. You have had a long day, I expect, and a long week, and I have kept you up far too late. Sit, please. Here, where you can see it."

The table was laid for two. Real linen. Real plates, white and heavy, and on them food that was not Temple food: a whole fish from the lake country of Naboo, baked in salt so that the crust had to be broken; a dish of small roasted roots in something sweet; green shuura cut in halves and bleeding on a plate; bread still warm in a cloth; a jug of water and a bottle of something pale. There was a cup in front of his place, a plain one, metal, with a lip worn smooth. He sat down in front of it and the Temple sat in the window above the Chancellor's shoulder, and the Chancellor sat down across from him with a small contented sigh, like a man taking the weight off, and smiled.


"I have heard a great deal," the Chancellor said, breaking the salt crust with the back of a spoon so that steam came out, "and I have heard it from three directions, and no two of them agree, which is generally how I know a thing is true. The holonet calls it heroism. I had my office pull the feeds; the word is used eleven times, which is a record for a Jedi of your years. The Council, " he lifted a piece of the fish onto Anakin's plate, white and flaking, and then onto his own ", calls it theft. Master Windu, when he deigned to answer my courtesy, used the word in writing. Theft of a courier. I have great respect for Master Windu's precision." He took up his fork. "I call it initiative. I have been in public life for a long time, Anakin, and I have learned that when a thing is called heroism by the people and theft by the institution, it is generally initiative, and initiative is the one thing institutions cannot bear."

"The courier was logged, Chancellor." A boy's voice. Careful, sullen at the edge. "Master Kenobi gave the order."

"So I understand, and so Master Kenobi has told the Council, and I am sure the Council has believed him, because it suits them to." The old eyes were warm. "I do not need you to tell me anything else. Eat. You are growing out of that tunic as I watch."

He put the fork in the fish. He put a piece of it in his mouth and it was hot and salt and perfectly cooked, and he chewed it, and it was like chewing cloth. He swallowed. He picked up the cup, which had water in it, and held it, and found that his hand wanted to go on holding it and that the metal was cool and the lip was worn, and he kept it in his hand.

"And how is Master Kenobi?"

"Recovering, Chancellor."

"I was so sorry to be unable to receive him tonight. Convalescence is the most tedious of all conditions; I have had my share. But I am glad. Glad for you." A pause of exactly the right length. "It is a great thing to have brought one's master home. I have wondered, you know, in six years, I have wondered, whether you knew how much you were valued, there." A small movement of the head toward the window, toward the spires. "They are not a demonstrative people. It is a virtue, I suppose. But a boy may do a thing in one night that a Knight would be knighted for, and bring eleven people home who would otherwise be in a Pyke's mine, and come back to be confined to his quarters. I found that hard to read. I will be candid; I found it hard not to intervene. I did not. One learns one's place."

"The Council has its reasons, Chancellor."

"The Council always has its reasons. That is what a council is for." He smiled. "Do you know, I had a message from the Senator from Naboo, about you? In the formal way, a committee note; she does everything in the formal way, our Senator. She wished to be assured of Padawan Skywalker's health after the incident. I told her you were in excellent health, as far as I knew, and that I would be seeing you soon and would say so. She was pleased. She remembers a boy on a ship, I think. We all have our boy on a ship." The fork moved. "She is a remarkable young woman. Difficult, in the Senate; she will make a great many enemies. I do what I can."

Anakin looked at the food.

It was all there. Every word of it. He had heard it first in a red-walled office at nine with his feet not reaching the floor, and at twelve in a speeder in the undercity, and at nineteen with the war on and his arm gone and Palpatine's hand on the stump, and at twenty-two in a box at the opera, and in a hundred rooms afterward when there was nothing left of him to groom and the voice had done it anyway, out of habit, the way a man keeps feeding a dog he has already decided to drown. They do not value you. I do. She is in danger; I do what I can. One learns one's place. He knew it by heart. He could have said the next sentence before it was said. And the horror of it, the thing that sat in his stomach with the fish, was how good it still was. How it still found the place. The Council had confined him. Windu had written theft. Obi-Wan had sat with a list face down and not shown it to him. None of that was a lie, and the voice took each true thing and turned it a quarter-turn, and the quarter-turn was the whole of the work, and at forty-five with twenty-three years of the suit in his bones and this man's death in his hands he sat in a fifteen-year-old's body and felt it work.

"Yes, Chancellor," he said.

"You are very quiet tonight."

"It's been a long week, Chancellor."

"So it has. So it has." The Chancellor set his fork down. He folded his hands on the linen, old and spotted and very still, and looked at the boy across the table with the candle between them and the Temple in the glass behind him, and his face was the face it had always been, and his voice was the voice, and the room changed.


It was not a stroll.

Near twenty times in that body he had sat across from this man and felt the touch come: a warmth at the edge of thought, a hand trailed along a garden wall, a man who had the key letting himself in to look at the roses. Six years of it before he came back and six months since. He had built the boy for it, in the first week, in the quarters at night with Obi-Wan asleep on the other side of the wall: a boy's surface, shallow and bright, with everything a boy of fifteen could be expected to keep in it. Palpatine had walked that surface near twenty times and found it, and had looked once at the wall at the back of it, and had gone away.

This was a hand on the door.

It came in through the eyes. That was how he had always come, in the other life, and Anakin had forgotten and remembered in the same second: the pressure behind the eyes, a weight that was not pain, and a taste at the back of the mouth like metal, and the sense, absolute, the way one knows which way is down, of being looked at from the inside. The voice did not stop. "I have been thinking," the Chancellor said, warmly, somewhere a long way off, "about what might be done for you, after this," and the hand was on the door and the hand was pushing, and the strength of it was the strength he knew, and it was all there was in the world.

He opened the door.

He let it in. Into the boy. Into the vault he had built, and had deepened since, in the weeks after Ilum, every night, in case: here was the resentment, bright and hot and childish, of being confined to the Temple like a youngling who had wet himself; here was Windu's face at the window and a boy's hatred of it, clean, uncomplicated; here was Bellas in the dojo saying slave boy and the memory of the blade a finger from a boy's throat and the shame that came after and the boy not knowing what had got into him; here was a girl called Mirren Dask in the second row of Vos-Kerrick's class and the way she pushed her hair back, and the boy's stupid heart; here was his mother. He let it in to his mother, because it always went to his mother, and he had built her for it: Shmi in the dust with Watto's tin, Shmi in the letters saying Owen is a good boy, Ani, you would like him, the boy's helpless longing and his anger that the Order kept him from her and his fear that one day the Council would stop it. And under the mother, newest, laid in on purpose over the last tenday: the tower room on Oba Diah, and the thing that had come out of the boy there, the way his hand had gone out and the Pyke had risen off the floor, and the boy's terror of it afterward, lying awake, not knowing what he was, not daring to ask his master, not daring to ask anyone, and wondering, just wondering, just once, the way a boy would, whether there was anyone who might understand it who was not a Jedi.

He felt the Chancellor find that. He felt the pleasure in it, the pure, almost paternal delight of a man who has turned over a stone in his own garden and found a rare and precious thing. The hand lingered there. It stroked.

And then it went on, as he had known it would, past the boy, to the back of the vault, to the wall.

The wall was old and scarred and built over years. Vokara Che had read it so. Dooku had read it so and gone white. The Chancellor laid his hand on it now, and Anakin felt him read the grammar of it the way a man reads his own handwriting on a page he does not remember writing, and felt the question rise in him, fully formed, cold and interested: who built this?

He could have held it. The wall would have held. It had held for twenty-three years against this man's hand, and it would have held tonight. Obi-Wan had said let it hold. Ahsoka had said don't let him see. He had meant it.

He opened the wall.

One second. He did not think about it; the thinking came afterward, on the bench. It was not the boy who did it and it was not Vader; it was whatever was left after both of them, the thing that had taken the mask off and looked at its son, and it was tired, and it had been looked at from the inside by this man for the whole of its life and it was not going to be looked at that way again. He opened the wall and let the hand through and let it touch, for one second, the thing behind it.

Not a memory. Nothing with a shape to it, nothing that could be told afterwards as a thing seen. A mind that had once held him by the throat, and the grip of it, closing. A fall, long, with no floor in it. Then a silence, very large. Anakin did not give him the shaft, or the blue light, or the old man lifted above his head; those stayed where they were, behind his teeth. He gave him only what they had left behind: the weight of the mind that had decided, and under it the thing that had no name and was not hatred and was not the dark and was not anything this man had ever had the use of. It would reach him as cold, and a dropping away, and quiet. He would not know whose, or where, or when.

Then he closed.

Not slammed. Closed, the way one closes a door on a room where someone is sleeping. The wall came down between them whole and old and scarred and the hand was outside it, and the boy's bright shallow surface came back over the top of it like water finding its level, resentment and Bellas and the girl in the second row, and the pressure behind his eyes went away, and the taste of metal went, and the candle was between them on the table, and the fish was going cold.

The Chancellor had not moved. His hands were still folded on the linen. His face had not changed at all, and that was the terrible thing: it had not changed, and Anakin, who had lived inside this man's moods for twenty-three years the way a fish lives in a current, could feel that under the face there was nothing. Not fear. Not anger. A stillness like the stillness of the moment after a very loud sound, in which a man takes stock of what he has.

Both of them knew that something had been done. Neither could say what, or say it aloud. There was not a sentence in Basic that either of them could have spoken across that table that would not have ended the Republic in the morning, and they both knew that too, and sat in it.

The Chancellor smiled.

"You have grown, my boy," he said.

"Yes, Chancellor."

The face, in the instant it understood. Not rage; that came later, on the way down. Only the eyes gone wide and the mouth a little open, an old man's face finding out, a half-second before the rest of him did, that the floor had ended. Then the sound in the shaft, the long thinning cry going down and down past the pipework until the dark took it, and after it the silence, which was the largest thing he had ever heard.

He took a mouthful of the fish. He chewed it. He swallowed it, and drank from the cup, and put the cup down.

"Forgive me," the Chancellor said. "I have kept you far too late, and talked a great deal, as old men do." He rose, and Anakin rose, and the Temple hung in the window between them with its one lit tower. "You must come again, when Master Kenobi is well. The three of us. I should like that very much." He put his hand out, and Anakin took it, and it was dry and warm and did not tremble, and did not hold on. "Moore will see you down. Good night, Anakin."

Sly Moore was outside the door. She had not moved, he thought, in two hours. She walked him to the lift along the corridor that smelt of Naboo wood and the guards at the lift did not look at him and the doors opened, and she stood outside them with her hands folded and her white eyes on him, or near him.

"Padawan," she said, and the doors closed.


He walked. He could have taken a transport from the foot of the tower; the Temple would have sent one if he had called; he did not call. He walked out through the pale lobby into the Senate District at night.

It was cold at this hour and there was wind at the kerb, coming off the lanes, and the plaza was empty. The Senate dome sat at the end of the long avenue with its lights on. Monument Plaza was past that. He walked, and his boots were loud on the stone, and he thought about nothing at all for the length of the avenue, with great effort, and then he came to a bench by a dry fountain where some senator's statue stood with a hand out to the empty air, and he sat down on it, because his legs had decided to.

He could not stand up.

He tried. He put his hands on the edge of the bench and pushed and the legs did not take it. They were shaking from the inside, in the long muscles, the way a boy's legs shake. His hands were shaking too. He put them between his knees. His face was hot and the wind was cold on it and he was so hungry he felt sick, and he was fifteen, and he had just sat across a table from Darth Sidious for two hours and opened his mind to him on purpose, which was a thing that at forty-five, with everything the suit could give him and everything the dark could, he had never once been able to do. He had never once in twenty-three years let the man in and chosen what he saw. He had been looked at. Tonight he had been looked at and had looked back, and the body that had done it was a child's, and it had used everything it had, and now it sat on a bench in the Senate District at the first hour and could not get up.

He did not know how long he sat. A speeder went over low and its lights went across the senator's bronze hand and off.

Someone sat down on the bench beside him.

He knew who it was before he turned his head, from the bond, which he had kept thin all evening and which was suddenly not thin at all, and from the way the person sat: carefully, favouring the left, with a small breath at the end of it. Obi-Wan had his cloak on over the sling and his hood down. His beard needed trimming. He looked across the plaza at the dome and not at Anakin.

"You were told not to come," Anakin said. His voice came out wrong.

"I was told, by the Chancellor's office, not to tax my convalescence. I have been sitting on a bench across the plaza for two hours and eleven minutes in a wind, which is not taxing. It is bracing." He did not look round. "I have sat in Monument Plaza for four years while you were in that office. I did not see why tonight should be the exception."

"He said, " Anakin stopped. He did not know what came next.

"I know what he said. He has been saying it since you were nine. I have heard the half of it that he says about me." Obi-Wan's good hand came out of the cloak and lay on the bench between them, on the cold stone, not touching. "You walked out of his house. Under your own power, down a lift, through a door, across a plaza. That is the whole of the evening, Anakin. The rest is detail."

Anakin looked at the hand on the bench. After a while he put his own on top of it, a boy's hand, narrow, over a man's, and left it there, and neither of them said anything for some time.

"You didn't eat," Obi-Wan said eventually.

"I ate two mouthfuls."

"Two. Well. Ahsoka will want to know. I shall tell her three."

Anakin laughed. It came out of him like a cough, like something breaking loose, and he put his face in his free hand and sat there on the bench with his shoulders going and Obi-Wan's hand under his and the wind coming off the lanes, and when he could stand, Obi-Wan stood with him, slowly, and they walked back to the Temple together, which was a long way, and said nothing at all.


Jurokk, the Gate Master, let them in at the gate without a word. The Temple inside was dark and warm and smelt of stone and the night-incense.

They did not go to the quarters. Anakin went to the stair that led up and up to the eastern roof and Obi-Wan followed him, slowly, holding the rail, and did not ask why, and did not complain about the steps. He was barely a week out of the tank, and he had walked the whole way from the Senate District on a side that had not finished mending, and now he was climbing to a roof; Anakin heard what it cost him in every breath on the stair and said nothing, because to name it would have been to ask him to stop.

She was there. On the parapet at the eastern edge with the city below her and the night going grey at the far rim of it, a small shape in a sand-coloured tunic with her knees up and her arms round them, the way she had sat behind the waterfall. She had not slept. He could see it from the door. She turned her head when they came out onto the roof and looked at the two of them, the boy and the man, the sling and the hunched shoulders and whatever was on his face, and she did not get up and she did not say anything, and the bond between them, thin as thread, went tight for a moment and then slack.

The convor was with her.

It sat on the parapet a hand's breadth from her hip, green, owl-faced, its feathers lifting a little in the wind, its round eyes open and on him. It had never flown, and it did not fly now. It looked at him the way it had always looked at him, as if it were checking something, and then it closed its eyes, slowly, and opened them again, and went back to looking at the city.

He crossed the roof and sat down on the parapet on her other side, so that the bird was between them. Obi-Wan came after and lowered himself onto the stone on Anakin's other side, with a breath, and let his back rest against the rail. The grey at the rim of the city was turning to something else. Below them the Processional Way had its lights still on and across the whole of Coruscant, a long way off, in the quiet that money bought, there was a tower with one window lit, and he did not look at it.

Nobody said anything. The bird did not move. They sat, the three of them and the convor, and let the morning come.

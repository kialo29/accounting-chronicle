Chapter Four. The Garden.


Every tenth day the Chancellor saw the boy.

It had been so since Anakin was nine and newly scrubbed, standing in the red office with Qui-Gon's death still in him like a swallowed stone, and the man behind the desk had come round it to crouch to his height and say that he hoped they might be friends. Six years of tenth days. Anakin had been back nine of them, and had spent most of those nine days knowing that the tenth was coming, the way a man in a cell knows the hour the door will open.

He dressed for it as a boy would. That took thought. The fifteen-year-old he was wearing had owned, he found, a better tunic kept for the Senate and a worse one kept for everything else, and he put on the better and found the collar sat wrong and left it. He looked in the glass over the basin and a narrow sandy child looked back with a braid behind his ear and no scar, and he tried the face he would need, the face of a boy who was a little proud of where he was going and would not say so, and the child in the glass did it well enough that Anakin had to look away.

Obi-Wan was waiting in the common room with his cloak already on.

"You needn't," Anakin said.

"I'm going that way." He was not. He had never, in the six years of tenth days that this body remembered, been going that way; he had always been going that way. "Dex owes me a conversation."

"Dex is in CoCo Town."

"Which is, if you consult a map, in the direction of the Senate. Loosely." He held the door. "Come along."

They went down through the Temple in the thin early light, past the Thousand Fountains where the water-noise came out into the corridor like a draught, down the Processional Way between the pillars with the city opening in front of them, and Anakin kept his shield at the depth he had settled on in the first week, a boy's sulk, a door pulled to and not latched, and felt Obi-Wan not quite lean on it. That was new. The Obi-Wan he remembered had, at this age, left the bond alone the way one leaves a cat alone. This one touched it, lightly, every few minutes, as if checking that a pocket still had a thing in it.

Anakin added it to the list in his head. Things that are different. The list was getting long.

They took an air-taxi to the edge of the Senate District because that was what they always did, and then they walked, because that was also what they always did; Obi-Wan said once that a Jedi should arrive on his feet, and had never said why. The district rose round them. The dome of the Senate sat on the skyline like a held breath. Monument Plaza was already busy with aides in grey and clerks in blue and a Rodian selling something hot out of a cart, and the air smelt of it and of speeder exhaust, and far above the lanes were full and moving and the light came down between them in bars.

Anakin had walked this plaza under a black cloak with the crowd going out from him like water from a dropped stone. He walked it now as a thin boy whose master had to shorten his stride, and a woman with a stack of flimsi walked straight into him and said sorry, love and went on. He stood for a moment feeling where her shoulder had struck him. It did not matter. It was only that nobody had walked into him in twenty-three years.

"Anakin."

"I'm here."

"You looked, for a moment, as though you were not." Obi-Wan's tone was easy. His hand, Anakin noticed, had gone to his belt and come away from it. "What are you going to say to him?"

"Whatever he asks me. He asks about classes. He asks about you."

"I'm aware of what he asks about me." They had come to the foot of the steps. The Senate Office Building went up above them, pale and ribbed, a thousand windows, and somewhere high on the far side of it a red room with a man in it. The Senate Guards at the doors wore the blue of the old Republic and held their pikes like men who had never had to use them. "You know that you may leave whenever you wish. You need not wait to be dismissed."

"I know."

"If you were unwell. If you were tired. If you simply did not care to stay."

"Master." Anakin made the boy laugh. It came out thin. "I've been doing this since I was nine."

"Yes," Obi-Wan said. "You have."

He stood at the bottom of the steps and did not go up them. He had never gone up them. Anakin had known that as a fact for six years of this body's memory and understood it now for the first time as a thing with a shape: a man of thirty-one who had been in the Senate Office Building on a hundred errands and had never once walked his Padawan to the Chancellor's door, because to walk him to the door was to be asked to come in, and to be asked to come in was to be asked, pleasantly, by a Chancellor with all the time in the world, to wait outside. He had tried. Anakin remembered that too, now that he was looking for it: a request to the Council, two years ago, that the audiences be reduced; a kind note from the Chancellor's office regretting that the boy was such a comfort to him; Obi-Wan's face reading it. The Council had sided with the comfort.

"An hour," Obi-Wan said. "I'll be back here in an hour."

"Dex might keep you."

"Dex has never kept anyone. It is one of his few failings." He put his hand, briefly, on the back of Anakin's neck, where the braid was, which he had not done when Anakin was nine or twelve or fourteen, and took it away. "Go on."

Anakin went up the steps. At the top he looked back, because a boy would, and Obi-Wan was still there with the plaza moving round him, not looking at the diner he was not going to, not looking at anything, a Jedi in a brown cloak standing very still at the foot of a building as though he had been told to guard it and had not been told from what.


Sly Moore met him in the outer office. She was as he remembered her, pale and smooth and hairless and entirely unreadable, and she inclined her head the exact depth she had always inclined it and said, "Padawan Skywalker. He's expecting you," and Anakin realised with a small cold shock that she would be standing on the far side of a door, seven years from now, when a black-suited thing came through it from the other way.

He did not let that into his face. The doors opened and he went in.

Red walls. The wide window with the city in it and, far across the skyline, small and white, the Temple. The desk, and the two chairs angled towards each other on the near side of it, because the Chancellor had never once in six years spoken to the boy across his desk. A jug on a side table with condensation on it. And the man, rising, in the dark robes of his office, fifty-eight years old, with his hair greying out of its red and his face creased and kindly and his hands already out.

"Anakin. My dear boy. Come in, come in. Sit. You look taller. Is that possible in ten days? I think it must be."

"Chancellor."

"Sit. I've had them bring the muja juice. I remember you don't care for the tea. Master Kenobi's influence has its limits, it seems." He poured it himself. He had always poured it himself. The jug tilted, and the juice came out pale orange and cold, and he handed the glass across and sat, and arranged his robe, and smiled, and the touch began.

Anakin had known it would. He had told himself, in the taxi, on the steps, in the outer office, that he knew exactly what it would feel like and that the knowing was his armour. It was. It was also true that he had never before felt it from this side with his eyes open, and the first thing he had to do, with the glass cold in his hand and the Chancellor asking whether the astrogation module had improved since last time, was not shut it out.

It did not push. That was the thing Jedi never understood about it, in the years when Jedi were still alive to misunderstand: they expected a Sith to force a mind, and they shielded against force. Sidious did not force. He strolled. The touch came in at the edge of Anakin's mind the way a man comes in at his own garden gate, dry and patient and proprietary, in no hurry at all, and it moved along the paths it knew. Anakin had lived under that touch for twenty-three years. He knew it the way a man knows the weight of a hand that has rested on his shoulder every day of his adult life: the exact dryness, the exact warmth, the way it paused at things it liked.

He gave it things to like.

He had built the surface over nine nights, lying in the small room with Obi-Wan's presence the far side of the wall, and he laid it out now the way a man lays out a table for a guest who must not go into the kitchen. In front: a boy's irritation, broad and real-feeling, at having been made to go back to the Shii-Cho forms that morning because Drallig said his footwork had gone lazy; the resentment had edges; Sidious liked edges. Beside it, a speeder. A racing speeder in a showroom window on the Temple approach, a Mobquet with a twin-cell, which this body had in fact stood in front of for a quarter of an hour the week before Anakin arrived in it, so that the wanting was in the muscle memory and the touch found it warm. Beside that, lower, a hollow he did not have to invent: his mother. He let it stay generic. A boy's hollow, the shape of a woman in a doorway, nothing in it with a name or a date.

And beside that, because a boy of fifteen with nothing of that kind in him would have been a thing for the touch to pause at, a girl. He had thought hard about the girl. She needed to exist. He had chosen a Padawan a year older, a human girl called Mirren Dask who sat two rows in front of him in Vos-Kerrick's politics class and who he had never, in either life, spoken to, and he had given her a way of pushing her hair back that the body could plausibly have watched, and a small clumsy heat around her name. It was the most careful work he had done since Endor. It was embarrassing. It was meant to be.

The touch moved through it all like a man walking his borders in the evening. It touched the resentment, and warmed. It touched the speeder, and was amused. It touched the mother, and lingered, as it always had, as it had lingered on every wound he had ever let it see. It touched the girl, and Anakin felt something that in a different face would have been a smile.

",  the trade bills again, I'm afraid," the Chancellor was saying. "I don't know why I tell you these things. Because you listen, I suppose, and no one else does. The Outer Rim Free Trade Act, which is neither free nor, if the Trade Federation has its way, about trade. Forty-one systems want the tariff schedules revised and the Core wants them left alone and Nute Gunray wants, as ever, whatever is worst for everyone. We've had it on the floor three times. Do you know what it is to have a thing on the floor three times, Anakin? It is to have it on the floor a fourth."

"Does it pass?"

"Something passes. Something always passes. Whether it is the thing one wanted is a separate question." He drank his own juice, which he had poured a quarter-glass of for form's sake. "And of course the reformers are at it. Senator Amidala's faction. Very energetic."

Anakin's hand did not move on the glass.

He had known the name might be said. He had known it with his whole body, in the taxi, on the steps; he had built nothing for it, because there was nothing that could be built for it, because the only thing in him that could be given to that name was the whole of him. She was twenty. She was in a building he could see from this window. She had never been to Geonosis, or stood on a landing platform in the fire with her hand on her belly, or said his name in a room on Polis Massa he had not been in and had seen afterwards, every night, for twenty-three years. She was a Senator of Naboo in her second year, and she was alive, and the touch was in his mind, moving, looking at what the name did.

He let it find what a boy would have. That was the only way. A boy of fifteen who had met a queen when he was nine and told her, in a hovel on Tatooine, that she was an angel, would flush at her name and hate that he had flushed, and so Anakin let the body flush, let the heat come up his neck into the roots of his hair where the braid was, let a small sharp humiliation sit on the top of the surface like a dropped coin, and the touch found it and was delighted, and underneath it, where the touch could not go, Anakin held the rest of her in both hands and did not make a sound.

"Energetic," he said. It came out thick. That was all right. That was the boy.

"Oh, very. She and Senator Organa of Alderaan, and that very composed young woman from Chandrila. Mothma. The three of them have found each other, which is what idealists do, and they have a bill of their own, which is what idealists do next. An anti-corruption measure. Full disclosure of corporate contributions. Quite impossible, of course; the Senate would strike it down nine to one; but they're young and they say it beautifully, and the HoloNet loves them. I confess I rather love them myself. One needs the young to be unreasonable. It is how one finds out what is possible." He smiled. "You should hear her speak. Perhaps one day you shall."

I have heard her speak, Anakin thought, behind the wall, and said, "The Council says Padawans shouldn't go to the Senate floor."

"The Council says a great many things." The touch moved on. "And on the subject of people who say things beautifully. Have you seen the Count's latest?"

"Count?"

"Dooku. Of Serenno. You'll have heard of him; Master Kenobi's master was his Padawan, which I suppose makes him a sort of grandfather to you, in the Jedi way of things." The Chancellor reached to the desk without rising and came back with a slim bound thing with a plain cover and no title. "He publishes these. Privately; he can afford to. This one is on the Senate's ethics, or want of them. He is a very fine writer. He argues that the Republic is rotten, which is true, and that the Jedi serve the rot, which has a certain force, and that a man of conscience should say so, which he does, in sentences I would give a great deal to be able to write. I disagree with every word and I have read it twice."

He held it out. Anakin took it, because a boy would, and his hands did not shake, and in the place behind the wall there was a hangar on Geonosis and a curved hilt coming round faster than a fifteen-year-old could have followed and then nothing where his forearm had been, and a room on a ship years later and a man on his knees with his hands bound in front of him and an old man's voice saying, kindly, do it. He turned the pamphlet over. The Count's seal was on the back, small and elegant.

"He left the Order," Anakin said.

"He did. Some years ago. Over, I'm told, a difference of conscience. Those are the only kind of differences worth leaving anything over." The touch had gone back along the path to the speeder, idly, as a man will pick a leaf off a thing he has already looked at. "He writes to me, occasionally. We are both, in our different ways, critics of the thing we serve. I have an advantage over him, of course."

"Chancellor?"

"I'm leaving." He spread his hands. "My term. The law is quite clear, and I have never been one to argue with the law, and when the time comes I shall go back to Naboo and grow something. I find I'm looking forward to it. One cannot hold an office like this for ever; it wears a man thin; and there are days, Anakin, when I would give the whole of it for a garden and an afternoon." He said it with real warmth. He said it, Anakin thought, with real wanting, and that was the horror of him and always had been: that the lie and the truth came out of the same mouth in the same voice and even the mouth did not always know which was which. "But not yet. There's work to be done first. There is always, it seems, a crisis of some kind to be seen through before one is permitted to be tired."

Yes, Anakin thought. I know which crisis. He drank his juice.

And the touch, which had wandered back along the edge of the garden where the girl was, where the mother was, where it had always stopped, because there had never been anything beyond that to stop at, went past them.

Anakin felt it go. He had known it would. The surface was furnished; it had to be; a bare surface was a confession, a boy with nothing in his head was a locked room with a sign on it. But a furnished surface had an edge, and beyond the edge there had been, for six years, the open country of a child's mind, unfenced, going down and down to things the child did not know he had. The touch had walked there every tenth day. It had taken its time. It had planted what it planted.

Now it came to the edge of the furniture and there was a wall.

He had built it under Sidious's own hand, over two decades, in a chamber on a Star Destroyer with a respirator counting his breaths, because a thing that had been inside your mind would be inside it again, and the only privacy a man in a suit had was the privacy he could hold between his teeth. It was not a Jedi's wall. A Jedi's shield was a clean thing, a pane of glass, a held breath; he had felt a hundred of them crack. This was old. This was scarred. It had been built in layers by someone who knew how the other side worked and had mortared every course with hate, and it did not look like anything a fifteen-year-old could have made, because it was not.

The touch stopped.

It did not press. He had been afraid of that and had been wrong to be; pressing was not how the man worked, not here, not in this office with the window open on the city. It stopped at the wall the way a man stops at a wall in his own garden where there was no wall the week before. It stood there. It was very still, and in its stillness Anakin felt, with a clarity that twenty-three years had given him and nothing else could have, that the man in the chair across from him was not angry, and was not alarmed, and was for the first time in a very long time surprised, and that the surprise was giving him pleasure.

",  Master Kenobi well?" the Chancellor said. His voice had not changed at all. "I never see him. I think he would prefer that I never see him. How is he?"

Anakin heard himself from a long way off.

"He's well. My Master has me on forms again."

He heard it as it left him. Not the words. The words were a Padawan's, any Padawan's, in any corridor of the Temple; a boy said my Master forty times a day. It was the weight. It was the way the two words had gone down at the end, flat and low and complete, the cadence of a man on one knee on a black floor with his head bowed, saying a thing he had said ten thousand times to the only person he had ever said it to. Yes, my Master. For twenty-three years those two words in that voice had meant: I am yours. He had said them in this room. Not this room. A room that was this room, later, with the red gone to black.

The Chancellor's eyes rested on him.

They were pale and watery and kind, the eyes of a tired man who had been good to a boy, and they did not move, and nothing moved in them, and Anakin had seen those eyes without the kindness in them and knew precisely how little the kindness weighed and how little it took to lift it off. The touch was still at the wall. The eyes were on his face. Two seconds. Three. Behind the wall a man who had killed this man held perfectly still, and on the surface a boy flushed again, at nothing, at the attention, and looked down at his glass.

"Forms," the Chancellor said gently. "At your age. He is very thorough."

"He says my footwork's lazy."

"Then I am sure it is, and I am sure it will not be by the next time I see you." The touch withdrew from the wall. It did not withdraw from his mind; it went back along the paths, unhurried, and stood a moment at the mother, and a moment at the girl, and let itself out at the gate. "You must forgive an old man's curiosity, Anakin. You seem ,  quieter. These last ten days. Has something happened at the Temple?"

"No, Chancellor."

"No one has been unkind to you?"

"No."

"Because you may tell me. You know that. Whatever it is, and whatever the Council thinks of it, and whatever Master Kenobi, with the very best will in the galaxy, does not understand." The hand on the shoulder; every sentence a hand on the shoulder. "I have no children. I shall have no children; the office does not permit it, and I was never the sort of man who would have managed it well. But I have you, every tenth day, and I find I look forward to it more than to anything else in a very long week. You must let me be of use."

"I'm fine," Anakin said, as a boy says it. "I'm just tired. Obi-Wan says I've grown four centimetres."

"Then that explains everything. Growing is exhausting. I remember it, just." The Chancellor rose, which meant that it was over, and Anakin rose with him, and the man took the pamphlet gently out of his hand and put it back on the desk and then, as he always did, walked him to the door with a hand not quite touching his back. "Give Master Kenobi my regards. Tell him he need not stand in the plaza. There is a perfectly good anteroom."

Anakin looked at him.

"He has stood in Monument Plaza for an hour every tenth day for six years," the Chancellor said, with great fondness. "It is one of the things about him that I most admire. Off you go."


Obi-Wan was at the foot of the steps. He had not been to CoCo Town. He had not, Anakin thought, moved: he was standing where he had been standing, in the same place in the stone, with the same plaza going round him and the Rodian's cart a little further along, and when he saw Anakin come out he did not look relieved, which was how Anakin knew what the hour had cost him.

"Dex?" Anakin said.

"Was out." Obi-Wan fell in beside him. He did not ask. He never asked, on the walk back, and Anakin had taken it for six years of this body's memory as indifference and knew now what it was: a man who could not bear to hear the answer and could not bear to be seen not asking, and so said nothing, and walked. "You'll want something to eat."

"I had juice."

"Juice," Obi-Wan said, in a tone that put the word on a list.

They walked out of the district the way they had come in, under the lanes, with the light in bars. Anakin walked with the boy's loose stride and let the boy's tiredness be in his shoulders, and it was not entirely performance. His legs ached. His hands were cold where the glass had been. He kept the surface up, because the surface was a habit now and would need to be a habit for a long time, and he kept the bond thinned to a sulk, and he did not look back at the building, and he felt it.

Not the touch. The touch was gone; it had gone at the gate, the way it always went, and the Chancellor was behind a thousand windows doing whatever the Chancellor did in the hours of a very long week. This was something else. This was the thing a house feels when a man has come up the path in the night and tried the door, and found it locked, and gone away without knocking. Nothing was broken. Nothing had been taken. There was only the handle, which had been turned, and which had held, and which would be turned again.

He had been prepared for the wall to be found. He had told himself, over nine nights, that it was the one thing that could not be helped: that the child's mind had been open and was now closed, and the only question was whether it was found closed by a man who thought it was a child's wall, or by a man who knew what a wall was. He had hoped. The hope had been a boy's hope and he had let it be, because he had so little else to be a boy with.

The man knew what a wall was. The man had been pleased.

Anakin walked. A speeder went over low and the downdraught pushed the hair off his forehead and Obi-Wan said something about the tariff schedules and Anakin said mm, and his hands were cold, and he thought: he will send someone. He will not come himself; he never came himself; he will look at the wall and he will think about who could have built it in a child and he will think of the only other man in the galaxy who could have, and he will send that man to look. And that man will come and put his hand on my mind and find in it a hate with his own name on it, with a history it cannot have, and he will go back and say so.

And then.

He did not know and then. That was the thing about being here: he did not know and then. He had spent twenty-three years in a story he already knew the end of and he was standing now in the Senate District in a boy's boots in a story that had, in a red room, an hour ago, stopped being that story, and he could not see past the corner, and the not-seeing came up through him like water through a floor.

Blue light. The whole of the room was blue light and his body was the colour of it, lifted, the lightning going in at the fingers and out at the spine, and the sound was his own voice coming apart. The old man's hands. The old man's face in the light of what came out of his hands, not angry, interested, the eyes pale and watery and kind. On the floor the boy, curled, screaming, holding his own hand. And then no floor; and then the weight, the astonishing weight of him, lighter than a man should be and heavier than anything Anakin had ever lifted, the robe and the bones in it and the lightning going through the armour into the arms that held him and the respirator counting, four, three, and the rail of the shaft coming up under them both, and the old man turning in his arms to look at him, and the look, the look at the end, which had not been fear.

"Anakin."

The plaza. The light in bars. He had stopped walking. Obi-Wan was a step ahead and had turned, and his face had gone very still, the way it went when there was a thing in the room he did not want to see, and his hand was out, not touching, two centimetres from Anakin's arm.

"I'm here," Anakin said.

"You said that this morning."

"It was true this morning."

Obi-Wan looked at him for a moment longer. Then he took the hand back and put it in his sleeve and said, in the dry voice, "He asked after me."

"He sends his regards. He says you needn't stand in the plaza. There's an anteroom."

Something went over Obi-Wan's face and was gone. "How kind of him," he said. "Come along. You need to eat." And he walked, and after a moment Anakin walked with him, a boy and his master going home through the Senate District in the middle of a bright morning, and far above them the lanes were full and moving and nobody looked down.

Chapter Six. Letters from Tatooine.


The courier had been and gone by the time Anakin got back to the quarters, and Obi-Wan was holding the letter out before the door had finished closing.

"This came for you."

He said it the way he said that the tea was made, or that the Council had moved a briefing. He did not look up from the datapad in his other hand. The flimsi envelope hung in the air between them at the height of a boy's chest, which was lower than it had been a year ago and would be lower still by the spring, and Anakin stood with his stung shoulder throbbing under the tunic and looked at it and did not take it.

It was a courier envelope, the grey kind with the Temple seal in the corner, the kind he had seen perhaps a dozen times in his first life and never addressed to him. Across the front, in the oblong Outer Rim hand that the Hutts' scribes taught and the slaves copied, was a name he had not seen written in forty years.

Skywalker, Anakin. Jedi Temple. Coruscant.

"It's from your mother," said Obi-Wan, still not looking, as though the boy might have needed telling. "I believe the Mos Eisley relay was down for a tenday; that is why it is late. You will want to read it before supper, I expect, so I shall not ask you to do anything useful until after."

Anakin took the envelope.

He took it with his right hand, which was his own, and the flimsi was slightly rough and slightly warm from Obi-Wan's holding it, and it weighed nothing, and he thought with great clarity: This did not happen.

"Thank you, Master," he said, and his voice came out at the right pitch, and he went into his room and shut the door.


He sat on the bed. He did not open it at once.

In his memory there were no letters. He had searched the memory now, standing in the common room with the envelope in the air, and he searched it again sitting down, methodically, the way he had once gone through a captured ship's logs deck by deck. There had been the parting in Watto's yard. There had been the thing he had made himself not think about for three years, and then had thought about every night for the next seven, and then the dreams. There had been Padmé's ship coming down over the Dune Sea and the farmer in the hoverchair telling him a name he had never heard and a month he had not been there for. He had not seen his mother's face from the age of nine to the age of nineteen, and the second time he saw it, it was in the dark of a tent, and that was all. That was the whole of it. There was no slot in it where a letter could have gone.

And yet the Obi-Wan in the next room had handed him one as if it were the fourth or fortieth, and had known which relay was down.

He turned the envelope over. The seal had been broken and resealed by the courier office, as they did, and under the Temple's stamp there was a smaller stamp in violet ink that said Lars Homestead, Anchorhead District, and then, because the courier droids were thorough, Tatooine, Arkanis Sector, Outer Rim.

He opened it.

He read it twice. The second time he read it with his thumb on the word Owen, as though it might be a mistake in the printing.

Then he sat with it in his lap and looked at the wall.

The light from the window had moved perhaps a hand's width across the floor before he thought to breathe properly, and when he did, it hurt, and he let it. His chest was narrow and his ribs showed when he stretched and the breath went into them with no hiss and no count and no machine deciding how much, and he had been back ten days and it still surprised him, every time.

Nobody asks whose I am.

She had never said that to him. She could not have. She had been dead before she was ever in a position to say it, in the only version of her life he had been given, and he had learned that she had been free from a stranger, after, over a grave. Here was the sentence in her own hand, in violet-stamped flimsi, in his lap. I have not got used to it and I do not mean to. That was her exactly. He could hear it. He had not heard her voice in forty years and he could hear it as though she were at the basin in the next room with her back to him.

He put the letter down on the blanket very carefully, as if it could bruise, and got the datapad out from under the pillow.


The courier office kept an archive. He had not known that either, but it was there when he looked, under his own name, a folder he had never opened, with a scan of every letter that had passed through the Temple in either direction. Hers were in one column. His were in the other.

Eleven of his. Nine of hers. Three years.

He opened the first of his because he had to start somewhere.

It was a scan of a flimsi sheet, and the sheet had been written on with a stylus pressed too hard, so that the back of each stroke showed through faintly as a shadow, and the lines went uphill across the page the way they had always gone uphill, and he had been twelve.

And a gap, where a boy had not known what came after then I'm, and had put the stylus down and picked it up again, because the next line was in a different pressure, lighter, as if written later or by someone who had been crying.

Tell him he has to be good to you. Tell him I'm a Jedi and I'll know.

He put the datapad face down on the blanket.

It was the handwriting. He had thought it would be the words, and the words were bad enough, but it was the handwriting he could not stand: the forward lean of it, the loop on the k of his own name, the way the lines climbed. It was the hand of a boy who pressed too hard because he was afraid the words would not stay. He had two hands now and they were both that boy's, and he looked at the right one, which had held the letter, and it was the same hand, with the same callus on the second finger from the stylus and the same scar on the knuckle from Watto's shop. The mechanical one had never written anything. It had signed things. It had closed on things. It had not, in twenty-three years, formed a single letter that leaned forward.

He turned the datapad back over, because he was not a coward, and opened the last one. The eleventh. The one she kept in her apron.

He had not come.

In the only life he knew, he had come three years from now, in a borrowed Naboo ship, with a Senator he was in love with and a dream he had been having for a month, and he had found the farm and the farmer and the stepbrother and the empty chair, and he had taken a speeder bike out into the Jundland Wastes alone, and

The tent. The dark of it, and the smell, and her face when he got the hood off, the lines in it that had not been there in Watto's yard, the cord marks at her wrists gone black at the edges, her mouth working at the first half of his name and not finding the second. The weight of her head when it stopped holding itself up. The sound outside that went on and on, and was him.

He was on his feet. He did not remember standing. His right hand had closed on the flimsi letter and crushed it, and he opened the hand and looked at the creases he had put into her sentences and made himself breathe, in, hold, out, the count the suit had done for him, which he could do himself now, which he had a mouth and lungs and a throat to do himself.

He smoothed the letter out on his knee. The creases stayed.

She was alive. She was in Anchorhead buying flour with nobody asking whose she was. Owen had fixed the fence. The vaporators were giving a third more. None of it was his, and all of it was in his lap, and the boy who had written I'll come was dead on a bank of black rock a long way from here and also sitting on this bed in his body, and he did not know which one of them was going to write back.


He wrote back.

It took him until the light had gone orange in the window and Obi-Wan had knocked once and said "Supper, when you want it," and gone away again without opening the door, which was another thing to think about, later.

The trouble was the hand. He sat with the stylus and the courier's flimsi and found that his hand wanted to make the letters square and fast and separate, the way the glove had signed things, and the first line he wrote looked like an order of the day and he tore it off. The second line was in the boy's forward lean, carefully done, and it was so obviously careful that it looked forged, which it was. He tore that off too. He had been a boy for ten days now and could do the voice and the walk and the sulk through the bond and had sat across a desk from the man who had made him and lied in his face, and he could not write Mum on a piece of flimsi without his hand going strange.

The third sheet he wrote on without looking, as the boy would have, pressing too hard.

Mum. Yours came late because the relay was down, Master Obi-Wan says, and I read it twice and then once more. Tell Cliegg the condensers were never the trouble. Tell Owen the south fence is probably fine but I'll know if it isn't.

He stopped. He had written I'll know because the boy had written it, and it was a boy's threat and a boy's joke, and in the only other version of this he had not known. He had not known anything. He had been nineteen and in love and had not known for a month, and she had been in the tent for a month, and

I have thought about you every day.

He looked at it. It was true and it was not a sentence a fifteen-year-old wrote to his mother, or not one who had letters from her in a folder, who had Cliegg and Owen and the vaporators as ordinary news. It was a sentence from the other side of a grave. He drew a line through it, and then he had a letter with a line through a sentence in it, and she would see the line and wonder, because she always saw.

He tore that sheet off as well. There were three on the floor now. His hand was shaking very slightly and he was annoyed with it, and the annoyance was useful; he was fifteen and annoyed and his shoulder hurt, and he wrote from there.

Mum. Yours came late, the relay was down. Tell Cliegg the condensers were never the trouble but I'm glad they're giving more. He sealed it before he could read it again, and put it on the table by the door where the courier would collect it in the morning, and stood with his hand flat on it for a moment as if it might get up and leave on its own.

Then he went back to the bed and sat down with the datapad and did the thing he had been putting off since the first morning, since the shaking hand in Drallig's hall, since the word letters had come out of Obi-Wan's mouth in a cell in the Chancellor's outer office as if it were nothing.

He started the list.


The question of what to write it in took longer than the first entries.

Basic was out; anyone could read Basic. Huttese was out; Obi-Wan read Huttese, badly, and the Chancellor read it well and would not say so. The Temple's own cipher for Padawan journals was a children's thing that Jocasta Nu could unpick in her sleep. He needed something that nobody in this building could read, that nobody in the Senate could read, that could sit face up on a table in front of the one man in the galaxy who had walked through his mind every tenth day for six years, and mean nothing.

He had one.

He had made it in the chamber on the Executor, in the hours when the mask was off and the air was a pressure and a smell, for the things he had not wanted the Emperor's slicers to find in his terminal when they went through it, as they went through it. It was a substitution cipher keyed to a number he had never told anyone and that no one alive now would know: the serial of the first respirator unit, the one from the surgical table, which he had read upside down on the housing while they were fitting it and had never afterwards been able to stop seeing. It was not a clever cipher. It had never needed to be clever. It had needed to be his, and it was the most entirely his thing he owned, because he had made it in a room where nobody else could breathe.

Nobody alive could read it. Nobody alive had ever read it. The man who had built it was dead on a floor in a reactor shaft, and the boy with the stylus had never had a respirator and never would, if he could manage it, and between them they knew the key.

He set it up. The datapad did not care what the characters meant; it took them as a private script and showed him his own ugly symbols on the screen, and he put the title at the top the way he had put a title on the first one, forty years and a death away.

Things that are different.

Letters. Three years. Eleven of mine, nine of hers. Obi-Wan handed it to me like a ration bar. In the other one there were none and I did not see her for ten years.

Then, because a list was no use without the thing it was a list for, he wrote the three theories, and numbered them, and did not like any of them.

One. My memory is rotten. Twenty-three years in the suit, and what the Emperor did to it, and what I did to it myself. The letters happened and I do not remember them because I chose not to. Obi-Wan was always like this and I chose not to see it. The child is a child.

Two. This was never mine. Close, but not the same: a place where the small things were always slightly different, where my master always let me write home, where a Togruta youngling always held her blade backwards, and I have been put into it by whatever put me here, and the differences are not clues; they are only the furniture of a house that is not my house.

Three. Someone else is here.

He looked at the third one for a long time. The symbols were ugly and meant nothing, and he knew what they meant.

He did not add anything to it. He put the datapad in the pocket inside his tunic, where it sat against his ribs, and went out and ate the supper Obi-Wan had left under a cover, cold, standing up, while Obi-Wan read in the other chair and did not ask him anything, and did not say my friend, and watched him eat with an expression Anakin did not turn round to see.


The trouble with the first theory was that it could not be argued with. If his memory was rotten, then his memory of his memory was rotten, and there was no floor to stand on. So he went to find a floor.

The Archives after the evening meal were nearly empty, which was the point. The lamps were down to their night level and the long galleries of blue-lit shelving went away into the dark on either side like the inside of a ship, and the busts of the Lost Twenty stood along the central aisle in their niches with the light coming up under their chins, and he did not look at the eleventh one. He had looked at that face across a kneeling man's blade once and did not need to see it in bronze.

He took a terminal at the end of the second gallery, where the chair was low enough for a youngling and his knees came up, and he put his hand on the reader and the Archive took his print and his rank and gave him what a Padawan was allowed.

He tested himself against the record.

Carnelion IV first, because he had put it in the boy's letter and the boy had been twelve, and because it was the only mission in his first life he could date to a tenday. The file came up. The dates were the dates. The crash was where he remembered the crash, the two sides had the names he remembered, the report was in Obi-Wan's careful hand with the paragraph about the Padawan's conduct that had been, he now saw at fifteen and forty-five together, a good deal kinder than the conduct. There was a line about the boy having raised the question of leaving the Order, and the Council's note that the Chancellor had taken an interest. All as it was. He felt nothing about it, which was its own information.

His own file next, which he was allowed to see in part. Skywalker, Anakin. He did not get up. He sat with his hand on the reader, in the blue dark, and thought, idly, the way one touches a bruise to see if it is still there, of the one word he could put into this machine that he knew for a certainty was not in it.

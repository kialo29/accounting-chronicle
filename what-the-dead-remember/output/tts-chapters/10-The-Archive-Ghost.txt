Part Two. The Watchers


Chapter Ten. The Archive Ghost.


Before first bell, in a hall with the lights at half, Anakin Skywalker showed the battlemaster the stroke that had nearly killed a boy.

He showed it three times, because Drallig asked three times. The first was slow, as Drallig wanted it, the training saber coming up over the right shoulder and down in the single economical line that did not belong to any form Drallig taught, one hand on the hilt and the other hand empty and open at his side, as if it were holding something that was not there. The second was at speed. The third Drallig stopped halfway with his own blade and held there, the two humming rods crossed at head height, and looked at the boy's wrist, and then at the boy's face, and said nothing at all, which was worse than the questions had been.

"Again tomorrow," Drallig said, when he let him go. "Before the bell. Until the Crucible leaves."

"Yes, Master."

"You are tired."

"I didn't sleep well, Master."

"No," Drallig said. "I imagine not."

He went out through the hall with his arm aching to the shoulder and his tunic stuck to his back, because a boy's body sweated when a man's did not, and because he had been awake until the third hour on the roof with a bird that had not flown. The corridors were still dim. Initiates were not up. There was only one thing in him this morning, and it had been there since the night he went down to the courier archive and found a line in a log that said his master had looked for a planet that did not exist.

He had told himself for ten days that he would leave it. Under the line on the list, in the dead man's cipher, he had written not yet. He had meant it.

He washed his face in the refectory refresher, so that he would look like a boy who had washed his face, and went to the Archives.


The Archives at the first hour were as he remembered them from both of his lives, which was the trouble with the Archives. The light came down blue from the high windows and lay along the stacks like water in a channel. The air had the dry, kept smell of old flimsi and older stone. Along the central aisle the bronze busts of the Lost Twenty stood on their plinths, looking at nothing, and the twentieth of them, the newest, had a long patrician face and a beard that had been cast in metal six years ago and had not changed since, and Anakin did not look at it as he passed.

Jocasta Nu was at her desk. She was always at her desk; he had never in either life come into the Archives and not found her there, with her white hair pinned and her hands folded and her eyes already on the door, as if the door were a reference she had filed and expected to be consulted.

"Padawan Skywalker," she said. "That is twice in a tenday. Your master's needs are extensive."

"Yes, Master Nu."

"And at this hour."

"I have a penance with Master Drallig before the bell," he said. "I thought I would come after. Before class."

"So I had heard." Her eyes went over him once, neutral as a scanner. "Half the Temple had heard by supper. What may I give you?"

The courtesy of librarians, he thought. He had forgotten it. It was the precise opposite of the way Palpatine gave a thing: you were told exactly what you might have and given all of it, and told exactly what you might not and given none, and the line was never pretended to be anywhere other than where it was.

"My master's training materials," he said. "The access record. He has me working through the Soresu commentaries he assigned and I wanted to see which he'd pulled himself, so I don't repeat him."

"That is a sensible thing to want." She was already reaching for a stylus. "A Padawan may see the access record for his own master's assigned training materials, as those are in part his. The general query log he may not see. It belongs to the Order, not to any one Jedi in it, and certainly not to any one Jedi's apprentice." She said it without weight, as a fact about the building, like the height of the windows. "If Master Kenobi wishes to know what he himself has consulted, he is welcome to come and ask me, and I shall tell him, because I remember."

"Yes, Master Nu."

"Terminal nine. I have opened it for you." She paused. "Padawan."

"Master?"

"You were here on the tenth night after the eighth hour, checking something for your master, you told my assistant. I mention it only so that you know it is written down." Her mouth moved, not quite a smile. "Everything is written down. That is what the Archives are. Nothing that was ever filed here has been lost, in a thousand years, and I will not have it begin with a Padawan who has not slept."

He thanked her. He went to terminal nine. He sat down in a chair that was slightly too large for him, in front of a screen whose top edge he had to lift his chin to see, and he put his two flesh hands on the input surface, and for a moment, before he had decided anything, they were still.

Nothing that was ever filed here has been lost.

She had said it with pride, and she was wrong. There was a planet missing from her Archives, cut out six years ago by a man with a Council member's codes, so cleanly that the index had closed over the wound without a scar, and the woman at the desk did not know, and would not know for four years.

He pulled up the Soresu commentaries. He looked at them for the length of time a boy would look at them. Then he went down.


The Archive's index was old. It had been old when he was nine, and old when his master had stood at this desk in another life asking about a planet, and very old in the year the Temple changed hands and someone was needed who understood how the Jedi had kept their records, so that the records could be used to find the Jedi.

He had been that someone. There had been a season after the Temple fell when the index was the most useful thing in the building: every Knight ever assigned a mission, every initiate ever found on a world the Order had visited, all of it filed and kept, because the Jedi had believed memory was a virtue. He had walked the architecture of it in the dark with the respirator going and the stacks dead around him, and learned which partitions the droids had built and which maintenance accounts had been made in the second century of the building and never retired, because librarians did not retire things.

His hands knew it. That was what frightened him. He had not decided; he had only sat down with the Soresu commentaries open and let his hands do what they had done before, and they had gone, in the way a man's feet go to a door in a house he grew up in, down past the public index to the catalogue layer, and through the catalogue layer by a gap the droids had left when the Temple was young, and into a maintenance account that had the plain, unguarded name of a librarian dead nine hundred years. It took four minutes. It made no sound. The screen above his chin went on showing the Soresu commentaries to anyone who walked past, and beneath them, in a window the size of his palm, the general query log of the Jedi Archives opened to him like a hand.

He looked at his own hands on the surface, narrow and knuckly and a boy's. Anyone watching would see a fifteen-year-old frowning at a text. The competence was thirty-five years old and had been learned in a building full of the dead, and it was in a child's fingers, and the fingers were not even cold.

The stacks dark and the red light coming through the high windows, and a woman with white hair standing in the aisle with her blade lit, and her voice, not frightened, telling him he was not permitted.

He breathed. It was still a surprise, to breathe. He put the memory down where he put things and went into the log.

KENOBI, O. He knew the line already. He read the whole record this time: two years ago, in the cold third of twenty-eight B B Y, from terminal four, the one nearest the desk and nearest Jocasta Nu, which was either carelessness or a kind of honesty. String: Kamino. Duration before abandonment: forty minutes. Result: null. The index noted, with the flatness of a thing that did not know it had been cut, that no world of that name was recorded in the Archives of the Jedi Order.

Forty minutes. Obi-Wan had sat at the terminal nearest the desk for forty minutes looking for a planet that was not there, and then, he supposed, got up and asked her, and been told it did not exist.

Anakin scrolled. He meant only to see what came after. What came after was four days later.

KENOBI, O. Terminal four. String: Sifo-Dyas. Result: filled.

He did not move for some time.

The record Obi-Wan had been given was the public one. Master Sifo-Dyas, of the High Council; a seer, by reputation, with visions that troubled the Council; lost in thirty-two B B Y on a diplomatic errand, presumed dead; a memorial entry three lines long. Obi-Wan had read it for eleven minutes. Then nothing else that week.

The boy whose body he wore had known that name in the way a boy knew the names of the dead. It had been said in corridors the year he came to the Temple, in the voice adults used for a thing that was over: Master Sifo-Dyas had gone out and not come back. He was not among the Lost Twenty, because he had not left; he was only lost. To a boy, that was all it was. A Master who vanished.

The man inside the boy knew the rest. He knew that Sifo-Dyas had gone to Kamino and ordered an army in the Order's name, and that his friend Dooku had learned of it, and had the Pykes of Oba Diah bring the ship down, and had used Sifo-Dyas's own codes to cut the planet from this room. He knew what the army was for, because he had led it up the Temple stair. And he knew that in the year his master had typed those two names into terminal four, the number of living beings who could have put them side by side was perhaps five, and that none of them was Obi-Wan Kenobi, aged twenty-nine, Jedi Knight.

He tried anyway.

Obi-Wan had been researching Dooku. That was a sane thing for a man to do in twenty-eight B B Y: his master's master had left the Order and was writing pamphlets, and Sifo-Dyas had been Dooku's friend, their names were in the same histories, one led to the other. That nearly held. And Kamino, a word from Dex, perhaps, a Besalisk's story over a counter, looked up out of idleness and found absent and forgotten. That held too, on its own.

What did not hold was the four days. He looked at the two dates until the numbers lost their meaning and became only shapes, and the shapes said what they had said the first time: a man had looked for a planet that had been erased, and when he did not find it, he had looked for the man whose codes had erased it, and he had done both in a single week, two years ago, at the terminal nearest the librarian's desk, as if he had nothing to hide.

Or as if he had learned, somewhere, that the best place to hide a thing was in the open.

Who taught you that, Anakin thought, and did not know, for a moment, whether he was asking his master or himself.


He should have closed the window then. He had more than he had come for. Jocasta Nu's assistant went past the end of the aisle with a cart of flimsi and did not look at him, and a sane man would have shut the maintenance account and gone to Vos-Kerrick's class and thought about it there.

He did not close it. He could not have said why, except that it was the same thing that had taken him to the courier archive on the tenth night and to the roof on the nineteenth: the sense that there was a second line in the log, if he only scrolled far enough.

The initiate files were in another partition. He had used them once to find out which children had been sent to which worlds for safekeeping, and the memory of that came up in him so fast that he had to put both hands flat on the surface and wait for it to go by.

TANO, Ahsoka. Togruta. Found on Shili by Master Plo Koon in thirty-three B B Y, aged three, in a village whose name the file gave and he did not read; brought to the Temple the same month; Bear Clan, then Hawkbat. The clan notes were the sort of thing clan notes were. Intense. Does not cry when the others cry. Prefers to watch. Grip faults persistent; corrected; recurs. A note from Drallig's assistant in the year she was seven, about the saber, in a tone of bafflement. A note from Master Sen, two years ago, that she was older than her years, and knows it, and would prefer we did not.

And between them, filed from the Halls of Healing, under a healer's initials he did not recognise, dated thirty-one B B Y.

Initiate Tano, aged five, brought by clan-mother. Persistent night terrors, some weeks' duration; wakes without crying out; will not describe content. Physically well. Asked this healer, unprompted, "whether a dream can be a memory." Did not pursue it when answered. Rest and observation advised. No recurrence.

He read it twice. The second time he read it under his breath, the way a boy might sound out a difficult word, and the sound of it in his own fifteen-year-old voice was so wrong that he stopped halfway.

Whether a dream can be a memory.

The floor of the Archives was stone and had been stone for a thousand years and it did not move. He was aware of this. He was aware of his heart, which was a boy's heart and went fast on nothing, going fast now, and of his hands, which had gone cold after all. He was aware that a healer had written no recurrence because the child had never said it again, and that a child who never said a thing again had either forgotten it or learned not to.

She was ten. She had been five. Children asked things. He had asked Qui-Gon, at nine, in the belly of a ship, whether the stars were the same ones from the other side, and it had been a boy's question. Children had night terrors. Children were brought to the Temple at three from mothers, and dreamed of them, and could not say what they dreamed because they did not have the words yet, and a quick child might ask whether the thing she saw at night had happened to her, because it felt like it, and a kind healer would say no and the child would nod and not ask again. That held entirely. There was nothing in it.

He had put a training saber into her hand in a hall nineteen days ago and she had turned it round, and she had looked at his hand and not at his face, and he had told himself she was a youngling with a grip fault, and he told himself so now, and the healer's note sat in its window the size of his palm and said whether a dream can be a memory in a voice he could not place and could not stop hearing.

He closed it. He closed the partition and the maintenance account and the gap the droids had left, in reverse order, carefully, the way a man leaves a house, and when the screen above his chin showed only Soresu, he sat for a while longer with his hands in his lap, so that if anyone looked they would see a boy who had finished his reading and was tired.

On his way out Jocasta Nu looked up from her desk.

"Did you find what your master needed?"

"Yes, Master Nu," Anakin said. "Thank you."

"Good." She returned to her stylus. "Sleep, Padawan. The commentaries will keep. We keep everything."


He did not go to Vos-Kerrick's class. He went up instead, to a maintenance landing on the east side of the Tranquillity Spire that nobody used, with a window the width of his shoulders, and sat on the sill with his boots against the far wall and took out the datapad.

He made a second page. He had the habit from another life, and had hated it then too: laying a thing out in lines so that it could be looked at without being felt. Four lines. He wrote them in the cipher and they looked like a maintenance schedule, and he read them back and every one of them was hateful.

One. Obi-Wan. The man who had searched for Kamino and then for the one name that explained it, who said my friend and touched his shoulder and sat on the edge of his bed at night and had never once let the Chancellor keep him for a whole day. If that man had come back, from wherever, then he had spent four years raising a boy he knew would burn, and had looked at that boy every morning across a teapot with the lava bank behind his eyes, and had been kind. Anakin could not bear it. He would rather have had the Obi-Wan who was only Obi-Wan. He would rather, he found, have been the only one.

Two. The child. A ten-year-old with her blade the wrong way round, who had asked at five whether a dream could be a memory. If the child had come back, then she had come from somewhere he had sent her, because there was nowhere she could have come from where he had not been the thing in the dark; and she had lived seven years in a body that could not reach the shelves, among children, telling nobody. He did not know which was worse: that she might have seen him through the cracked mask and come back to this Temple to find him waiting with a training saber, or that she might simply be a girl who had bad dreams at five, and he was building a cathedral on her.

Three. Both. He did not write anything under that. He looked at the word for a long time.

Four. Neither. Twenty-three years in the suit, and a memory that had been through a fire. Perhaps Obi-Wan had always been this warm and he had not noticed, being a boy and then a monster. Perhaps the healer's note was a healer's note. Perhaps the bell he had heard on the first night was his own skull ringing, and the bird on the roof was a bird, and he was alone in a child's body in a building that would burn, and mad. He had thought that the kindest of the four when he wrote it. Reading it back, it was the one that frightened him most, because it was the only one in which nobody was coming.

He put the datapad away. Below the window the Processional Way was filling with the morning, and the small sand-coloured shapes of a clan were being walked to a class, two by two, and he watched them until he could not tell which clan it was and then stopped.

He had to know. He could not ask. The thing was to find a question that could be asked without being one.

Something small, he thought. Something safe. Something a boy might say.


The Chancellor's standing audience fell that afternoon, as it fell every tenth day, and Anakin went, as he had gone on the tenth. Palpatine was pleasant; he talked of the Outer Rim conference and the Senate's tempers and the price of patience, and somewhere between the second cup and the third he tried the wall again, with a lighter hand than before, a thumb laid against a door to learn whether it was still a door. It held. Palpatine only smiled, pleased rather than troubled, like a man who finds the lock he has been trying has been oiled, and Anakin carried that smile back across the Senate District and liked it less than he would have liked anger.

They ate in the quarters that night, because Obi-Wan had been in the Council tower again until the seventh hour and came back with the look he had when he had been asked things, and said he would rather not sit in the refectory and be looked at, and did Anakin mind. Anakin did not mind. He brought the trays up himself from the kitchens, and set the common room's one table, and when Obi-Wan came out of his room with his hair damp and his belt off he had the tea going, which made Obi-Wan stop in the doorway.

"Well," Obi-Wan said. "I shall have to arrange for you to be in disgrace more often."

"It's only tea, Master."

"It is only tea, which you have made unasked, on the day after I watched you put a blade a finger from a boy's throat." He sat. "Sit down; you look like something Drallig has been using to sand a floor."

"He makes me do it slowly," Anakin said. "Three times. He doesn't say anything."

"He said a good deal to me." Obi-Wan poured. He did it as he had always done it, in both lives, with the care of a man for whom the pouring was the point. "None of it you need to hear tonight. Eat."

They ate. It was ordinary: the two of them at the small table with the city going violet in the window, the way Obi-Wan put his fork down to say a thing and picked it up when he had said it, the warmth that came down the thinned bond like heat from a wall with a fire on the other side. It had been like this once. Anakin had been too young to know it was precious and then too old to remember it had been real. He sat in it now, across from a man who might be a stranger wearing his master's face, and ate stew, and waited, and the moment came, as moments did, in the ordinary place.

"Vos-Kerrick had us on the Outer Rim conference," he said. Vos-Kerrick had, three days ago; it was true enough to be checked. "The neutrals. She said the Duchess of Mandalore has refused to send anyone at all, not even an observer. Half the class thought that was cowardice."

He said it to the stew, with a boy's lazy, half-interested scorn, the voice of someone trying on an opinion from class to see if it fit, and he let the name go past as if it were any name, a title out of a politics lesson, the kind of thing a fifteen-year-old repeated without having thought about it.

Obi-Wan did not stop eating. He did not look up quickly or slowly; he looked up in the time it took to look up. He finished the mouthful. He reached for his tea.

"Who told you about Satine?" he said.

Mildly. Entirely mildly. The way he might have said, who told you Drallig was in a temper. There was interest in it, and a very small amount of something that might have been amusement, and nothing else, and the bond carried the same low warmth it had carried through the stew, and Anakin sat with his spoon in his hand and could not read it.

He could not read it. He had been reading men's faces for forty years, across tables and the decks of ships and through a lens, and Obi-Wan's face in the violet light told him only that he had once known a woman called Satine and was faintly surprised to hear her title in his Padawan's mouth. It was the face of a man of thirty-one who had spent a year on Mandalore at twenty and did not talk about it. It was also, exactly, the face of a man of fifty-seven who had lost her, and had heard his Padawan say her name in another life, and had decided in the time it took to finish a mouthful to give nothing away. Both faces were the same face. That had been the whole problem since the first morning.

"Master Vos-Kerrick," Anakin said. "In class. Why? Do you know her?"

"I did," Obi-Wan said. "A long time ago, on a mission with Qui-Gon, when I was a few years older than you are. She was not a coward then. I doubt she has become one." He drank. "You may tell half the class so, if you like, with my compliments, and the other half that neutrality is a great deal harder work than war. Though I would not say so in front of Vos-Kerrick. She has opinions."

"Yes, Master."

"Eat your supper."

He ate his supper. The conversation went elsewhere, to the Crucible, to Huyang, who Obi-Wan said would like Anakin and never admit it, to whether Yoda wanted Anakin useful on Ilum or merely out of the Temple for a tenday, which Obi-Wan did not answer. The city went from violet to black. Obi-Wan cleared the trays and would not let him help, and said, "Early, tomorrow. Drallig," and went to his room, and the bond went slack and easy, the way it did when he slept, or wished to seem to.

Anakin sat at the empty table with the cold teapot.

He had not learned anything. The test had been small and safe and had told him what a small, safe test told, which was nothing, and he had known it would, and what he was sitting with now was not the nothing. It was that for the length of a mouthful of stew, with a name in the air between them and his master's eyes coming up to meet his at exactly the ordinary speed, he had felt his pulse go in his throat the way it had gone in a cockpit once, in the half-second before a turn, and he had liked it.

He had liked it. He had sat across from the man who taught him to hold a cup and played a card against him and waited to see if the man would play one back, and whether or not Obi-Wan had, it had been a game, two players and a table, the oldest game he knew, and some part of him that had been a boy long before it was anything else had leaned into it with its whole weight and thought, again. He had been something that did not play, for a long time. And here was the pleasure, undiminished, exactly where he had left it at fifteen, in a body that was fifteen, and he could not tell any longer which of them was enjoying the lie.

The shame came after, as it always had, slower and heavier and more honest.

He went to his room and did not light the lamp. Two days, he thought, lying on his back with his hands on his chest, where the respirator plate had been. Two days, and then the Crucible, and a world of ice, and six small figures going down into a cave at first light, the last of them a girl who had asked a healer at five whether a dream could be a memory, and Yoda watching him, and his master left behind in the Temple with a name Anakin had put into his mouth at supper, and nothing on his face at all.

Who told you about Satine.

He would be asked that again, he thought, in some other form, by someone. He would have to decide, before Ilum, whether he was going to go on enjoying it.

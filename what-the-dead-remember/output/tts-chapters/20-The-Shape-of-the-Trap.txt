Chapter Twenty. The Shape of the Trap.


Ahsoka Tano had learned, in seven years, that the safest place to hide a question was inside a smaller one.

"Does the Council sit every day, Master Plo? Even on rest days?"

She had asked that one three tendays ago, with her boots not reaching the floor from the refectory bench and Kessa Ruun beside her chewing with her mouth open, and Master Plo had answered it the way he answered everything she asked him: fully, gently, as though a child's curiosity were worth the whole of his attention. Most days, he had said. And she had nodded and asked whether the chairs were comfortable, and he had laughed behind the mask, and the first question had gone into the ground like a seed.

Since then she had learned that if she asked him what is the Council doing today in a voice that wanted a story, he would give her the shape of one, with the names left in. He did not think of it as telling her anything. He thought of it as talking to little 'Soka, who had asked him at five whether a dream could be true and had never since asked him anything that mattered. She had spent seven years making sure that nothing she said to him mattered, so that now, when it did, he would not hear the difference.

She hated it more than she had expected to.

"Master Plo's here," Kessa said, and kicked her under the table, and Ahsoka looked up with a child's face already on.

He came down the refectory between the long benches with his hands folded into his sleeves, tall in the way all adults were tall to her now, the breath-mask hissing softly. Master Imari Sen, at the end of the table with her grey hair pinned and her own hands in her own sleeves, inclined her head to him; the clan-mother had been told, some weeks ago, to keep an eye on the Tano child, and had been keeping it, and Ahsoka had made sure there was nothing for it to find.

"Hawkbat Clan," Plo said. "Initiate Ruun. Initiate Tano." He sat on the end of the bench, and the children laughed because it tipped. "What are you learning this tenday?"

Kessa told him, at length. Ahsoka let her, and ate her porridge, and listened with half of herself to Kessa and with the other half to the thin, faint thing at the back of her mind that was not Kessa and not Plo.

It was always there now, since the nineteenth night, a thread no wider than a hair, running from her to a boy somewhere above her and to the west. It carried no words; it carried a mood, and this morning the mood was flat and careful, which meant he was in front of someone. She did not pull on it. A Padawan bonded to a youngling would be seen by any Master who happened to look, and so they kept it so faint that some mornings she had to be very still to be sure it was still there, and some mornings she was not sure, and that was worse than she had told him.

"And you, 'Soka?"

"I'm learning to lose at sabers," she said. "Tam says I hold it wrong."

"Do you?"

"Yes, Master."

"Then I expect you will keep doing it." The mask turned towards her and stayed a moment: the silence that meant he had decided, again, not to name what he saw. "The Council has a long day. I came to see your faces first."

"What's the Council doing?" Kessa said, because Kessa always said it, because Ahsoka had made sure, over three tendays, that Kessa always said it.

"Reports," said Master Plo. "Petitions. A great deal of sitting." He rose. "Eat your breakfast, Hawkbats."

That was all, that morning. Nothing had come through the drop for days except the one thing, and the one thing she had read eleven times.


It had been under the moss two nights after the reception, and she had carried it back inside her tunic and read it under the blanket with the glow-stick they were not supposed to have, moving the letters seven places back in her head.

She called me Ani. I was cold to her and it worked and I do not want to talk about it. D. touched. The wall held. One second over the top before I shut it. He felt it. P. watched him feel it. Three for three. S.

She had lain in the dark with the flimsi against her chest and had felt, for the second line, nothing she could use: a woman who had been kind to her and right about everything and had died for it had stood on a terrace and said Ani to a boy who had already killed her once, and he had been cold, and it had worked. She could not help him with that.

It was the next line that had kept her awake.

One second over the top. She knew what one second was. She had stood within arm's length of Count Dooku once, his curved hilt lit and his face entirely without expression, and had understood, the way one understands cold, that he missed nothing because he had never needed to. One second of Anakin's hatred over the wall was not a slip. It was a signature. Dooku had read it, and turned and reported it to the other one, and the other one had watched him read it and been pleased.

Three for three, he had written, as if it were a joke. She had made it a joke, in her own note, so that he could walk into that room. She had told him don't look at her like she's already dead and he had looked at her like a stranger instead, which was the same thing in better clothes, and now there were two old men in the city who knew something was wrong with Obi-Wan Kenobi's Padawan, and knew it in the only grammar they trusted.

She had put the note back in the lining of her belt with the Togruti list and had slept, in the end, because the body she wore was ten and did what it was told.


The next clan up had the Room of a Thousand Fountains the hour before Hawkbat did, and so Hawkbat always passed them coming out: twenty children of twelve and thirteen in tunics a size larger, walking in the straight quiet line the older clans were proud of, the one at the back always turning to shush whoever had laughed.

The one at the back was always Barriss Offee.

Ahsoka had never managed to arrange her life so that she did not see her. The Temple was too small: a floor apart in the dormitories, an hour apart in the fountain room, two benches apart in the refectory. A thin Mirialan girl with the black diamond tattoos still new across the bridge of her nose, hood up even indoors, earnest in the way the Temple produced earnestness, forever helping. If a younger child dropped a stylus Barriss picked it up. If a younger child cried, Barriss crouched and asked why, in a low voice, as if crying were a reasonable thing that could be discussed. She was, by every measure Ahsoka had, a good child. She would be a good healer, with careful hands.

Ahsoka went past her in the doorway and Barriss, catching her eye, smiled at her, the small polite smile the older clans gave the younger, and Ahsoka smiled back, because a child would, and went on into the green wet air with the smile stuck to her face like something she had walked into.

A hangar, and the noise coming first, and then the heat; a corridor with the lights gone red; a cell with a bench; a circle of chairs and the faces in them deciding; a woman's hand on her shoulder on the Temple steps that she had stepped out from under, saying sorry, and meaning a different thing by it than they had heard.

Twelve. The girl was twelve. She had never set a charge or lied under oath or put a saber in someone else's hand and watched them hang for it. She was a child who picked up styluses. And some part of Ahsoka that was not a child stood in the doorway every tenday and looked at her thin back and did the sum, and could not make it come out. If a thing has not happened, it is not a crime. If it has happened to you, it is not nothing. There was no column for a debt owed by a person who did not yet exist, and she could not decide, had not been able to decide in seven years, whether to hate a twelve-year-old, and the not deciding was its own small daily wound.

She found her place on the moss by the long pool and sat down to meditate with the others, and did not.


The water fight was Tam Dorreth's fault, as most things were.

Master Sen was called to the door by a Knight with a datapad, and in the forty seconds she was gone, Tam, who could not be still for forty seconds if the Order depended on it, flicked a handful of the long pool at Wix. The Ithorian boy made the double-throated honk that meant outrage and put both long hands in. Ilyo squealed. Brannick Sel, who was Nautolan and could not be made wetter, waded in up to his knees on principle and began using his tunic hem as a bucket.

Kessa grabbed Ahsoka by the wrist and pulled, laughing, and Ahsoka let herself be pulled.

She had meant to stand at the edge and get a little wet, as a careful child with a reputation for intensity would. But the pool was cold and Tam was shouting Tano's on my side, and the body she wore was ten years old and strong for its age and had not been allowed to do anything with its strength for a very long time. She found she had put a wave over Tam's head that made him splutter and sit down. She found, which was stranger, that she was laughing, out loud, a child's laugh, from somewhere she had not known was still there.

For perhaps a minute the Room of a Thousand Fountains was full of nothing but wet shrieking children, sand-coloured tunics gone dark to the chest, Ilyo's green head bobbing, Wix honking, Brannick standing in the middle like a small benevolent island, and the waterfalls going on overhead as they had for a thousand years.

Then she saw the Guard.

He was on the gallery above the east door, masked, the yellow pike upright, as still as the stone. Her body stopped before her mind did; the laugh died and her hands came out of the water, and for a moment she simply stood, hip-deep, and looked at him. Then the mind caught up and did its checking. Human height, not taller. Shoulders set wide. Not the one. The one stood on the Council tower doors now, tall and narrow and patient; this was some other Knight behind some other mask, watching younglings make fools of themselves in the sacred quiet.

It did not matter. Her hands were shaking under the water and she pressed them against her thighs until they stopped.

"Hawkbat Clan."

Master Sen had not raised her voice. The clan went silent in a single wet second, twenty children turning to the doorway with water running out of their boots, and Master Sen stood with her hands in her sleeves and looked at them for a long time.

"Out," she said. "All of you. On the moss. Sit."

They sat. The Guard on the gallery had not moved.

"This room," said Master Sen, "is where Masters of this Order have come to be quiet for longer than the Senate has existed." Ahsoka did not look at the third waterfall, or at the stretch of grass forty paces from it where she had been told a man she had never seen fight would one day be killed by a boy she had. "You have been given it an hour each day because we trust you. Initiate Dorreth."

"Yes, Master."

"Was it you?"

Tam, to his credit, said yes.

"Then you will clean the long pool filters this evening instead of supper. The rest of you will change, and come back, and sit this hour again, in silence, and the Guard will watch you do it. Initiate Tano."

Ahsoka looked up. Had to: she was sitting, the clan-mother was standing, and the distance was absurd.

"I am surprised at you," Master Sen said, not unkindly, and Ahsoka felt it go into her like a needle, because the woman meant you are usually so careful, and that was the whole of her disguise, and she had let it slip for a minute because a boy named Tam had wanted her on his side.

"Yes, Master," she said. "I'm sorry."

Barriss Offee's clan was passing along the gallery. Barriss saw twenty dripping children on the moss, and her face did something small and sympathetic, and she took the towel from her belt, the one the older clans carried, and leant over the rail and dropped it to the nearest child, who was Ahsoka.

It landed in her lap, warm, smelling of soap. She held it a moment and then, because a child would, used it.


They played Jedi and Sith on the grass terrace after the second meditation, with their tunics still damp, because it was the game the clan always played when they had been in trouble and needed to be heroes again.

The rules were a child's rules. The Jedi were everyone. The Sith was one, and stood at the top of the terrace steps with a broken-off bit of trellis for a blade, and the Jedi came up the steps and killed him, over and over, with sound effects, and when he had been killed enough he lay down and they stood on him and said something noble. Nobody ever wanted to be the Sith. Being the Sith meant losing, with twenty children watching.

"Tano," said Tam, who had forgiven her for the wave. "Tano can be the Sith. She holds it backwards anyway."

"That's not fair," Kessa said, loyally, and then, less loyally, "but she does."

"I don't want to," Ahsoka said, in the voice.

"You're always on my side," Tam said. "Be on nobody's side for once."

So she went up the steps with the trellis reversed in her small fist and turned at the top, and twenty children spread out below her with their sticks up and their faces lit, and she stood there, a girl of ten with her lekku barely past her shoulders and her boots full of water, and looked down at them.

She knew how to do this. That was the thing. She knew exactly how a Sith stood at the top of a flight of steps and looked down at the people coming to kill him. She had seen it from below: the mask and the long black and the red light making a line down the centre of it, the stillness that was not calm but something underneath calm, the patience of a thing that has already won. She could show them. She could drop the reversed stick into the grip her hand wanted and set her feet the way she had set them on a stone floor in a temple older than this one, and only she would know whose feet she was standing in.

She played it as a child would. She growled. She said I am the dark side in a stupid deep voice and Ilyo shrieked with delight and they came up the steps at her. She blocked a few, let a few through. Tam got her in the ribs and shouted got you, and Wix got her in the back, and then Kessa came in with her eyes screwed shut and hit her on the shoulder, and Ahsoka lay down on the grass and died.

They stood on her. Not hard. Children's boots and the smell of wet grass, the terrace lamps coming on against the dusk, and Tam with his foot on her stomach saying something noble about the light, which he had got from a holo.

She lay with her eyes open and looked past him at the first stars and thought about a floor that had tilted and a ceiling that had come down, and a hand she had not been able to hold onto, and a voice through the cracked place in a mask saying her name the way it had been said when she was fourteen. The children cheered. She lay there a moment longer than the game needed, and nobody noticed except the small boy sitting at the edge of the terrace with his knees drawn up, who was not in Hawkbat Clan and should not have been there at all.


He followed her down the stairs afterwards, the way he always did, a floor behind, pretending he was not.

Caleb Dume was seven and in Bear Clan and had attached himself to her some months ago for no reason she had ever found. He was a scrap of a boy with dark hair in his eyes and a habit of standing too close to things he wanted to look at, and he had already, at seven, the stubborn set of the jaw she remembered on a man leaning on the rail of a freighter with a mask over eyes that did not work, telling her it would be fine.

She did not slow down for him. It was the one thing she could do for him: not look.

The lip of the fourth step from the bottom was worn into a shallow dip that every child in the Temple learned the hard way. She heard him miss it, the catch of breath and the scuff, and turned, already moving, and had him before he hit the landing: a hand under one arm and a hand in the back of his tunic, his whole weight swinging against her, which was nothing, a seven-year-old's weight, and then he was standing on the step below her with his face very surprised.

She should have let go then.

She did not. She stood on the stairs in the last of the light with a boy of seven held against her by the shoulders, his hair against her chin, the small quick heart going under her hand, and went on holding him, and could not have said why except that he was warm and he was there and both of his eyes worked. She had seen him walk into a thing he could not see with his hand out. He had been very calm. She had hated him a little for it.

"You can let go," Caleb said into her tunic. "I'm not falling."

She let go.

He stood back and looked up at her with his head on one side, as if she were a problem in astrogation.

"Why are you crying?"

"I'm not," she said. She was not. She put her hand to her face to be sure and the hand came away dry. "Watch the fourth step. Everyone misses it."

"I know," said Caleb, with dignity, and went on down ahead of her, and missed it again.


Master Plo came back at the evening hour, which he did not always do, and found her on the bench outside the Hawkbat dorm with a datapad on her knees, the maths she was pretending to do.

"You are in disgrace, I hear," he said, and sat. "I gather there was a wave."

"Tam started it."

"Tam always starts it. You do not always finish it." He let the mask hiss a while. Then, because he was tired, and because she had taught him over three tendays that it was what the child asked, he said, before she could: "You will want to know what the Council did today."

She made her face want to know. It was not hard. "What did it do?"

"Sat," he said. "Mostly. There is a thing on Oba Diah. Do you know where that is?"

"No, Master." She did.

"Nor should you. It is a bad place in a bad stretch of space, run by people called the Pyke Syndicate, who are not very nice." His voice had the mild, telling-a-story cadence she had spent seven years earning. "A Republic survey team went there to map a mineral ridge, eleven of them, with permits, and the Pykes have decided the permits were not sufficient and are holding them against a fee. The Senate does not pay fees. So the Council is sending someone to negotiate."

The bond at the back of her mind was faint and flat. He was somewhere above her, with his Master, perhaps, who was being told this now in some other voice.

"Who?" she said.

"Master Kenobi. He is good at it. The request came through the Chancellor's office this morning and a Senator asked for him by name, which is not usual, but he is the hero of Naboo and Senators like a name they have heard." Plo turned the mask towards her, and the goggles gave her her own small face back. "It is a routine thing, 'Soka. A tenday there and back. He will be home before Tam has finished the filters."

She said something a child would say, something about whether Master Kenobi was frightened of the Pykes, and he laughed, and said that Master Kenobi was not frightened of anything so far as anyone had been able to determine, and put his hand briefly on her montrals, and went.

She sat with the datapad on her knees and looked at nothing.

She knew what the Pykes were.

She knew them from Mandalore, the domes under the sun and the helmets in the streets, where the thing in the shadows had worn the Pykes as one of its three hands, with Black Sun and Death Watch for the other two, and had taken a planet with them. She had stood in a throne room full of their lanky grey-faced soldiers with a man in white armour at her shoulder whose name she said to herself now, deliberately, Rex, because no rule in this skin forbade her to think it. The Pykes had been bought that time. They were always bought. And she knew them from after, from the years when she had been a voice on a hidden channel and not a Jedi at all, when the Syndicate's reputation came to her over and over on other people's reports: they did not take hostages for fees. They took hostages for clients. A Pyke did not hold eleven Republic surveyors for the Senate's money; the Senate had no money a Pyke could not get easier elsewhere. A Pyke held eleven people because somebody had paid him to make sure that a Jedi came.

Which Jedi did not need asking. A Senator had asked for him by name. Through the Chancellor's office. Twelve days after an old man in a Count's coat had put his fingers into the mind of that Jedi's Padawan and found something there that frightened him.

She had, she realised, stopped breathing, and made herself start.

Isolate the master. Hold him on the far side of the map with people who could not be reasoned with, and the boy was alone in the Temple with a thin bond and a tenth-day audience and nobody between him and the office with the red walls. Obi-Wan would not see it, because he had been given the shape of a thing he did well, and because he had already decided, for reasons of his own that she could not read, to be the one who went where the Chancellor's requests pointed rather than let the boy go. Plo thought it was routine. He had used the word, and she had sat beside him with her boots not touching the ground and said nothing, because she was ten.

The thread at the back of her mind was as faint as it had ever been. She could not tell whether he knew. She could not have told him down it if she tried; it was mood, not words, and she would not have dared put this mood into it with Masters on every floor.

So she went to bed and lay in it, and counted the breaths of nineteen sleeping children, and when the count was right she got up.


The east door of the Room of a Thousand Fountains was never locked, because nothing in the Temple was locked against its own, which had been, once, the whole trouble. She went along the dark gallery with her boots in her hand and counted the waterfalls by their sound. The third. Down the wet steps, along the ledge where the spray made the air cold enough to hurt, to the place where the moss was thicker than it should be, and under the moss the box.

She had written the flimsi on the bench with the maths in front of her, shifting each letter seven back as she went, so that if Master Sen had looked over her shoulder she would have seen a child's scrawl in an alphabet that did not quite make sense.

Unfolded, by the glow-stick, it was two lines.

Oba Diah. Pykes. It's a trap. Who asked for him by name?

S.

She put it in the box and the box under the moss and crouched there a moment with the water going past a hand's breadth away, loud enough to cover anything, loud enough that a man could die forty paces from here with the whole Temple awake and not be heard.

Then she went back along the ledge and into the dorm and lay down beside Kessa, who did not wake, and put her hand flat on her chest where the thread was, faint, faint, a boy's mood somewhere above her in the dark, and could not tell, and would not know until the next night and the next count whether the box would be empty or full.

She lay awake and waited for it, and the Temple, which was not on fire, went on being quiet around her.

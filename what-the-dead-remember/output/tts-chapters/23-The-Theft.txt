Chapter Twenty-Three. The Theft.


The bond tore open while Anakin Skywalker had a seven-year-old's wrist in his hand.

It was the sixth morning since the north-bay courier had gone, and Drallig had Bear Clan in the small hall, fourteen of them in sand-coloured tunics with training sabers too long for their arms, and Anakin was walking the line the way he had walked it every tenday for months, correcting grips.

Two days ago, at the tenth-day audience, with Obi-Wan's place empty beside him, the Chancellor had pressed so gently: how hard it must be for a boy, alone in the Temple with his master gone, and how readily the Council leaves him behind when it has weightier things to weigh. Anakin had given him what he wanted to see, a boy's worry, a sleeve twisted in the fingers, and kept the wall up behind it, every course of it. He had walked out under the old man's kind regard and had not quite stopped feeling it since.

The child in front of him was a Nautolan girl with her thumb wrapped over her fingers. He had just turned her hand so the thumb lay along the hilt, and said there, like that, in the voice he used for children, when something went into his left side below the ribs, one hard blow of pain, and the hall went white.

He was on one knee. He did not remember going down. The girl was looking at him with her saber still up in the corrected grip, and the floor of the hall was very close and very cold, and the pain was not his. He knew that at once, the way one knows a voice in the next room. It had come down the bond, the thin held-ajar thread he had kept for five months between himself and the man who had left on the courier, like a blade down a wire, and behind it the presence at the other end had gone out.

Not gone. Going. A lamp with the fuel running low.

No.

He took hold of it. There was nothing to take hold of; a bond is a direction one's attention leans. He leaned the whole weight of what he was, every year of it, the years nobody in the hall could see, down the thread towards the dark it ran into, and he pulled. He pulled the way one hauls a rope with a man overboard on the end of it. He felt the thread take the weight and hold, and he felt, far off, something at the other end answer, faint and bewildered, as a man answers who is being hauled up out of sleep by the collar.

"Skywalker."

Drallig. Boots beside him. The battlemaster's shadow across the mat, and fourteen children gone absolutely silent, which children of seven never are.

"Cramp," Anakin said. His voice came out a boy's: high, cracked at the edge. Good. "Sorry, Master. Stood up too fast. I'm, " He put a hand flat on the mat and pushed and got up, and the hall tilted and righted. The Nautolan girl had lowered her saber. "I'm fine."

Drallig looked at him: at his face, which Anakin could feel had gone the colour of the mat, and at his left hand, which had gone to his own side under the ribs and was pressed there as if to keep something in.

A hall of water and green, a tall man with grey in his hair turning on a path with his blade already lit, the look on his face not fear but recognition, and the stroke that took him, 

"Go to the Halls," Drallig said. "If you are going to be sick, do it there."

"Yes, Master."

He walked. He did not run; running would be remembered. He walked out of the small hall past the rack of training sabers and past the Temple Guard at the gallery stair, whose mask turned to follow him, and into the corridor, and some minutes after the first the pain came again, a second pulse, higher, in the left shoulder, and with it the long drop of a body going down onto stone, and he put his back to the wall and stood there with the thread in his hands, pulling, pulling, because the presence had dimmed again and he could not tell any more whether he was holding it or only holding the place it had been.


She found him through the other bond first.

It was so faint he almost did not feel it: a touch at the back of the mind, the thinnest line they had made together nineteen nights after the Crucible came home and kept at a whisper since, because a Padawan bonded to a youngling was a thing the Council would see from across the Temple. It did not carry words. It carried her, for a second: a small hard attention, like a hand laid flat on his chest, and a direction. The fountains. Then it was gone, or she had let it go, because even now she would not risk it being seen.

He went down. He went down the service stair and through the east door of the Room of a Thousand Fountains, counting waterfalls because he could not think and the count was something to do, one, two, and behind the third there was the ledge, and the moss, and the box under the moss that held their notes, and standing on the ledge with her boots in the spray was a girl of ten in a sand-coloured tunic with her lekku dark with wet.

She had come from class. She had the chalk of the mechanics room on her sleeve. She was small; she was always smaller than he expected; she had to look up at him and she did, with her blue eyes gone very steady, and he saw that she had felt it too, not the pain, the pain was his, but what the pain had done to him, and that she had already understood it.

"He's hit," Anakin said. "Side and shoulder. He fell. He's, I can feel him, he's there, but he's going, Ahsoka, he's, "

"Go," she said.

A child's voice. A child's pitch, a child's size, the water coming off the ledge behind her in a sheet; and in the word, under it, the thing he had heard in that voice on a hundred bridges and in the Kalevala sky over Mandalore, the flat certainty of a commander who has looked at the board and sees one move. She was not asking him what he thought. She had been thinking since the moment she felt him go down, and she had finished.

"I can't get a ship."

"Yes, you can." She stepped off the ledge. The spray had made her markings stand out white on white. "I'll get you the ship. You get him. Walk with me and don't talk unless I talk to you first. You're a Padawan who's been sent for. That's your whole face. Can you do that?"

"Yes."

"Then do it."

She went first. He went after her, a boy following a youngling through the green, with his master dying at the end of a thread in his chest and a ten-year-old girl laying out the sequence in a murmur ahead of him as they walked: the guard, the hangar officer, the log; what she would say and what he would not; where he would stand.

He had seen her do this before. He had seen her do it with a battalion.


The Temple Guard at the hangar level stood where it always stood, at the turn of the corridor where the lifts let out, with its pike grounded. Anakin had never once seen it move, and it moved now, a quarter-turn, because a youngling of ten was out of class on a hangar corridor at midmorning with a Padawan behind her.

"Initiate."

"Master Plo sent me, Guard." She did not stop walking; she slowed, which was better. "I was in mechanics. He came to the door and sent me to find Padawan Skywalker and bring him to the north bay, because Master Kenobi's courier's still out and he wants the other one checked before it's rostered. I'm meant to go straight back after." A breath, a child's breath, slightly too fast. "He said to say it was his word if anyone asked."

The mask considered her. It considered Anakin, who stood with his hands at his sides and his face arranged into the face of a boy who had been sent for and did not know why and was slightly anxious about it, which was not difficult, because his left side was on fire and the thread in his chest had gone quiet for the last forty paces.

"Go on, then."

"Thank you, Guard."

They went on. He waited until the turn.

"Plo will say he didn't."

"Plo will say he didn't in about four hours, when someone thinks to ask him, and by then you're gone and it's my problem, and I've been a problem since I was five. Don't look at the guard. Look at the floor. You're a Padawan who's been sent for."

Knight Sabo Thane was in the north bay with a datapad and a crew chief and the second courier, the sister of the one that had taken Obi-Wan, a Temple shuttle with the port repulsor cowling off and a droid under it. Thane was a big quiet man with a scar through one eyebrow who had lent a fifteen-year-old his own two-seater and asked no questions, and Anakin found now that he could not look at him.

Ahsoka could.

"Knight Thane. Master Plo sent me down." The straight face. It was not a blank face; a blank face is a lie on its own. It was the face of a child delivering a message she only half understood and was proud to have been given. "He wants the courier flown before it's rostered, because of the coil, and he wants Padawan Skywalker to fly it, because he's rated on the type and the knights on the roster are all in Council this morning. He says it's on the log already. He says he's sorry it's short notice."

Thane looked at the datapad. Anakin, looking at the floor, saw the pause, and in the pause he saw what she had done, because he had done it himself a thousand times in another body: the log had already been sliced. Somewhere between the mechanics room and the fountains she had put her small hands on a crew chief's terminal she had been visiting for a tenday on a permission slip, and entered a maintenance flight, port repulsor test, post-repair, pilot to be assigned, under the crew chief's own code, dated this morning. The lie fitted the record because the record had been built to fit the lie. She had not come to him until the paper was done.

"Huh," said Thane. "He didn't comm me."

"He's in Council, Knight Thane."

"He is." Thane scratched the scar with the stylus. "Skywalker. You rated on this?"

"Yes, Master." The boy's register. "Master Kenobi had me do the type conversion last year."

Which was true, as it happened. She had chosen a lie with a true floor under it.

"Six hours," Thane said. "Up through the Temple corridor, out to the test box over the Works, run the coil through its range, bring it back. No lanes. No stunts. If Plo Koon wants it done today he can have it done today, but if that coil throws a fault I want it on the pad in ten minutes, not in Monument Plaza. Clear?"

"Yes, Master."

"Chief, button it up."

The crew chief went to put the cowling on. Thane went to sign the pad. Ahsoka stood with her hands folded in front of her the way initiates were taught to stand before a knight, looking up, small and wet-lekku'd and ten, and Anakin stood behind her looking at the floor of the north bay and understood two things, one after the other.

She was better at this than he remembered. He had taught her to lie to Separatist commanders over a comm and she had been good. But this had not come from him. This had come from seven years of being a child in a building full of people trained to feel a lie from across a room, from crying on schedule and losing on purpose and asking a healer whether a dream could be a memory and learning from the healer's face never to ask again. She had made herself an instrument for exactly this, in a body of ten, and the instrument was flawless.

And it was horrible to watch. That was the second thing. A child of ten standing in front of a decent man and lying to him with a face that would never be caught, and doing it for him, because she had been a child for seven years in order to be ready for this and this was what ready looked like.

He wanted to put his hand on her head. He did not.

"Padawan." Thane held out the pad. "Thumb."

He gave it his thumb.

"Go on, Initiate. Back to class. Tell Master Plo it's done."

"Yes, Knight Thane."

She went. She did not look back at Anakin, because a youngling would not; and down the faint line she had made between them, as she crossed the bay, there came one thing, hard and brief, the shape of a hand on his chest, pushing. Then the line was gone, and she was through the door, and he was climbing into a courier shuttle under the eyes of a man he had just robbed.


The seat was too big.

It had been set for a knight: the back a hand's breadth too far from the yoke, the pan a hand's breadth too low for a boy's narrow backside to reach the pedals square. He pulled it forward on the rail with a jerk that made the crew chief look up, and sat, and the harness came down over his shoulders set for a chest that was not his, and he cinched it, and it was still loose, and he cinched it again. His hands on the yoke were a boy's hands. Thin wrists; the right one with the knob of bone that had not finished growing. He looked at them for a half-second as if they belonged to someone else, which they did, and then he stopped looking, because the hands knew what they were doing and the boy did not need to.

The courier came up off the pad and out through the Temple's air corridor into the morning.

He flew it the way the other hands flew. On speeder day he had flown Thane's two-seater like a talented child, with too much of everything, and it had been a joy. This was the thing under the joy. The shuttle went up through the Temple corridor at exactly the speed the corridor allowed, and at the top, where the lanes began and Thane's test box lay off to the east over the Works, it did not turn east. It went up, through the gaps between the lanes, through three stacked levels of morning traffic in a line so clean that nobody saw it until it was gone, and out of the top of the city into the dark with the harness biting his shoulders and the thread in his chest so faint he had to hold his breath to feel it.

The navicomputer asked where he was going.

He told it: Oba Diah, Kessel sector. It began to chew. It would take ninety seconds; it was a Temple courier with a Temple courier's brain. He did not have ninety seconds. He had the route in his head already, because he had flown it, not in this body, not from this end, but he had flown it, the Kessel approaches and the Pyke lanes and the dust-shoals off Oba Diah's third moon, in a ship with a black hull and a reason to go to Pyke space that he did not let himself look at now; and the solution was there the way a word is there, and he put it in by hand, the exit vector and the mass-shadow margins and the three corrections, and the navicomputer was still chewing when he pulled the lever. The Temple's filed route to Oba Diah took a courier five days, because it ran the cleared lanes, past every checkpoint and customs beacon where a Jedi ship was expected to be seen and logged; his took hours, because it ran through the gaps nobody had filed, where nobody flying meant to be seen at all.

The stars went to lines. The lines went to the blue tunnel. The courier settled into the long hum.

Then there was nothing to do.

He sat in the too-big seat with his hands on a yoke that had nothing to steer, and in his chest was the thread, and it was so thin. It had been thin for months because he had made it thin, because closed would have been a confession and open a worse one. Now it was all there was. He held it. Sometimes it pulsed, faint, a man's presence turning over in the dark, and sometimes for a minute or five it did not, and in those minutes he sat absolutely still, not breathing, and the terror was simple and total and had one shape: that it would stop. That he would feel it stop, in a borrowed ship with a boy's hands on the controls, and know, and still have four hours to fly.

He had been the other thing.

That was what came, in the fourth hour. He had come down out of the dark onto a world whose name he could not now remember and found a Jedi bleeding in a stairwell, a woman who had held off three Inquisitors for a day and a night and had nothing left, and he had stood over her with the respirator going and done what he had come to do. He had been the dark at the end of someone's thread, on a hundred worlds, for twenty-three years, and every one of those someones had had a Padawan somewhere, or a master, or a child, who felt it go.

He sat with that. It did not resolve anything.

Then he made the vow, and he made it in a man's voice in his head, without ceremony, because it was not a thing that needed ceremony. Nobody has him. Not the Pykes. Not the Count. Not the old man. Not the Council, afterwards, with their questions. Not me. Whatever I was, I am going to be it for this, and then we will see.

The thread pulsed. He let out his breath.


Oba Diah came up out of the tunnel black.

The spires of the Pyke city stood up out of the night in a cluster on their mesa like a handful of needles pushed through cloth, lit along their edges in a cold violet that did not reach the ground, and the courier came down through the upper air with its lights off and its transponder saying what Ahsoka had made it say, a maintenance flight, a nothing, and nobody challenges a nothing. He found the tower from the bond. The spires were a hundred and the tower was one of them, but the thread ran to it as a plumb-line runs to the ground, and he brought the shuttle down on the pad at the tower's foot with the thread going straight up through the floor of the cockpit into the dark above him.

The pad was empty. There was no Temple ship on it. The six hours had run out, then, and the pilot had done as he was told; Anakin filed that and did not look at it.

He undid the harness. He stood up out of the too-big seat and his legs were a boy's legs and they shook once, and then they stopped shaking, because the other thing had come up through them and taken them, and he went down the ramp with his saber in his right hand, unlit, and walked across the pad to the door.

There were two Pykes on the door.

They were taller than him by a head and a half, narrow bodies and small heads inside breathing masks and long grey hands on long rifles, and they had time to see a boy in a Jedi tunic crossing the pad, and one of them had time to raise the rifle. The blade came on. He did not deflect the shot. He went under it and through the man with one stroke, upward, no turn of the wrist, no second movement; and the second Pyke had got his rifle up and the stroke that took the rifle took the hands with it, and the Pyke went down screaming behind his mask and Anakin stepped over him and put his left hand on the door, and the door, which was locked and barred and ten centimetres of plasteel, went inward off its track and down the corridor beyond like a sheet of flimsi in a wind.

The cargo lift at the end of the corridor went up the inside of the tower through forty levels with a sound like a long breath, and he stood in it with the blade down at his side and the thread going up and up and then, at the fortieth level, sideways.

The doors opened on the round room.

He had it in a glance, because the other thing took rooms in a glance: the drains in the floor; the narrow windows; the gantry running round the wall at two storeys with its cargo lines hanging; the stair up to it; six droids on the gantry with their heads swinging round at the sound of the lift, Trade Federation skeletons with their hull-codes unscrubbed, plain as a manifest, the same numbers Obi-Wan must have read from this gantry; four Pykes on the floor with rifles; one more Pyke standing apart by the far window in a better coat, not Lom, Lom was not in the room, Lom had gone the way a man goes when he has paid for a thing and does not want to watch it done. And blood. Blood on the floor under the gantry in a smear that went from the centre of the room across the drains to the foot of the stair, and under the stair, in the wedge of dark where the treads met the floor, a shape.

The thread went to the shape. It pulsed, once.

He went across the room.

He did not go round. There was a Pyke between him and the stair, and the Pyke fired, twice, and the blade took the first bolt back into the Pyke's chest and the second into the window behind him and he did not slow down, because there was no need to slow down, because the stroke that came next was already in the arm. It was a single stroke, overhand, one-handed, the whole weight of the shoulder behind it and nothing in the wrist: a heavy, falling, economical thing with no beauty in it. It had been made in a body that could not turn fast and did not need to, and it came out of a boy's narrow frame as if the frame did not matter, and the Pyke's rifle and the Pyke's arm and a good deal of the Pyke went to the floor in pieces.

The droids opened up from the gantry.

He walked through it. He took the stair in four strides and came up onto the gantry among them and did not spin or turn or do anything a Padawan would have done; he went down the line of them the way a man goes down a row of something that needs cutting, and each stroke was the same stroke, down, through, and each droid came apart at the waist with its top half talking and its bottom half standing, and the top halves went over the rail one after another onto the floor of the round room.

Below him a Pyke had got to the foot of the stair with a rifle, between him and the shape in the dark.

Anakin looked at him.

He did not raise his hand. His left hand was at his side where it had been, with the fingers curled a little, as a man's fingers curl when he is holding something that is not there. The Pyke's rifle came up and then stopped coming up. The Pyke's long throat, inside the collar of the mask, drew in. His boots left the floor. He went up, a hand's breadth, two, with his heels kicking and the rifle falling out of his hands and clattering down into the drain, and the small grey hands went to the collar and found nothing there to pull at; and Anakin stood on the gantry and felt, under the fingers of a hand that was not there, the long cartilage of a Pyke's windpipe and the narrow bones either side of it, and knew exactly how much more it would take, and did not take it. He let go when the rifle was in the drain. The Pyke fell and lay with his legs moving and his breath coming in a sound that was wrong, and did not get up, and Anakin had already stopped looking at him.

The two who were left broke. One got to the lift. The other ran at him, which was brave and pointless, and Anakin put him into the curved wall of the room with a gesture so small that it was less a gesture than a glance, and the Pyke hit the wall at the height of a man's head and came down it and lay still.

Then the room was quiet, and in the quiet he looked up.

There was a gallery above the gantry. It was not a gallery, it was a maintenance walk, a strip of grating under the roof where the cargo lines ran to their drums, and standing on it with one hand on a line and the other hand loose at his side was a man in Mandalorian armour, silver-blue, with the T of the visor pointed straight down at him. He was not armed. That is, his pistols were in their holsters; he had not drawn; he had not moved while six droids and five Pykes came apart under him. On the side of the helmet above the ear a small light burned red.

Recording.

Anakin knew the helmet, by a route that did not pass through thought: the rain on Kamino running off that silver-blue; the arena at Geonosis; a boy with the same face holding that helmet in both hands and looking into it. And the helmet knew something; the visor did not move, but the man's weight shifted on the grating, the slight settling of someone who has just revised a price.

He could reach him. Even up there. He could feel the line the man's hand was on and the drum it ran to and the way the grating hung from the roof.

Under the stair the thread pulsed, and was fainter than it had been.

He let him go. He turned his back on Jango Fett, which was a thing he would not have done in any other body, and went down the stair, and behind him the jetpack lit with a cough and a roar and the roof vent banged and the roar went up and away and was gone, taking the red light with it into the Oba Diah night. He did not look. He was already on his knees in the blood under the stair.


Obi-Wan was on his right side with his back to the treads and his knees drawn up, the way a man lies who has crawled somewhere and has nothing left for arranging himself. The robe was gone. The tunic was black with blood from the left armpit to the belt. There was a burn through the left shoulder from front to back that had cauterised itself the way blaster wounds do, and a second one lower, under the ribs on the left side, that had not, that had gone through muscle and out and was bleeding, slow and dark, into the drain.

Anakin put two fingers to his throat.

There. Thready, fast, a pulse like something trying to get out. Alive. The eyes were half open and did not focus; the beard was wet; the lips moved and made nothing. Alive.

"I've got you," Anakin said. He heard his own voice say it and it was not a boy's voice and he did not care. "I've got you. Don't move. Don't help."

On the floor three metres away, in a smear of its own, was a lightsaber. He knew the hilt. He had seen it last in another life, lit, on the bank of a lava river, in the hand of a man who had just taken his legs; he reached, and it came across the floor to his hand, and he clipped it to his belt beside his own without looking at it, and put his hands under Obi-Wan's shoulder and hip to lift him.

He could not lift him.

He had known he could not, and had not let himself know it, and now it was simply a fact: he was fifteen, and narrow, and the man under the stair was thirty-one and heavy with muscle and heavier with blood, and when Anakin got his arms under him and heaved, the man came up a hand's width and his own knees went out and they both went down into the drain, and Obi-Wan made a sound that was the worst sound he had heard in this life or the other one.

"All right," Anakin said. "All right. The other way, then."

He did it the other way.

He had lifted men before with the thing he was, by the throat and by the chest and off the floors of ships, to break them, and he had never once lifted a man to carry him, and he found that it was the same and not the same. He knelt in the blood with his hands open over the body under the stair, and the Force came up through him and under Obi-Wan the way water comes up under a boat, and the body rose. It rose flat, as it lay, on its side with its knees up, a hand's breadth off the floor, then two, with the blood running off the tunic hem and pattering onto the drain grille beneath. He stood. He kept one hand on Obi-Wan's chest, not for the Force, the Force did not need his hand, but because he needed to feel the chest go up and down. He walked backwards. He walked backwards across the round room with his master floating beside him at the height of his own hip like a boat on a tow-line, and at the lift door Obi-Wan's boots struck the frame and swung, and Anakin turned him with a thought, the way one turns a sheet of flimsi over, and brought him in.

It was absurd. He knew it was absurd. A boy with a braid behind his ear, walking backwards into a cargo lift with his left hand out flat, and a bearded man in a soaked tunic hanging in the air beside him with his head lolling and his blood dripping onto the lift floor at intervals, tap, tap. In any other life it would have been the thing one tells afterwards. He did not think about any other life at all.

On the lower level the loading bay doors were open and there were people in it. Deshan Orr had all eleven of her surveyors packed into a cargo lift that had jammed on a crate, its door stuck a hand's breadth short of open, and a Pyke's rifle in her hands that she had nowhere to point. Anakin freed it in passing, the crate out of the track and the door off its stop with a glance, and did not break step; he said, "The Pyke's ship's on the lower pad; take it; go now," and Orr looked at the boy and at the man in the air beside him and lowered the rifle and said nothing at all, which was the right thing to say. Eleven faces saw a boy and a floating man, and nothing else in the world.


The courier's medkit was under the co-pilot's seat where Temple couriers kept them. He had it open before Obi-Wan was down.

He put him on the deck behind the seats because there was nowhere else, on his right side as he had lain under the stair, with the lifted leg dropping last and the whole weight of him settling onto the plating with a sound Anakin felt in his own back. Then his hands went to work, and he watched them.

They were a boy's hands. Small; the right wrist with its knob of bone. They did not shake. They cut the tunic from hem to armpit with the kit's shears and peeled it back off the wound under the ribs and looked, and what they knew, they knew from a place the boy had never been. He knew what a blaster bolt did going through a body because maintenance droids had explained it to him, in detail, in a chamber with the air hissing, about his own; he knew the smell of cauterised muscle, which was the smell of the chamber; he knew that a wound that bled slow and dark would stop with pressure and one that bled bright would not; that the shoulder was the lesser wound because the bolt had sealed it, and that the side was the one that would kill, not from the hole but from the slow draining, a man emptying himself an hour at a time into a drain on a Pyke tower. The hands tore the bacta patch and laid it over the exit wound and pressed. They laid the second over the entry. They put the field dressing round the whole of the ribs, over both, and pulled it tight enough that Obi-Wan's breath caught, and tied it, and put the heel of one hand flat on the dressing and leaned on it with a boy's slight weight, and held.

He looked up from the hands and Obi-Wan was looking at him.

The eyes had come back. Not far; they were the eyes of a man a long way under water looking up at the light. But they were on him, on his face, and they had been on him for some time; and Anakin understood, with the heel of his hand on his master's ribs, that they had been open in the round room. That the man under the stair had seen the stair, and the gantry, and the stroke, and the Pyke go up by the throat without a hand on him. That he had seen all of it. That he was seeing it now.

Obi-Wan's lips moved. Nothing came.

"Don't," Anakin said. "Don't talk. We're going."

He went forward and did not sit; there was no time for the seat. He stood at the yoke with one knee on the pan and took the courier up off the pad with the ramp still closing and the Pykes' violet spires going past the ports and the thread no longer a thread but a man on the deck three metres behind him, breathing, and he put the exit vector in by hand again, Coruscant, the Temple, the Halls, and the stars went to lines and the lines went to blue, and he let go of the yoke and went back and got down on his knees on the deck.

The dressing was holding. The dark had stopped coming through it. He put his hand on it anyway, flat, and the chest under it went up, and down, and up, and the eyes stayed on him, and he did not think about anything; he was not thinking; it came out of him in a man's voice, the one he used when he was alone, low and quiet and worn, without any decision at all.

"Stay with me, Ben."

Chapter Eighteen. The Reception, Part Two.


She did not come to him at once. She came down the steps into the Naboo, who closed around her the way water closes over a thing dropped into it, and Anakin stood where Obi-Wan's hand held him and watched her be a Senator for a while, because that was what she was, and because he could not yet have walked.

He knew the moves. He had watched her make them for three years of war from the edges of rooms like this one, in a Knight's robes, pretending to be bored. The hand to the elderly delegate from Theed, both of hers over his one. The half-turn so that the woman standing behind him was not left out of it. The listening, which was real, and which was the thing people could not forgive her for, because it meant they had been heard and could not pretend afterwards that they had not. She was twenty. She had done all of it at fourteen with the paint on, and now she did it with her face bare, and it was better, and it was worse, because he could see her doing it.

"Breathe," Obi-Wan said, very quietly, beside his ear. "You've stopped."

He breathed. The body took the air gratefully and he hated it a little for that.

Across the gallery the Chancellor had not moved from between Amedda and Moore, and his glass was still full, and his kind face was turned towards the Naboo with the expression of a man watching his own garden come into flower. He did not look at Anakin. He did not need to. Dooku was somewhere behind the pillars to the east with the Serenno party and Anakin did not look for him either, because looking for him would have been a thing to be seen.

Then she lifted her head over the Theed delegate's shoulder and found him.

He had known she would. He had stood here once and felt it as a thing happening to the front of his body, and it happened to the front of his body now: her eyes crossing the room, catching, stopping, and the small change in her face that was not a smile yet but was the place a smile came from. She said something to the delegate. She touched his arm. She came through the Naboo and out the other side with Typho a pace behind her like a shadow that had been issued a sidearm, and the gallery, which had been pretending not to watch her all evening, pretended harder.

He had perhaps nine seconds. He used them to put the fifteen-year-old on over himself like the formal tunic with the sleeves a finger short: the shoulders back and a little too far, the chin up, the hands not knowing where to be. He let the face flush. It was going to flush anyway.

Don't look at her like she's already dead.

She stopped in front of him. She looked up, because she had to now; he had six centimetres on her, which was absurd, which was wrong, which was the single stupidest thing in a galaxy that had been very stupid to him, and her eyes went up his face and found the braid behind his right ear and came back down, and she said, with a laugh under it that she did not let out, "Ani? My goodness, you've grown."

Here was where the boy had said something about her having grown too, and gone red, and made a mess of it, and she had been kind. He had the whole sentence in his mouth. It had been waiting there for thirty years.

He bowed. The Temple bow, hands at the belt, the exact depth a Padawan gives a member of the Senate, not a degree lower.

"Senator Amidala," he said. His voice held. "It's an honour to see you again."


Everything he did not say to her stood behind him in a line, and he could feel it there, the way you feel people in a corridor behind a door.

You will be twenty-seven. It will be night, and far from here, and there will be a medical droid, and you will say my name to a man who is not me.

"Master Kenobi," she said, turning, and Obi-Wan had let go of Anakin's elbow in the second before she reached them and was now a Jedi Master at a reception, dry and courteous, with his hands in his sleeves. "I was told you'd both be here. I didn't quite believe it. The Temple doesn't usually lend its people out for a party."

"The Chancellor was persuasive," Obi-Wan said, "and it is your anniversary, Senator, not a party. We were told so most firmly at the door."

She laughed then, properly, and it was the laugh, the one he had heard across a lake. Anakin stood inside the boy's body and let the laugh go through him and out the other side and did not move.

"You look well," she said to Obi-Wan. "You look exactly the same. Does the Order issue that, or is it practice?"

"Practice. A great deal of it, and tea." He inclined his head. "You, if I may say so, look like a Senator who has read every line of a very long bill and intends to make the committee read them too."

"Oh, they've read them. That's what they're frightened of." She said it lightly and glanced, without appearing to, towards the east pillars, where a heavy blue Twi'lek was laughing too loudly with a trade delegation. Then she turned back, and the glance she gave Anakin was the one she had given the boy in the Queen's ship on the way to Coruscant, when he had been cold and she had put the blanket round him: curious, kind, a little amused, a person deciding to take an interest. "And you. Padawan Skywalker. Six years. I kept expecting to hear about you. Every time a Jedi was in the news I looked for the name."

The arena. The chains. Your shoulder laid open and you climbing the pillar anyway, and the look you gave me from the top of it, which was not fear.

"The Temple doesn't put Padawans in the news, Senator."

"No, I gathered." She tilted her head. "I did try. Did you know? After Naboo, after the funeral, after you'd all gone, I wrote to you. To the Temple. I addressed it to Ani Skywalker, because I didn't know what else to call you, and I expect that's why it came back." She was smiling at herself. "A very polite note. Something about Padawan learners and private correspondence. I wrote again a year later, properly, Padawan Skywalker, care of Master Kenobi. I never heard. I assumed it had been lost, or that you'd been told not to, or that you'd forgotten. You were nine. It's allowed."

He had not known. He went looking for it and it was not there: not in the boy's memory, not in the man's, not in the twenty-three years of the suit when he had gone through every record of her he could reach, twice. Two letters. A year apart. The first stamped and returned by some clerk in the Temple's correspondence office who had done his job. The second to Master Kenobi, care of, and never heard of again.

He did not look at Obi-Wan. Obi-Wan did not look at him. The bond, thin as a thread, gave nothing at all, which was in itself a thing.

"The Temple is strict about letters," Anakin said.

"So I learned."

"It isn't personal. They don't want us, " he found the word a fifteen-year-old would use, ", pulled. Outward. By people."

"By people," she repeated, and he saw her hear it, and file it, and not know yet what it was.

You in white on a terrace at Varykino with the lake behind you and the light coming off the water onto your throat, saying no to me, and meaning it, and the way you stood when you said it.

"That's a shame," she said. "I'd have liked to know how you were."

"I was well, Senator. Thank you."


He saw the moment she registered it, because he had been watching for it, because he had decided, somewhere between her second sentence and her third, what he was going to do.

It was not a decision in the way the Council meant the word. It arrived whole. He was standing in a boy's body in front of the only person in the galaxy he had ever been entirely happy beside, and she was looking at him the way she had looked at him at the start, before the start, with the beginning of a fondness in her that was the fondness of a kind woman for a child she had once helped, and he saw the whole road from that look to a table on Polis Massa laid out in front of him like a landing approach, every beacon lit. It began here. It began with her liking him. Every version of her death he could construct began with her liking him, and then more than liking, and then the lake, and then the arena, and then a sunset on a terrace with two droids for witnesses, and then her belly under his hand on a balcony with the Senate dome going gold, and then a landing platform in a red light, and then her feet leaving the ground.

Her hands at her throat. Her feet leaving the ground. Obi-Wan's voice from the ramp saying his name as if it were an order, and it had not been.

There was no way to be loved by her and keep her alive. He had tried the first and it had been the thing that killed her. So: the other. Be a thing she could not love. Be the Temple. Be the boy in the news she would never read about, polite and closed and a little cold, the one who had been told attachment was forbidden and had believed it, the one she would mention to Typho in the speeder afterwards and then, not unkindly, forget. He could do it. He had been a wall for twenty-three years; he could be a smaller one for a quarter of an hour.

It would cost her a little. He saw that and did it anyway. A small hurt now, a puzzle, against the whole of the rest of her life. He had made worse trades for less.

So when she asked whether he still flew, with that bright interest in her face, the podrace in it, the boy at the controls of a Naboo fighter with his hands on the wrong switches, he said, "When the Order requires it, Senator. Master Kenobi prefers that I not fly for its own sake."

"That must be hard."

"It's the discipline."

"You were quite good at it, as I recall." Dry. She was trying him. "You blew up a droid control ship."

"The ship did most of that. I was nine." He gave her the Temple's small polite smile, the one Padawans gave visiting dignitaries who wanted to be told that the Jedi were charming. "We're taught not to make much of it."

She looked at him. It was a short look and it did two things: it took a small hurt, somewhere behind the eyes, a quick flinch like a finger near a flame, and then it put the chin up, which was her tell, which had always been her tell, and over the hurt the Senator laid a puzzle. He could see her turning him over. She had met a boy who could not stop talking. She had expected, perhaps, a youth who could not stop talking either, red to the ears, and she had come across the room ready to be kind to that. This was something else. This was a well-mannered door. She did not know what she had done to close it and she was a person who, when she did not know a thing, went and found out.

That was the risk. He had known it was the risk. He held the door.

"The Temple's made a Jedi of you, then," she said, and there was the smallest edge on it, not for him; for the Temple.

"Not yet, Senator. It's trying."

"And is it, " she hesitated, which she did not do, ", are you happy there, Ani?"

The name. He had not expected her to use it twice.

A hangar on Naboo and a bundle of blankets and her hand on the boy's cheek, the paint still on, saying his name as if it were a thing worth keeping.

"Padawans aren't meant to be happy or unhappy, Senator," he said. "We're meant to be useful." He heard his own voice say it, light and flat and fifteen, with the smallest crack on useful where the body betrayed him, and he thought: good. Let her hear that. Let her think the Temple did that to a child.

Beside him Obi-Wan had gone very still. Not the stillness of a Jedi in a reception; a different one, deeper in, the stillness of a man who has recognised something and does not yet want to have recognised it. Anakin felt it down the bond as a kind of pressure change, the way a cabin feels before the seal fails, and he did not let himself turn his head. Obi-Wan knew what Anakin was doing. Obi-Wan had watched this boy be in love with this woman across half a war and had said nothing until it was far too late, and now he was standing here listening to the boy put the love away in front of her, deliberately, sentence by sentence, with the Temple's own manners for a tool, and whatever he thought about it he kept behind his beard and his folded hands. But the bond did not lie as well as his face. It said, in the mood it gave, something very like grief.

"Useful," Padmé said. She turned the word over as she had turned by people. "That sounds like a thing someone told you."

"Everyone tells us that, Senator. It's the first thing we're told."

"Then I'm sorry." She said it simply. "I remember a boy who wanted to see all the stars. I'd hoped the Order would let him."

"It will, Senator. In its own time." And then, because the fifteen-year-old would have, because the door needed a handle on it or she would go on working at the hinges: "I am grateful you wrote. Even if I didn't get them. I'd have liked to have had them."

A true thing. It cost more than the lies had.

Her face eased, a little, and he saw her decide to let him be what he was; to let the puzzle lie for now; to be, since it was all he was giving her room for, kind. He had counted on that. He had counted on her being kind and it worked, and it was the worst thing he had ever done to her except the thing he had actually done.


They talked for a few minutes more, the three of them, the way people do at such things. She asked Obi-Wan about the Order's view of the Outer Rim and Obi-Wan said the Order's view was that it had no view and she said that was a view, and they both enjoyed that. She spoke, briefly and with the heat kept under, about the bill: the franchise disclosure, the Rim tribunal, the committee that had been very polite and was about to take the heart out of it with a scalpel. The Chancellor supported it, she said. The Chancellor had been generous with his time. She said this looking at nothing, and Anakin, who had lived inside the man she was speaking of, heard the thing she did not yet know was there under generous, and kept his face.

"He is always generous with his time," Obi-Wan said, in the tone of a man remarking on the weather, and she glanced at him, and the glance was a Senator's glance, and she put something away behind it.

The holo. Blue and grainy, the box going through the streets of Theed with the flowers piled on it, her hands folded on her chest with the japor snippet between them, and the respirator counting while he watched it for the ninth time.

And he saw she was about to go. The room was pulling at her; Organa had appeared at the edge of the Naboo with his heavy patient face, and a tall pale woman beside him whose face Anakin still could not see without the wanted notice behind it, and Typho had shifted his weight in the way of a security man who has been given a schedule and intends to keep to it. She would go, and he had been the Temple to her for a quarter of an hour, and it had worked, and the Chancellor would have her in his office next tenday and be generous with his time, and she would find the thing under it herself, in her own time, in four years, in a pod in the Senate with her hands over her mouth, when it was too late to use.

He had meant to give her nothing. He found there was one thing he could not keep.

"Senator," he said.

She turned back. She had already half gone; she came back to him with her eyebrows up, the Senator's attention given to a boy because the boy had asked for it and she was that kind of person.

"May I say something? It'll sound odd."

"I like odd. I spend my days with people who are careful."

He let the fifteen-year-old find the words, the way a boy repeats a thing he has heard a master say and half understood and wholly believed. He made it awkward. He made it come out too fast.

"Don't trust anyone who tells you the Senate needs a stronger hand. Anyone. However reasonable it sounds. However much they seem to be on your side. If they say it, or if they get someone else to say it for them, then that's the thing they want, and nothing else they say matters." He stopped. His voice had cracked on matters and he let it. "That's all. I thought someone ought to say it to you. I don't know why me."

There was a pause, and then she laughed.

It was not the lake laugh. It was a Senator's laugh, delighted and a little startled, a woman being told the time of day by a child and finding it was the right time. Behind her Typho, who had not changed his expression in the whole of the evening, allowed the one eye to go, briefly, to the Jedi boy and then back to the room.

"You sound like Captain Typho," she said. "Word for word. He says it to me in the speeder every morning, in case I've forgotten overnight."

"Then he's right, Senator."

"He's always right. That's why I don't listen to him." She was still smiling. "Thank you, Ani. I'll remember who said it."

And she put her hand on his arm.

It was nothing. It was the touch any person gives at the end of a conversation to a youth who has said a thing out of turn and meant it: light, quick, above the elbow where the sleeve ended a finger short, so that her fingers were on the cloth and the heel of her hand, for an instant, on skin. He felt it as if a door had been opened onto a furnace. He felt it in the places where there had been metal. The body stood and let it happen and did not move, not a hair, not a breath, and she took her hand away and nodded to Obi-Wan and said something about hoping the Order would lend them out again, and turned, and went across the gallery to Organa and Mothma with Typho at her shoulder, and was a Senator, and did not look back, and he watched her not look back until the Naboo closed over her again.

"Anakin," Obi-Wan said.

"There's a terrace," he said. "Where is it."

"Through the pillars. To the west. Anakin, "

He was already walking.


The terrace was a strip of stone along the outside of the dome, two storeys below the gallery, reached by a stair that nobody at a reception used because it was cold and had no servers on it. The stone was wet; it had rained earlier and the city was still steaming. A rail at hip height, old bronze, the long curve of it following the dome. Beyond the rail the drop, and then the Senate plaza a thousand metres down pricked with lights, and then Coruscant going out and up on every side in its lanes and towers to a sky the colour of a bruise, with the Temple's five spires standing against it far off to the south like something that had been left there.

He got to the rail. He got both hands on it. Then it was as though whatever had been holding him together from the inside, the thing he had been standing inside of for the last quarter hour, simply let go of the walls.

It came out through the hands first. They would not stop. He gripped the bronze until the knuckles went white and the hands shook anyway, not the fine tremor of a tired boy but a deep one, from the forearm, the kind that in the suit would have meant a servo failing. He looked at them. They were a boy's hands, long and unfinished, no burns, no scars, the nails bitten, and they were shaking as if they were very old. The breath went next. It came short, and then it would not come, and then it came in a kind of drag that he heard himself make and could not stop, and he thought, absurdly, that he had been breathing through a machine for twenty-three years and had never once been short of it, and that this was what it was for, this, to be used up by a thing like this. He bent over the rail. He put his forehead on the back of his hands on the cold bronze and his body shook from the shoulders down in complete silence, because the gallery was two storeys up and sound went up, and because he had learned a long time ago to do this without a sound. Not a sob. Nothing so useful. Only the body going, and going, with no noise, like a building settling.

Her hand on the arm. The heel of it on the skin.

Her feet leaving the ground.

Luke's face above the mask with the light on it. "Tell your sister." The same eyes. Hers.

She had touched him and gone and she was alive, and she would go home to the Senate apartments in a speeder with Typho telling her to take the long route, and sleep, and get up, and fight the committee, and lose, and go on fighting, and never once think about the boy at the reception who had been strange and cold and had said a thing about a stronger hand that sounded like her captain; and in that she would live. That was the shape of it. That was the whole of what he had done, and it was right, and it was the only thing he had ever done for her that was wholly for her, and it felt like the lava bank. It felt exactly like the lava bank. He had not known the body remembered that.

He stayed there a long time. The rain started again, lightly, and stopped. Below, the traffic went round and round the dome in its lanes, red one way and white the other, as it had always done, as it would do when she was dead and when he was dead and when the dome was a thing the Emperor addressed the galaxy from. His hands stopped first. Then the breath came back, in pieces, and he put them together. He straightened. He wiped his face on the too-short sleeve, which was a thing a boy did, and which he was.

He did not hear Obi-Wan come. He felt the bond stir, as a mood stirs, and then there was a man at the rail beside him, not close, an arm's length off, with his hands on the bronze as Anakin's were and his face turned to the city.

Obi-Wan said nothing. He did not say it for a long time. The traffic went round. A tower off to the east went dark, floor by floor, as someone put it to bed. Anakin's hands on the rail were nearly steady now and he kept them there to be sure of it. He thought that if Obi-Wan said my friend he would go over the rail, and Obi-Wan did not say it. He thought that if Obi-Wan touched him he would break again, and Obi-Wan did not touch him. He only stood, in a Master's dark robes with the sleeves that fitted, and looked out, and was there, and let the being there do what it did.

Then he said, quietly, to the city: "I know."

"You don't."

Anakin did not look at him when he said it. It came out in a man's register, low and flat and certain, nothing of fifteen in it, and he heard that and did not care.

Obi-Wan was silent a while. The rain came and went again, a breath of it.

"No," he said at last. "I suppose I don't."

He said it slowly, and gently, in a voice Anakin could not read. It might have been a man who had stood beside a weeping boy and admitted he did not know what the boy had lost. It might have been a man who knew exactly, who had buried her, who had stood on that ramp and said that name as if it were an order and watched it fail, and who was saying I suppose I don't the way you agree to a lie for someone else's sake, because they need the lie more than you need to be known. It could be read either way. Anakin, who had spent four months reading this man, could not tell which, and the not being able to tell was so like the whole of this year that he almost laughed, and did not, because the body would not have survived it.

They stood. Below them the Senate plaza lay with its lights on, empty and clean, and far off the Temple's spires held up the dark.

It was Obi-Wan who turned his head first, not to Anakin; along the rail, to the east, where the terrace followed the dome's curve out of the light of the gallery doors and into the shadow of the next pillar.

Anakin followed the look.

Count Dooku was standing at the far end of the terrace with a glass in his hand he had not drunk from, alone, very upright in his dark Serenno coat with the silver at the collar, perhaps forty metres away. He was not looking at the city. He was looking at Anakin, and had been, with the steady unembarrassed attention of a man studying an object he has been told the provenance of and does not believe; and when Anakin's eyes found him he did not look away, as a courtier would have, but went on looking a moment longer, as a swordsman does, and then inclined his head, a fraction, and did not come nearer.

And in the gallery doorway behind Dooku, where the light fell out onto the wet stone in a long yellow shape, the Chancellor stood with Sly Moore's pale face a step behind his shoulder, and his glass, still full, in his hand. He was not looking at Anakin at all. He was looking at Dooku's back, at the set of Dooku's shoulders, at the stillness of the old man's head as he watched the boy; and on the Chancellor's kind face was an expression Anakin had seen from the inside and had never, until now, seen from without: the look of a man who has set two instruments side by side and is listening, very carefully, for which of them is out of tune.

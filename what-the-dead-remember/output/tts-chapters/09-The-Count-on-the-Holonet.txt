Chapter Nine. The Count on the Holonet.


Master Vos-Kerrick kept the politics room dark for the holonet and so the first thing Anakin saw of Count Dooku, after twenty-three years and a death, was the light of him on the faces of children.

There were fourteen Padawans in the room, on benches built for adults that most of them had grown into. Anakin had not. He sat with his knees high and his hands under his thighs, which was how he kept them still, and watched the blue wash of the projector move across a row of profiles: a Zabrak girl with her horns cropped for the dojo, two human boys who sat together in every class, Mirren Dask, a year older than he was, whom he had told the Chancellor he was in love with and who did not know it, and at the end of the bench, long legs crossed at the ankle, Orrin Bellas.

The Master stood beside the projector with her arms folded in her sleeves. Tahlin Vos-Kerrick was Chalactan, tall and spare, with the small gold marks of her people set into the skin above her eyebrows so that she seemed always about to raise them. She had a way of letting the holonet speak for a long time before she did, as if the Senate were a witness she had called and intended to let hang itself.

"This was recorded four days ago," she said, "at the Serenno Institute of Letters, at the opening of a gallery which the Count has paid for. You will notice he does not mention that. Watch him. Listen for what he does not say."

The feed resolved.

He was standing at a lectern of pale wood with a wall of books behind him, and he was wearing black, and he was seventy-six years old. Anakin had not expected the age. The Dooku he carried in him was a man of the war, straight-backed and cold and quick, a curved hilt in a gloved hand, and this one was the same man with the years not yet pressed out of him by what was coming; the hair whiter, the face a little looser at the jaw, the hands at rest on the lectern as though he had never needed them for anything but turning pages. His voice came into the dark room low and unhurried, every consonant placed, and Anakin felt the boys on the next bench stop fidgeting.

"I was asked, in preparing these remarks, to say something uplifting," the Count said. "I was told that an opening is a hopeful occasion. I agree. And I thought a long while about what a man might say, honestly, that would be both hopeful and true, and I found that the only hopeful thing I could say honestly was this: that we are still permitted to notice."

A pause, exact, like a rest in music.

"We are permitted to notice that there are worlds in this Republic where a child will be born tonight and will die before she has a name, of something a bacta tank on Coruscant would cure between breakfast and lunch, and that the vote to fund the tank for her world has been on the Senate's calendar for eleven years. We are permitted to notice that it has been deferred eleven times, and that each deferral was procedural, and that each procedure was lawful, and that the law was written by the people who benefit from the delay. We are permitted to notice that no one in that chamber is a villain. I have sat with them. They are courteous people. They are fond of their children. That is the whole horror of it. A Republic that was run by wicked men could be saved by replacing them. A Republic that is run by decent men who have learned, over a thousand years, that decency is a thing you do with your face while your hands do what the committee requires, cannot be saved by anyone. It can only be noticed. And then, perhaps, it can be left."

Somebody on the far bench breathed out through their teeth.

"I do not say this to you as a Jedi," Dooku said, and the dry smile in it made Mirren Dask laugh once, under her breath. "I have not been one for some years. I say it as a citizen of Serenno, which is far from here, and which has learned what it means to be far. Distance in this Republic is a kind of poverty. The Core does not hate the Rim; it has simply never had to think about it. And the only argument the Rim has ever been permitted to make is the argument of patience. Wait. The vote will come. The tank will come. Be patient, and be grateful, and be far away."

He looked up, and for a moment the recording gave Anakin the eyes, dark and level and, in this light, almost kind.

"I would like to say something hopeful. I will say instead something true. Patience is not a virtue when it is the only thing you are allowed. It is a sentence. And I have come to believe that the most dangerous man in the galaxy is not the one who tells you that the Senate is corrupt; you know that already; you have always known it. The most dangerous man is the one who tells you that it is not yet time to say so."

The feed held on him for a moment, standing in his black among the books, and then Vos-Kerrick killed it and brought the lights up a little, enough to see faces by, not enough to make anyone feel seen.

"Well," she said. "Now you have been seduced. Tell me how it was done."

It was Bellas who spoke first, because Bellas always did. He uncrossed his ankles and leaned forward with his elbows on his knees and the long braid swinging, and he said that it was done by being right. "He didn't say anything false, Master. Every word of it is in the record. The medical appropriations for the Rim have been deferred since before any of us were born. You've taught us that yourself."

"I have. Go on."

"So where is the seduction? He noticed something and he said it. If that's seduction, the whole of the Archives is a brothel."

Two of the boys laughed. Vos-Kerrick did not. "You have confused the content of an argument with its purpose. Padawan Dask."

Mirren Dask had her chin on her fist. "He didn't propose anything," she said slowly. "He said the Senate couldn't be fixed and then he stopped. He said it could be left. He didn't say where you would go."

"Better. Where would you go?"

"I don't know. That's what bothers me. He made me want to go somewhere and he didn't say where, and I think that's on purpose. He wants you to fill it in yourself. Then it's your idea."

"And a man of seventy-six who was trained in this building to persuade does nothing by accident." Vos-Kerrick unfolded her arms. "He is not lying to you. He is telling you the truth in such an order that you arrive, by yourself, at a conclusion he has not had to state. First the dying child, so that you are angry. Then the courteous Senators, so that your anger has nowhere lawful to go. Then patience as a sentence, so that waiting feels like cowardice. And then he stops. He is very good. The Order lost him, and you should ask yourselves, as he certainly has, why."

The Zabrak girl said that none of that made him wrong, and Bellas said that it made him right, actually, and Dask said that it made him somebody who had decided being right was enough, and then the room was arguing, voices rising the way children's do when they have been given leave, and Vos-Kerrick let them, and watched them, and once, briefly, watched Anakin.

He had said nothing. He sat with his hands under his thighs and his face arranged, and heard the Count's voice go on in the room without the Count, in Bellas's mouth and Dask's, each of them carrying a piece of it like children carrying a dead bird they had found, and he thought: yes. That is how it was done to all of us. He had heard Dooku speak many times and never once with the sound turned on. He had been in a ship's observation deck with a man on his knees before him and two blades crossed at the man's throat, blue and red, the red one his, both hands full, and the old voice from the chair behind him, patient, avuncular, a hand on the shoulder, saying, Do it, and he had done it, and the head had gone one way and the body the other, and the thing he had felt was not horror but relief, a boy's relief at being told.

That was six years from now. It was in another life. He had been twenty-two, and it had been the first thing, the first of all the things.

He realised Vos-Kerrick had said his name.

"Padawan Skywalker. You have been very quiet, which in my experience is not your custom. What did you hear?"

He looked at her and let the boy answer. "I heard a man with a lot of books, Master."

"That is an observation, not an answer."

"Sorry. I mean." He shifted on the bench and scratched the back of his neck under the braid, which was a thing a fifteen-year-old did. "He's never had to wait for anything. He's a count. He says patience is a sentence, and he's right, it is, I've done some, but he's never done any, so when he says it, it's, I don't know. It's borrowed." He shrugged. "He talks like somebody who has already decided what he's going to do and wants us to think of it first."

The room went quiet in the particular way of a room in which a child has said something adult by accident.

"Yes," said Vos-Kerrick, after a moment. "That is the seduction. Thank you." And she went on to the Amidala bill, and the disclosure vote, and the question of whether a Senator from a world that had been occupied had standing to lecture Senators from worlds that had not, and Anakin took his hands out from under his legs and found they were steady, and did not look to the end of the bench, where Bellas was looking at him.


Drallig's hall was on the fourth level, with the tall windows, and at mid-afternoon the light came down through them in bars and lay on the floor like something that had been dropped. The advanced class was ten Padawans and the Battlemaster, and he worked them without an assistant because he said that at this level he wanted to see every mistake himself.

"Pairs," Drallig said. "Bellas, Skywalker. You've unfinished business, I think. Go to the far end where you won't hit anybody."

Bellas grinned.

Anakin had lost to him eight days ago, in this hall, after a one-handed parry he had not meant to make and that Drallig had watched as a man watches a bird fly into a room. He had thrown that bout the moment he understood what he had done, badly, a boy's throw, and walked home with the welt. Since then he had been careful: in the way he walked and stood and held the hilt with two hands on it, like a boy who had been told to, and he was tired of it in his body in a way that had nothing to do with the body being fifteen. He walked to the far end of the hall with his training blade humming at low power in his fist and thought about four days. The Crucible would go in four days, with Master Yoda aboard and seven children and one Padawan to carry things, and the Padawan would be him, because his master had arranged it and had not said why, and the seventh child on the list was a Togruta girl who held her saber backwards and had looked at his hand instead of his face.

He put it away. He had got good at putting things away. He turned and saluted and Bellas saluted and Drallig said "Begin."

Bellas was seventeen and had the reach. He was nearly a head taller and his wrists had stopped growing, and he fought Form Five the way Drallig taught it, with both hands and a lot of hip, big strokes and a quick recovery, a strong boy's form. He was good. He was the best in the class and he knew it and he had been Drallig's favourite for three years, and he came in high and fast and Anakin went back before him with the blade up and gave ground the way a boy a head shorter gives ground, two-handed, elbows in, and let the second stroke beat his guard down and took the sting on the forearm and felt it, the hot thin line of it, and stepped out.

"Point," said Drallig. "Again."

They went again. He gave him the second bout at the fourth exchange, a half-turn too slow on a cross-parry that any Padawan in the hall could have made and that he made late, so that the blade came past his ear and touched his collar, and he hissed and dropped his guard and Bellas stood back, breathing, pleased.

"Point. Skywalker, you're slow today. Were you up all night again?"

"No, Master."

"Then wake up. Again."

Bellas came close, resetting, and spoke under the hum of the blades, in the voice boys used for things they did not want the Master to hear. "It's all right, slave boy. Nobody's keeping score."

Anakin saluted.

"That's the thing, though," Bellas said, pleasantly, as if they were discussing the weather, as if the words had no weight at all. "Nobody is keeping score. Not Drallig, not the Council. You know why Kenobi took you? Everybody knows. Jinn made him promise, on the floor, with a hole through him. That's all. He didn't choose you. He's been keeping a promise for six years and he's too polite to say so, and you think it's, "

The hall is on fire and Drallig is on his knees in the doorway and the blue blade is in him and the old man's face is only puzzled.

"Begin," said Drallig.

He did not decide. That was the thing he would say to himself later, on the roof, with the city burning its ordinary lights below him: that he had not decided. Bellas came in high, as he had both times, the big two-handed stroke with the hip in it, and the body that Anakin lived in now had been fifteen for fifteen years, but the body he had lived in for the twenty-three years before that had been taller and heavier and had never once in all that time needed both hands, and it was that body that moved. The left hand left the hilt. The right came up and over, straight down, with the shoulder behind it and nothing else, no flourish, no step, a stroke the way a door is a stroke, and it met Bellas's blade a third of the way down and took it out of his hands as if the boy had been holding a stick, and the training saber turned once in the air and went the length of the hall, the whole length, and skidded under the far windows and lay there humming in the bars of light.

Bellas's hands were still up and empty. His mouth was open.

The blade stopped a finger's breadth from the hollow of his throat and stayed there, one-handed, without a tremor in it, close enough that the skin under it went pink.

The hall was silent. It was the silence Anakin knew from the other hall, with the other floor; not the silence of nobody speaking but of ten people who had all at once stopped breathing. Somewhere down the room a blade switched off and nobody turned to see whose.

He looked at the skin going pink under his blade and thought, quite clearly, in a man's voice: you have done it again. And then, because the body was fifteen and quick to flush and quick to tire, his arm began to shake, and he took the blade away and thumbed it off and stood in front of a seventeen-year-old with his chest going and his face hot, a boy who had just done something and did not know what.

Drallig had crossed the hall. He had not run. He stood a little to Anakin's left, where he could see both of them, and his face was not angry; it was the same face as eight days ago, the face of a man watching a bird in a room, and he looked at Anakin's right hand, which was still closed on the hilt, and then at the empty place at his left side where the other hand had not been.

"Where did you learn that?"

The hall waited.

"I don't know, Master."

It was the truth, as far as it went. He had not learned it. He had become it, over years, with one arm that was his and one that was not, and then none, and nobody had ever called it a stroke; it had only been the way his body went when something was in front of it. He had never heard a hall go quiet for it, because the halls he had used it in had been full of blaster fire and dying. He looked at Drallig and the Battlemaster looked back and neither of them said the thing in the air, which was that no one in this building could have taught it, and that the Battlemaster would have known.

"Bellas," Drallig said, not turning. "Fetch your saber. Then you will tell me, in front of the class, what you said to him."

Bellas went red to the hair. "Master, "

"I heard the first part. I will hear the rest from you. Go."

The boy went down the length of the hall under the windows and the class watched him go, and Anakin stood where he was and became aware, slowly, as a man coming up out of water becomes aware of air, that the doorway at the near end of the hall was not empty.

Obi-Wan stood in it. He was in his brown robe with his hands in his sleeves and he was perfectly still, and there was no way to know how long he had been there, whether he had come for the end of the class and seen the whole of it, or had come down the corridor at the sound of a blade hitting the floor; and Anakin, who had kept the bond thin for eighteen days, thin as a thread, a boy's sulk and nothing else, felt along that thread what his master was feeling.

It was not surprise.

He did not know what it was. He stood in the bars of light with the hilt in his hand and reached for it, carefully, the way one feels for a stair in the dark, and it was there, steady and old and entirely contained, and it was not surprise, and that was the whole of what he could say, and it frightened him more than the silence of the hall had done.

Obi-Wan's face gave nothing. He inclined his head a fraction to Drallig, who inclined his back, two masters agreeing on something across a room full of children; and then Bellas was back with his saber and the Battlemaster turned his attention on him like a lamp.

"Say it."

Bellas said it. He said it to the floor, and left out slave boy, and Drallig made him put it back in, and then said in a voice the whole hall could hear that Padawan Bellas would spend the next tenday with the Bear Clan, holding a training saber for six-year-olds, and would address every one of them by name. The class did not laugh, because nobody wanted to be next. Then he turned to Anakin.

"And you. You lost two bouts you had no business losing and won a third you had no business winning, and I don't know which worries me more. You will come to this hall every morning before first bell until the Crucible leaves, and you will show me that stroke, slowly, as many times as it takes, until one of us understands it. That is not a punishment. Don't look at me as if it were."

"Yes, Master."

"Dismissed. All of you. Go and think about your mouths."

They went. Obi-Wan stood aside from the door to let them pass and the Padawans went by him with their eyes down, children past an adult, and when the hall was empty but for the three of them he came in a few steps, no more, and looked at the far windows where the saber had lain.

"The whole length," he said.

"Yes," said Drallig.

"Hm." He did not look at Anakin. "I came to walk him back. The Council wants me at the sixth hour and I thought we might eat first."

"Take him."

In the corridor, in the first flight of stairs down, with the tall windows gone and the walls close, Obi-Wan said, "You stopped."

"Yes."

"Good."

That was all, for three levels. Then, at the turn above the refectory, with a knot of initiates going past them with trays, small children carrying things too big for them, his master said in the same voice, a voice deciding nothing and asking nothing, "He was wrong, you know. Bellas. About the promise."

"I know."

"I don't think you do," said Obi-Wan, "but we'll leave it," and they went down into the noise.


The roof of the Temple was not a place Padawans were supposed to go, which was why there was a way up to it that Padawans had been using for a thousand years.

He went up at the second hour after midnight, when the quarters were dark and his master's side of the bond had the slow weight of sleep, and climbed the maintenance ladder in the Tranquillity Spire's shadow with his hands going cold on the rungs, and came out through the hatch onto the flat of the ziggurat's top tier, where the stone was wide and bare and the night air came across it from the city like a thing with a smell. The city burned below him in all its ordinary lights, red lanes one way and white the other, the Senate dome a dark curve against the glow. Anakin stood and breathed. It was still new. The air went in and out and nothing clicked, and after twenty-three years of hearing every breath announced he had still not got used to the silence of his own lungs.

He sat down with his back to the base of the spire and his knees up and looked at his right hand.

He had not decided. He kept turning it, trying to find a place in the afternoon where he could have put his hand and stopped it, and there was none; there was Bellas talking and the hall on fire and then the blade a finger from a boy's throat. In between there was nothing. Not anger. He had been angry as Vader and knew its shape, the hot clean narrowing of it; this had not been that. His body had done it the way the chest did the breathing. Of all the lies he had told in eighteen days, I don't know had been the truest.

And his master in the door. Thin as a thread, and what came down it was not surprise.

It was cold on the roof. He put his hands in his sleeves, like Obi-Wan, and let the shield down a fraction, not the wall, never that, but the outer part that kept the sulk in place, because there was no one up here to feel it, and because he was so tired. The body wanted sleep the way a child wants it, totally and at once, and the mind would not let it.

That was when he saw the bird.

It was on the parapet, three metres off, where the stone dropped away, and he did not know how long it had been there. It had not flown in; he would have heard it. It was simply there, as if it had been there when he came up and he had not had the eyes for it. It was the size of a small cat, and it sat very upright with its wings folded, and it was green. Not the green of anything on Coruscant. The green of leaves in a world that had weather, with a paler breast and a crest of feathers like a crown someone had been careless with, and a round flat face with a hooked beak and two round eyes, owl-like, enormous, that were looking at him.

Not at the city. Not at the lights. At him.

He did not move. His hand had gone, on its own, to the hilt at his belt, and he made it stop and come back, and the bird watched the hand go and come back as if it had understood the whole of that and had an opinion.

He had seen a bird like it. He could not have said where. Twenty-three years in the suit had taken the catalogue of small living things out of him and left only the catalogue of deaths, and he reached into the first for the bird and found only a feeling, the way one feels the shape of a dream at noon: a sky that was wrong, a light that was not the sun, a woman in green; and then, from much later and much worse, a dark wall of stone and a doorway falling and something flying out of it, up, into a black sky, while he walked away. He did not let himself go there.

"What are you?" he said. His voice was a boy's, thin in the wind.

The bird turned its head, the whole head, smoothly, through a quarter turn and back, and did not fly. He waited for it to fly. Birds flew; it was what they were for; a Padawan on a roof at night was a thing birds flew from. This one blinked, slowly, its lids coming up from below, and settled its feathers, and went on looking at him with the vast round patient attention of something that had been given a task and did not intend to leave until it was done.

He felt it then. Not from the bird; from behind it, or through it, he could not have said; the way one feels, in a dark room, that the dark has a shape. He had been looked at before. He had been looked at by Sidious every tenth day for six years, in a red room with a window, and he knew that attention better than he knew his own hands, the warmth of it and the patience and the gentleness of a man counting his property. This was not that. This had no warmth in it and no cold. It had no wanting. It was the attention of something that had been watching for a very long time and had not, in all that watching, learned to be surprised, and it was on him now, through two round eyes on a parapet, as plainly as the city light was on the stone.

Someone is watching me, he thought. Not Palpatine. Something older.

He sat with that. Below him the hall where he had nearly killed a boy was dark, and somewhere in the clan dorms seven children who would leave for Ilum in four days were asleep, or six of them were, and in two small rooms off a common room his master lay with the thread of the bond slack and whatever had come down it that afternoon put away wherever Obi-Wan put things, and in the Senate District a man who had walked in a boy's mind for six years was not asleep. All of them he had been counting since the night he woke: who knew, who did not, who had looked at him too long. He had made a list of them in a dead man's cipher and believed, because it was the only belief he could afford, that the list could be finished.

The bird had not flown.

He understood, with the cold coming up through the stone, that the list had been counting the wrong things. None of them, not the old man in the red room, not his master, not a ten-year-old girl with her saber the wrong way round, had put him here. None of them had the reach. Something had taken a dead man out of a fire on a forest moon and put him in a boy's narrow body in a bed in the Temple and had not said why, and whatever it was, it had been on this roof before him, and it had not been surprised to see him, and it was not finished.

He looked at the convor and the convor looked at him, and neither of them moved, and the city burned beneath them, and he thought, for the first time since he had opened his eyes in the dark eighteen nights ago, not who else is here, but who sent us.

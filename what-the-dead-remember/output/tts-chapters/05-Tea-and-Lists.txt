Chapter Five. Tea and Lists.


The kettle had a note it reached just before it boiled, a thin rising sound like a question, and Obi-Wan Kenobi had learned to take it off the ring on that note and not on the boil, because the boil was too late and the tea went bitter. He had learned it in this kitchen. He had learned it, he would have said if anyone had asked, over four years of mornings, and no one had asked.

He poured. The leaves turned over once in the water and settled. Outside the window the sky over the Senate District was the colour of a knife left out in the rain, and the lanes were already full, and the Temple's shadow lay across the city to the west in a long five-fingered hand that would shorten all morning and be gone by noon. He had stood at this window often enough to know where the shadow fell at each hour. He did not think this was a useful thing to know. He knew it anyway.

The boy's door was shut. It had been shut when Obi-Wan woke and it would stay shut until the second bell, because Anakin now slept until the second bell precisely and came out dressed, with his braid tidy, and said "Good morning, Master," and ate what was put in front of him. He had done this for eleven days. Before that, for four years, he had come out at any hour between the first bell and the third in whatever he had slept in, with his hair standing up and his braid chewed at the end, and had eaten standing, and had said "Morning" to the room at large, and once, memorably, to the kettle.

Obi-Wan drank his tea and kept his accounts.

Every master kept accounts of a kind. It was a discipline, like breathing in fours; a way of looking at the day before it looked at you. He began where he always began, with the small things, because the small things were the ones that could be moved.

The letters. Three years now. A courier on the Temple staff who did not ask, a flimsi packet every month or so from a moisture farm on a world he would not think about before his second cup, and a reply that the boy wrote at this table with his tongue between his teeth and did not show him. He had argued for the letters in front of the Council in the voice he used for treaties. A wound in the open heals, Masters. A wound under a bandage goes bad. Master Windu had said that the Order had managed attachment for a thousand years without his assistance. Yoda had said nothing for a long time and then said, Try it, we will, in the voice that meant he was not sure, and Obi-Wan had gone back to his quarters and sat on the edge of his bed with his hands between his knees for an hour, because he had not expected to win, and because of what he would have done if he had lost.

The classes. Two years. Drallig had wanted a senior Padawan to help with the initiates and Obi-Wan had put Anakin forward before anyone else could, and said, It is good for him, and smiled, and had not been able to explain to Drallig or to himself why he had needed it so badly. The boy had hated it for a month. Then he had stopped hating it. Obi-Wan had gone to watch once, from the gallery, and seen Anakin on one knee with his hands round a six-year-old's hands on a training hilt, saying Like this. No, like this, you're strangling it, and had left before the class ended and walked round the Room of a Thousand Fountains twice without seeing any of it.

The Chancellor. Every tenth day for six years. He had never managed to end it. He had managed, by a long accounting of small refusals, to see that it was never a whole day: a morning audience, a luncheon once that he had cut short with a mission that did not strictly exist, a tour of the Works that he had declined on the boy's behalf with such courtesy that the Chancellor's aide had thanked him for it. He had stood in Monument Plaza in rain and in the white glare of midsummer and in one memorable grit-storm that had come off the industrial districts, and he had never once gone up the steps, and he had never once let the boy come back to the Temple alone.

Yesterday he had meant to go up the steps. He had decided it in the night: he would walk the boy to the door this once, and nod to the blue-robed guards, and wait in the anteroom that had been offered him with such kindness. He had got as far as the foot of the steps. His legs had done something he had no word for. Not weakness. A refusal, from the knee down, as if the body had read the stair and declined it. He had stood there at the bottom, not going up, long enough for Anakin to reach the top, turn round and look back at him, and see whatever there was to see.

He put that in the accounts under things to do better. There were a great many things under that heading.

There were other entries, further down, that he did not go into this morning. A name he had looked for in the Archives and not found, and the look Madame Nu had given him over the top of her spectacles. A friend in CoCo Town who had been asked a strange question over a plate of eggs and had answered it. A list, in a drawer, in a hand he had trained to look unlike his own, that he added to seldom and did not reread. Those were not small things, and the morning was for small things.

He drank his tea.

The bond he put nowhere, because it did not fit.

He would have described it, to a healer, as a presence at the back of the mind. A mood more than a voice. For four years it had been a boy's weather: sulks and bright patches and the occasional thunderstorm, and under them, always, the thing he had no better word for than warmth, a lamp left on in another room. Ten nights ago, at some hour before dawn, it had closed. Not faded. Not gone quiet, as it did when the boy slept. Closed, like a door, with a sound he had felt in his teeth, and on the other side of the door: nothing. Not a bad feeling. Nothing. A wall where a window had been.

He had lain still and listened and told himself every reasonable thing. By morning the door had opened a crack and let out exactly enough of a sulk to be convincing, and it had stayed at that crack for eleven days, and he knew the width of it to the hair, because he had never in his life stopped measuring anything.

He had told no one. He had, in four years of mornings at this window, acquired a certain fluency in telling no one.

The second bell went. The boy's door opened.

"Good morning, Master."

"Good morning. There's tea."

"Thank you." Anakin sat. He was in his tunic, with his braid lying flat behind his right ear and his hair wet from the refresher. He was fifteen and had grown five centimetres since the turn of the year and none of his clothes fitted at the wrists. He took the cup in both hands, the way he had never taken anything, and looked at the steam. He had the face of a boy who had slept, which was not the same as a boy who had.

Obi-Wan looked at his hands. He did it before he could stop himself doing it. Two hands, brown from last month's sun, the right one with a healing scrape across the knuckles from the speeder he was not supposed to be rebuilding. Two hands on a cup. He looked away.

"You have Drallig at the fourth hour," he said.

"I know."

"Bellas is back from Corellia."

"I know that too." A boy's flat tone, well done. "Are you coming?"

"I may. Eat something, my friend. Drallig doesn't feed you."

The boy ate. Obi-Wan watched him do it, and the shadow of the Temple went on shortening across the city, and neither of them said anything about steps.


The summons came by a Temple Guard, which was Yoda's way of being discreet, and it said only that Master Yoda would be glad of a word before the third hour, in the small room below the Council chamber, if Master Kenobi could spare it.

He could. He went up the long stair of the eastern spire with his hands in his sleeves, past the windows where the city turned slowly as the stair turned, and found the old Master alone with his stick across his knees and the room's one window open to the wind.

"Obi-Wan."

"Master."

"Sit, sit." The chair was too low, as it always was, and he sat in it, as he always did. Yoda looked at him for a while with the look that had been assessing Padawans since before the Republic had its present name. "Tired, you look."

"I'm told it's the beard."

"Hm." The ears moved. "Ten nights ago. Where were you?"

He had known, of course, that it would be this. He had known it when the Guard spoke. He said, in the voice he used for everything, "In my bed, I think. It seems the sort of place I would have been."

"Woke, did you?"

"I'm a light sleeper."

"Slept, the younglings did. Every clan. Woke, none of them." Yoda's claws tapped the stick. "Felt it, I did, all the same. A shadow, entering the Temple. A bell, struck, where no bell was. And here the shadow is still. New, it is. Close. Not an old darkness. Not a thing from outside." The green eyes did not move from him. "A stain, Obi-Wan. Of the dark side. Here, in the house."

The wind came in and moved the hem of Obi-Wan's robe. He let it.

He thought: I could say it. There was a door, Master, and it closed, and I have been standing outside it for eleven days knocking very quietly. He could see the sentence whole. He could see Yoda hearing it. He could see, with great clarity, as if it were a holo thrown on the wall opposite, the thing that would happen next: the Council in its circle; the boy in the middle of it; twelve faces turned on a child the way a city turns on a fire. He had watched that circle look at that boy before. Not for this. For less. He had watched what being looked at did to him.

He thought: I do not know that it is him. That was true. He held on to it the way a man holds a rail.

"A shadow," he said. "In the Temple."

"Say it twice, you need not."

"I only wondered whether you meant a person or a place. Shadows have both."

"A person, I think." The old eyes narrowed. "Your Padawan. Slept badly, that night. Said so, you did, to the healers' roster. Written down, it is."

"Anakin has slept badly since he was nine, Master. If the Council means to investigate every night he's had a dream, I'll need a larger datapad." He heard himself and did not like it; it was too easy, and Yoda was not easy. He made his voice slower. "He's fifteen. He has nightmares. He has them more often around the Chancellor's audiences, which I have said before, in this building, to no particular effect."

"Hm."

"If you are asking whether I have felt the dark side in my own Padawan, " He stopped, because he had set himself a rule, years ago, at this window or one like it, that he would not lie to this particular person outright if there were any other road. He looked for the road. "I would not call what I have felt in him that."

Which was true. He would not call it that. He would not call it anything. He had been very careful not to.

Yoda was quiet. Then: "Look, the Council will. All of it. Every corner of this house. The healers, I will ask. Master Windu already is asking." A small sound, not a laugh. "Hide, a thing cannot, that is close and new. Known, it will be. Only a question of when." He tilted his head. "Near, stay. Again I will want you. When I send, come."

"Then I hope the Council finds what it's looking for," Obi-Wan said, and stood, because his legs wanted to, "and I hope it is kind to whatever that turns out to be."

He was at the door when Yoda said, "Obi-Wan."

He turned.

"Your door, also, I felt close. That night."

The wind. The city turning. He put a hand to his face and found the beard short, and took the hand away.

"I'm a light sleeper," he said again, and went down the stair.


Drallig's hall at the fourth hour smelled of sweat and ozone and the particular dust of a floor that had been fallen on for a thousand years. Obi-Wan took the gallery, which ran round three sides above the sparring floor and was built, he had always thought, for exactly this: for masters to watch their Padawans and pretend they were not. There were two other masters up there, and a knot of initiates in sand-coloured tunics who had slipped in and would be found and sent out, and he nodded and said nothing and leaned on the rail.

Bellas was on the floor already. Seventeen, tall, with the kind of shoulders that arrive early and the kind of confidence that never leaves; Drallig's favourite, which Drallig denied and everyone knew. He had come back from Corellia with a new way of standing and was showing it to the room. Anakin stood opposite him with his training saber held the way a boy holds a thing he is good at and wants to be seen being good at: a little high, a little tight. He was a head shorter than Bellas and his tunic hem was uneven where he had caught it on something, and when he settled his feet on the mark he did it twice, because a boy who is growing does not trust his feet.

"Begin," said Drallig.

Obi-Wan had watched Anakin fight for four years. He knew what he was watching the way he knew the kettle's note. The boy fought forward. He fought with his whole face. He took a hit the way he took everything, as a personal remark, and came back harder, and lost his temper around the fourth exchange and won or lost on the strength of it. It was ugly and it was gifted and Drallig had said, in Obi-Wan's hearing, that in ten years it would be something no one in the Temple could stand in front of.

This was not that.

This was a boy fighting well. Correctly. A beat behind Bellas and never more than a beat, so that it looked like a good Padawan trying hard against a better one. The blade work was clean. The feet were clean. The face, the face was a boy's face, mouth open, breathing hard, flushed to the ears; it was so exactly a boy's face that Obi-Wan found himself watching only it, and so he saw the moment, in the third exchange, when Bellas overreached on a cut and left his whole right side open from hip to shoulder, and the boy's blade was already moving, and stopped, and went somewhere else.

Nobody else saw it. Drallig might have. Obi-Wan looked along the rail and Drallig was watching the floor with his arms folded and his face shut, and that could have meant anything.

Bellas took the point. Anakin went down on one knee with the sting of it, as a boy does, and got up, and said "Again?" with the right amount of breath in it, and they went again, and Obi-Wan stood with his hands on the rail and thought: he is losing. He is choosing to. He is choosing how.

He did not know why that frightened him more than if the boy had taken Bellas's head off.

He had seen Anakin lose. Badly, with his whole face, with a fist against the wall afterwards. He had never seen him lose quietly. He had never seen him give a point, and he thought, with the part of him that kept accounts, that if he had a list of everything in this boy he had never seen before, it was eleven days old and already longer than his arm.

The fourth exchange. Bellas was enjoying himself now, and the enjoyment made him generous, and generosity made him big: he came in with a high overhand stroke, the Corellian thing, all shoulder, the kind of blow that is meant to be seen coming and to arrive anyway.

Anakin took it one-handed.

He did not step. His left foot moved perhaps the width of a finger. The blade came up turned outward, the elbow tucked, the whole weight of Bellas's shoulder caught and sent down and sideways with no more effort than a man closing a door on a draught, and for half a second the boy stood inside the other's reach with his saber low and his free hand open at his side and his face entirely still, and the stillness was not a boy's. It was not anyone's. It was the stillness of something that had already decided and was waiting, out of some private courtesy, for the other party to realise it.

Then he stepped back, and stumbled on the step, and Bellas's blade caught him on the shoulder and he yelped like a fifteen-year-old and sat down.

Obi-Wan's breath had stopped.

He noticed that it had stopped as a fact about the room, as if someone else's breath had stopped nearby and he was being polite about it. His right hand had come off the rail and gone to his face, to his beard, and it had reached for something longer than what it found, and it stayed there against his jaw, cupped round a shape that was not there. His eyes had gone to the boy's right hand. The boy's right hand was on the floor, flat, taking his weight, with the healing scrape across the knuckles. Two hands. He looked at them both. He made himself.

He had seen that parry before.

He did not think where. He noticed himself not thinking it, the way he had noticed his breath. There was a place in him that he kept, as a man keeps a locked room in a house he otherwise lives in, and it was from that room that the recognition had come, clean and immediate and total, and he did not open the door to see what else was in there. He stood on the gallery with his hand on his face and the door shut, and below him Drallig said, "Enough. Bellas, your footwork is a disgrace, Corellia has made you vain. Skywalker, "

A pause.

"Skywalker. That block. Where did you learn it?"

The boy, sitting on the floor, looked up. His face was red and wet and open, and he said, with his breath still short, "Which one, Master?"

Drallig looked at him for a moment. Then he looked up at the gallery, straight at Obi-Wan, and raised his eyebrows half a centimetre, which in Cin Drallig was a speech.

"I didn't teach him that," Drallig said.

"No," Obi-Wan said. His voice came out perfectly level. "Nor did I."

Which was true, and was the kind of truth he had got very good at.

Anakin got to his feet, and bowed to Bellas, and bowed to Drallig, and did not look at the gallery at all, and Obi-Wan thought that this was the most frightening thing the boy had done all morning, because for four years he had looked at the gallery first.


He went back up the eastern spire because Yoda had said when I send and had not yet sent, and because the stair was long and he wanted it to be long, and because he did not want to go home and find the boy at the table with his tongue between his teeth, writing a letter he would not show him.

The small room below the Council chamber was empty. The window stood open as it had that morning. He stood at it with his hands in his sleeves and let the wind have his face.

He thought about the accounts. He thought that it was possible to keep very careful accounts for a very long time and have them come out, in the end, to a number you had not expected. He thought that there was a flaw in the whole enterprise that he had never properly faced, which was that you could only change the things you could see, and a man standing at a window, whatever he thought he knew, could not see what was coming up behind him on the stair.

Something moved on the ledge outside.

He looked. He had expected a pigeon, or one of the Senate District hawks that nested in the spires and brought the younglings feathers. It was neither. It was a bird he did not know: owl-shaped, broad-faced, green, a deep soft green from crown to tail like moss on the north side of a stone, with two long feathers lying back from its head and eyes that were, he saw, as it turned them on him, not an owl's eyes at all. They were yellow and whole and very old. It sat on the stone ledge a hand's breadth outside the open window and looked at him, and he looked at it, and neither of them moved.

He did not know the bird. He was certain of that; he knew what he knew, he kept lists. He had never seen a creature like it in the Temple or on this world or anywhere he could name.

And the feeling came anyway.

It came up through him the way the refusal had come up through his legs at the foot of the Senate steps, from below, without permission, and it was not dread. That was the extraordinary thing. He had been braced for dread all day; he had a long familiarity with dread and would have known how to hold it. This was the other thing. This was old and bright and enormous, like light off water a long way away, like the first warm day after a winter, like something he had once been given and had been sure he would not be given again. It had a shape and the shape was a person's and he could not see the face.

His eyes stung. He had not told them to.

He stood there, a Jedi Master of thirty-one with his hands in his sleeves and his eyes wet, looking at a green bird, and thought of nothing. He could not have said what he thought of. There was no name on it. There was only the brightness, and the sting, and under them both a feeling he recognised from somewhere with more certainty than he had recognised the parry, and liked less: the feeling of a door he had closed and locked and leaned against, moving, very slightly, in its frame.

The bird blinked, once, slowly, the way a thing blinks that has all the time in the world.

Then it opened its wings, which were wider than seemed possible, and stepped off the ledge into the wind, and was a green mark against the city, and then was nothing.

He stayed at the window for a while. The wind dried his face. The shadow of the Temple had gone to nothing under the noon and the city lay flat and bright and unshadowed to every horizon, and somewhere under it a boy was walking home from Drallig's hall with a stung shoulder and two hands and a face he had been using for eleven days, and somewhere a Council was beginning, in its slow, thorough, well-meaning way, to look.

He found that he had taken his hand out of his sleeve and put it on the stone of the sill, and that it was not quite steady, and he looked at it with interest, as at someone else's.

"Not again," he said, to the empty room.

It was not loud. The wind took most of it.

"Whatever this is," said Obi-Wan Kenobi. "Not again."

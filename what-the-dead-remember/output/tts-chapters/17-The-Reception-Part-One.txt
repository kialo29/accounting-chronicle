Chapter Seventeen. The Reception, Part One.


The invitation came through the Council, which was how Anakin knew it could not be refused.

It arrived nine days before the reception, in the form Council business always took: a summons to the antechamber below the High Council Chamber, where Master Windu kept them standing. The Chancellor's office had written to the Order. The sixth anniversary of the Battle of Naboo would be marked by a reception in the Senate, and the Chancellor, mindful of the debt the Republic owed, had asked particularly that the heroes of that day be present: Master Kenobi, who had killed the Sith; his Padawan, who had been nine and had flown; and the Senator for Naboo, who had been Queen. Windu read it out in his clipped way without looking at either of them, and when he had finished he said, "Master Kenobi has asked the Council to decline on the Order's behalf. The Council has considered it. The Order accepts."

Anakin had not looked at Obi-Wan. He had looked at the floor between his boots, which were a size too small again, and had felt down the thin line of the bond something held very flat and very still, like a hand pressed on a tablecloth.

He knew why Obi-Wan had tried. There was the plain reason, the one anyone in the Temple could have given: Kenobi did not let the Chancellor have his Padawan for a whole day, had never let him, had sat in Monument Plaza through six years of tenth-day audiences like a man waiting outside a surgery. An evening in the Senate with the Chancellor as host was a whole day by any reckoning. And there was the other reason, the one only Anakin could have given, which was that Count Dooku of Serenno was to be the Chancellor's guest, and Obi-Wan, this Obi-Wan, the one who had searched the Archives for a planet that was not there, might know exactly what Count Dooku was.

Or might not. That was the trouble with the second reason. It explained everything and could be proven by nothing, and Anakin had lived with it long enough now to distrust how well it fitted.

"You'll wear the formal robes," Windu said. "Both of you. The Count will be there." He said the name the way the Council said it, with the faint ceremonial distaste reserved for the Lost Twenty. "He has written to say he attends to honour Qui-Gon Jinn's memory. You will be civil."

"We are always civil," Obi-Wan said.

"You are always something," said Windu, and dismissed them.


The night before the reception he went to the Room of a Thousand Fountains after the ninth bell, when the lamps dimmed to the blue the gardeners used for the night-flowering beds and the paths belonged to whoever was willing to get wet.

Third fall from the east door. The ledge ran behind the water where the rock had been cut to carry a pipe, and the moss on it was thick enough to hide a hand. He crouched with the spray on the back of his neck. He still noticed water, every time, the cold of it on skin, as if the body were reporting the fact to someone who had been away a long while and ought to be told.

Her note was there, folded to the size of a thumbnail. He read it standing in the dark behind the fall, tilted to the blue light, the shifted Aurebesh unlocking in his head letter by letter without his needing to count any more. Seven along. She had small, hard handwriting, the letters pressed deep, as if the stylus were a chisel and the flimsi had offended her.

Reception tomorrow. I know. The whole clan knows. Kessa thinks you'll meet the Queen and wants to know if she's pretty. I said yes.

Three things.

One. The Count will touch you. Not with his hand. He can't help it, it's what he does; he did it to me once on a landing platform with four hundred people watching and nobody but me felt it. Let him find a boy. You know how.

Two. The Chancellor will be watching the Count watch you. Don't give him a show. Nothing he can use twice.

Three. Don't look at her like she's already dead.

She'll see it. She noticed me the first day we met and I was trying. Look at her like a Padawan who met a queen once and has been thinking about speeders ever since. You can do that. You did it for years.

If the Count offers you a drink, it's poisoned. (Joke.) (Mostly.)

S.

He stood with it for a while, the water loud at his back. The thread between them lay where it always lay, east and down, faint as a hair across the eye: she was awake, she was in the dorm, she was waiting to feel him find it. He let her feel it. Then he took the stub of flimsi from his sleeve and wrote against the rock, seven along, in a hand that was still, after everything, a boy's.

Three for three. If he offers me a drink I'll hand it to the Chancellor. Sleep. S.

He folded it to a thumbnail and set it under the moss and pressed the moss down, and the thread east and down gave a small tug, like a line with something on it, and went still.


The formal robes came up from the quartermaster in the morning in a sealed bag with his name on it and smelt of the cedar they kept the stores in. Cream under-tunic. Tabards in a brown so dark it was nearly black. The outer robe heavier than the one he wore every day, with a weight in the hem to make it hang. He had been fitted for them at the start of the year and in the months since he had grown out of the sleeves, so that when he put his arms down the cuffs stopped a finger's width short of his wrists and showed the bone there. He stood in the common room in his socks and looked at his wrists and did not know what to do with them.

He moved the two lists from the belt he wore every day to the lining of the formal one. Hers in Togruti, his in the cipher. Then he put the belt on and the lightsaber on the belt, and the weight of it on his left hip was the only thing about the whole outfit that felt as if it belonged to him.

Obi-Wan came out of his room in his own formals already dressed, beard trimmed, and stood and looked at him.

"Stand still," he said.

"I am still."

"You are vibrating." He came over. He took the Padawan braid between finger and thumb and ran it to the end and found the end-tie had slipped, and re-tied it, which took him longer than it should have because his hands were not quick at it. Anakin stood with his chin up and looked at the wall over Obi-Wan's shoulder. Then Obi-Wan straightened the collar of the outer robe, one side and the other, the way he had done a thousand times for a nine-year-old and a twelve-year-old and now did for a fifteen-year-old who was already nearly his height. The hand stayed a moment at the collar. It was not holding anything. It was simply there, flat against the cloth over Anakin's collarbone, as if it had been set down and forgotten.

"You will stay where I can see you," Obi-Wan said.

"Yes, Master."

"I mean it, Anakin."

"I know you do."

The hand came away. Obi-Wan stepped back and looked at the sleeves and his mouth did the thing at the corner it did when he had decided not to say something.

"The Count," he said, "was a great Jedi once. Qui-Gon loved him. That is all you need to know about him tonight." He said it to the sleeves, lightly, and then looked up, and his eyes were perfectly mild. "If he offers you his hand, take it. If he offers you his opinion, nod. If he offers you anything else, I shall be standing next to you."

"And if he offers me a drink?"

"Then you have had a long evening," Obi-Wan said, "and we are going home."


The Senate Guards at the upper doors wore the blue, the full ceremonial blue with the high helms, and stood like fence-posts. Anakin walked between them behind Obi-Wan with his hands in his too-short sleeves and let the boy's register come up in his face: a Padawan at his first real reception, a little dazzled, a little bored, looking at everything. It was the easiest thing in the world. He had been that boy. He had stood in rooms like this at fifteen and seventeen and nineteen and thought about speeders.

The reception was in the Dome Gallery, which ran round the upper rim of the Rotunda like the inside of a crown. On the outer side the windows gave on to the city at dusk, traffic in its lanes, the towers of five hundred Republica, and far off across the haze the five spires of the Temple with the last light on them. On the inner side the wall was transparisteel from floor to ceiling, and through it one looked down into the Senate chamber itself, empty, immense, the thousand pods docked in their tiers and dark, like boats at a quay at night. Between the two walls the gallery curved away out of sight in both directions, and it was full.

Naboo's colours hung from the ceiling in long banners, the pale greens and golds of the lake country. There was a Gungan delegation near the windows, a dozen of them in their stiff ceremonial collars, towering over the Senate staff who were trying to bring them drinks; the Boss himself was there, enormous, his jowls shaking as he laughed at something. There were Naboo in the dress of the Royal House, there to represent Queen Réillata, who had sent her regrets and a long message. There were senators. He recognised them the way one recognises faces from a bulletin: the dark-haired man from Alderaan with the listening face, Organa, younger than Anakin had ever seen him; a young woman in white from Chandrila, Mothma, who was twenty and looked it, and whom he last remembered older by thirty years on a wanted notice with a price beneath her name. He looked away from her. He looked at the pods below, dark, docked, waiting.

Mas Amedda stood at the centre of it all with his horns and his staff and his look of a man who had been Vice Chair for six years and had not yet forgiven anyone for it. A step behind him, where there was always a step behind him, the Umbaran woman stood with her hands folded and her white face turned a little to one side, as if listening to something in the next room. Sly Moore.

"Stay close," Obi-Wan said, under the noise.

"I'm close."

They were given drinks they did not drink. They were introduced to the Naboo party, who were gracious and wanted to talk about the Queen, by which they meant the former Queen, by which they meant the Senator, who had not yet arrived; she had been detained in committee, they said, with the air of people who had been told this would be the case and did not approve. A heavyset Twi'lek senator with a jewelled headpiece, Taa of Ryloth, drifted past with two aides and said to no one in particular, as if continuing an argument begun elsewhere, "Franchise disclosure. A tribunal for the Rim. She has been in this building three years and she thinks a ledger is a weapon. I have seen weapons." The aides laughed. Obi-Wan said, "Senator," with a courtesy so exact it could have been measured with instruments, and Taa went on round the curve of the gallery trailing his laughter behind him.

Anakin found that he had been holding his glass hard enough to whiten his knuckles and made his hand let go.

"Yes," Obi-Wan said quietly, not looking at him. "Quite."


The Chancellor came to them. That was how it was done: the Chancellor came to the heroes of Naboo, and the room turned to watch him do it.

He came with Amedda at his left and the Umbaran at his right, in the plain dark robes of office that he had worn for six years and would wear for thirteen more, and his face was doing the thing it did, the kind face, the pleased grandfather. He took Obi-Wan's hand in both of his and said something about the Order's generosity in sparing them, and Obi-Wan said something about the Order's honour, and the two of them held the shape of it for the length of time the room required. Then Palpatine turned.

"And here he is," he said. "My boy."

The hand came down on Anakin's shoulder. He had known it would and had set himself for it, and it was still a thing to be stood through: the weight of it, the warmth through the robe, the thumb finding the collarbone where Obi-Wan's hand had been that morning. And under it, as there always was, the other touch. Not a hand. A pressure in the mind like the pressure of a man leaning on a railing, testing it, the way one tests a railing one has leant on before. It found the surface Anakin kept for it. A boy's surface: the too-short sleeves, which were an embarrassment; the Gungans, which were funny; a Padawan called Mirren Dask, who had not looked at him in politics class for a tenday and whose not-looking took up a great deal of room. Under that, the wall. The pressure touched the wall as it touched it every tenth day now, without surprise, almost with a kind of proprietary satisfaction, the way a man touches a fence he has decided is his.

He had stopped pressing it weeks ago. Sidious had found a locked door in his garden and tried it once, and then simply left it, and planted round it, and came back every ten days to admire it. A man who does that is not waiting for the door to open. He is waiting to be told who has the key.

"You've grown again," Palpatine said, with the kind face. "They'll have to let out those sleeves."

"Yes, sir."

"Six years. I remember a boy who had to be lifted into the cockpit." A squeeze of the shoulder, and the hand came away, and the touch under it came away with it, unhurried. "I shall embarrass you in a moment in front of everyone, I'm afraid. It's a Chancellor's privilege. Bear it as you bear everything, my boy."

He went to the centre of the gallery and Amedda struck the floor with his staff and the room went quiet.

The speech was short, which was the mark of the man. He spoke of Naboo. He spoke of a small world's courage and a great Republic's debt, of a people who had not waited for help and of the help that had come anyway, late and insufficient and welcome, and the Naboo in the room stood a little straighter and the Gungans rumbled. He spoke of the Jedi, and this was the part Anakin had been waiting for. He said that the Republic had been served on that day, as it was served on every day, by men and women who asked nothing and were given, too often, exactly that. He said that one of them stood in this room tonight, who had faced a thing in the Theed generator complex that the Republic had believed a thousand years dead, and had killed it, and had never once in six years asked to be thanked. The room looked at Obi-Wan. Obi-Wan inclined his head a quarter of an inch, which was what he had. And beside him, Palpatine said, a boy of nine, from a world the Republic did not reach, had got into a starfighter he had never flown, and flown it, and ended a battle, and was now fifteen and could not keep his sleeves long enough. The room laughed, warmly. The room looked at Anakin. He flushed on cue, and it was not even a performance; the body did it for him, the blood came up his neck into his face the way it had at nine and at fifteen, and he looked at his boots.

"To Naboo," the Chancellor said, and raised his glass, and the gallery said it after him, two hundred voices, a sound like surf.

Anakin raised his glass with the rest. Over the rim of it, across the width of the gallery and the length of the toast, he saw the man in black.


He had seen him already, the moment they came through the doors, and had put him where he put Mothma, in the part of the mind where things are seen and not looked at. Now the toast was done and the room was moving again, and the man in black was moving with it, towards them, without hurry.

Count Dooku of Serenno was seventy-six years old. He was tall, taller than Obi-Wan, and he carried the height the way old soldiers do, from the spine, as if it were something he had been issued and was responsible for. Silver hair swept back from the forehead. A silver beard cut close and precise. The black was not mourning black and not Senate black; it was a Count's black, a long high-collared coat of a cloth that drank the light, and a cloak clasped at the throat with a plain silver chain, and nothing else on him anywhere that shone. He had a face that had been handsome and had decided, at some point, to be formidable instead. The hands were long. He was, Anakin thought, holding his glass again too hard, exactly as he remembered him, which was the thing he had not been ready for: not that Dooku was here, but that he was the same, that the man who had taken his arm on Geonosis and the man who walked towards him now across a gallery full of senators were one man, wearing one face, and this one did not know him at all.

The blue blade coming across and his own arm leaving him at the elbow, the white flash of it before the pain, the smell.

He breathed. The thread east and down stirred, as if someone far off had turned over in bed.

Dooku reached them and stopped at the distance etiquette required, and bowed, from the neck, to Obi-Wan.

"Master Kenobi," he said. His voice was deep and unhurried and had the kind of accent that had been expensive for a thousand years. "My student's student." And his eyes moved, without the head moving, to Anakin. "And his."

"Count Dooku." Obi-Wan's bow was a fraction deeper, as was proper from the younger to the elder, from a Knight to a man who had sat on the Council before the Knight was born. "Qui-Gon spoke of you often, and always with respect."

"He spoke of me often and always in disagreement," Dooku said, "which from Qui-Gon was the same thing. You were there at the end."

"I was."

"And it was quick."

"It was quick."

"He would have preferred it otherwise," Dooku said. "He never did anything quickly in his life. I am glad to have found you, Master Kenobi. I had wondered what sort of a man he had made, and I see he made one."

Anakin listened to it the way one listens to a lock being turned. Obi-Wan's courtesy was flawless. Every line of it was the right length. The bow was right and the deference was right and the small dry warmth was right, exactly the warmth of a man who had loved Qui-Gon speaking to a man who had taught him, and under it, down the thin line of the bond, there was nothing. Flat. Still. A hand on a tablecloth. Anakin, who knew what this man had done to Obi-Wan in another life, who had seen Dooku stand over him on Geonosis and put the red blade through his shoulder and his thigh and leave him on the floor, could not tell whether the courtesy was flawless because Obi-Wan was courteous, or because it had to be. He could not tell. Here was the test he and Ahsoka had agreed on and never designed, handed to him in a crowded room, and he could not read it.

"And this is the boy," Dooku said.

He had turned. He stood now a pace from Anakin, looking down, and his hand came up from his side and moved towards Anakin's shoulder, and did not arrive. It stopped a finger's width from the cloth of the formal robe, open, as if to rest there, and hung. A courtesy, perhaps: the old-world gesture of a man who did not touch a Jedi without leave. Perhaps. The hand did not touch him.

What was under the hand touched him.

It was nothing like the Chancellor's. The Chancellor's touch was warm and heavy and everywhere, a man leaning on a railing. This was cold and narrow and exact. It came in under the sleeve of the mind like the point of a jeweller's glass, looking for the stone, and it had a flavour to it that Anakin knew in his wrists before he knew it in his head: the flavour of Makashi, of a blade held low and a wrist turning, of economy and contempt. A Sith's assessment. It found the surface, the boy's surface, the sleeves and the Gungans and Mirren Dask, and it did not stop at the surface the way Palpatine's did. It went through it with a kind of weary precision, as if the surface were a lie it had been told to expect, and came down on the wall.

And Anakin, who had let the Chancellor lean on that wall forty times, who had stood through it in the red office every tenth day with a boy's face on, felt the wrong thing come up.

The red blade crossing his own, two blades, scissored at the throat of a man on his knees on a steel deck. The silver head bowed. The old eyes looking up, not at Anakin, at the chair behind him, with something in them that Anakin had not understood for twenty years and had understood, in the end, perfectly: the look of a man who has just learned what he was for. And from the chair, mild, patient, a hand on the shoulder: Do it.

The hatred came up through him like water through a floor. Not Vader's, not the long cold thing he had carried in the suit; this was older and hotter and had a boy in it, a nineteen-year-old on his back in a hangar on Geonosis with the stump of his arm smoking and this man walking away. The arm. The kneeling. Do it. It went up the wall and over the top of it before he could put a hand to it, one clear flare of it, a lamp uncovered, aimed at the man in front of him and at nothing and no one else in the galaxy.

He clamped it. He clamped it so hard his jaw ached with it an hour later. He brought the wall up over it the way one brings a hand down over a lit match, and the surface back over the wall, the boy's surface, the sleeves and the speeders and the girl who did not look at him, all of it, in less time than it took to breathe in.

It was a second too late.

He saw it land. He saw it arrive in Dooku's face, and he had never seen this in Dooku's face in two lives, not on Geonosis, not on the deck of the Invisible Hand: something behind the eyes went very still, the way a man goes still who has put his foot on a stair that is not there. It was not fear. Dooku did not have fear in him the way other men did. It was a kind of recalculation. The eyes, which had been assessing, were now, for the length of a held breath, simply looking, at a fifteen-year-old in too-short sleeves, as though the boy had spoken to him in a language he had not heard since childhood and could not place.

The hand that had not touched him came away. The touch beneath it withdrew, not quickly, with care, as a man draws his hand back from something he has found warmer than it should be.

"Qui-Gon's last message to me, before Naboo, concerned a boy from Tatooine," Dooku said, in the same deep unhurried voice, "and I have wondered since what it was that he saw."

Anakin bowed. It was a Padawan's bow, slightly too deep, slightly too fast, a boy who has been spoken to by a great man and does not know where to put his hands. "Count," he said.

That was all. Dooku looked at him a moment longer. Then he turned the look on Obi-Wan, and it was a different look, and it said nothing at all, and he inclined his head and went on along the gallery with the crowd opening before him, and did not look back.

Anakin found that Obi-Wan's hand was on his elbow. He did not know when it had got there. It was holding him, not steadying him, the way one holds a thing that might go.

"Breathe," Obi-Wan said, barely moving his mouth.

He breathed.

And he looked, because he could not help looking, because Ahsoka's second rule was in his head in her chisel letters, across the width of the Dome Gallery to where the Chancellor stood with Amedda on his left and the Umbaran on his right and a glass of something gold in his hand that he had not drunk. Palpatine was not looking at the Gungans. He was not looking at the Naboo. He was looking at Dooku's back as it moved away through the crowd, and then, with no haste at all, at Anakin, and then at Dooku again, the way a man looks from one hand to the other when he has just felt something pass between them. The kind face was on. But under it there was a thing Anakin knew better than he knew his own name, because he had lived inside the mind that produced it for twenty-three years and had learned to read it the way a dog reads weather. Pleasure. Not the pleasure of a man who has had a question answered. The pleasure of a man who has heard his two instruments strike the same note, and did not know until this moment that they were tuned to each other.

Don't give him a show. Nothing he can use twice.

He had given him something. He did not yet know what.


Then the doors.

They were the great doors at the north end of the gallery, where the Naboo party had been standing all evening pretending not to watch them, and they opened inward on their silent hinges, and the chamberlain at the top of the steps struck his staff once, as Amedda had, and said in a voice trained to carry over surf, "The Senator for Naboo."

The room turned. Anakin turned with it.

She came through the doors with a man a pace behind her, a broad young Naboo in the dark uniform of the Royal Security Forces with a leather patch over one eye and a hand that did not leave the vicinity of his sidearm: Typho. She wore blue. Senatorial blue, the deep clear blue of the Naboo delegation, a long gown with a high collar and the sleeves cut close, and over it a short cloak of a darker blue clasped at one shoulder. Her hair was dressed the Naboo way, up and back, held with something that caught the light, and her face was bare of the Queen's paint and was the face he had known under it. She was twenty years old. She was twenty years old and it was six years since Theed and she stood at the top of the steps for a moment while the room looked at her, with her chin up and her hands folded at her waist, and she was not dead. She was not grey. She was not lying on a table on a dark rock at the edge of nothing with two children crying beside her and a word on her mouth that he had not been there to hear. She was twenty and she was alive and she was looking for someone in the room, and he knew who, because the Naboo were already moving to her, and because the Chancellor had turned his kind face towards the doors, and because he had been fifteen once and had stood exactly here and had known, with his whole body, that she was looking for him.

Don't look at her like she's already dead.

He looked at her. He could not have said, afterwards, how.

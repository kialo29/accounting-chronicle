# Chapter 3: The Healer

Every morning Barriss Offee lit a candle for the man who had carried the bomb into the Temple, and every morning she was the only one who did.

She came before dawn, when the memorial alcove above the rebuilt hangar was empty and the great ventilation fans in the walls turned slowly enough that the flames stood straight. The shelf ran the length of the eastern wall, seven tiers of pale stone. On the top tier burned six flames for the six Jedi, each set before a small bronze plate, Tutso Mara's first because he had been a Knight. Below them, row on row, burned the flames for the clone troopers and the hangar crew, their plates stamped with numbers or with names depending on what they had been when they died.

There was no plate for Jackar Bowmani. He had walked into the hangar that morning with the nano-droids already in his blood, put there by a wife who had smiled at him over breakfast, and he had not known what he was until he stopped being anything at all. The Temple had not lit a candle for him. The Temple did not know how.

So Barriss lit one, at the far end of the lowest tier, with nothing in front of it. She held the taper to the wick and watched the flame catch, and the flame shook, because her hand shook.

She set the taper down and pressed both palms flat against her thighs and waited. The tremor did not pass. It had not passed since the night in the old munitions district, and it had grown worse on the gallery above the Dovrin Line. She had slept since then, she supposed. In pieces. An hour folded on a stool in the clinic's supply room. Half an hour on her cot before she woke with her heart going like a struck bell. She was a healer, and she could have written the chart without looking: sleep debt, adrenal exhaustion, fine-muscle tremor, appetite suppressed. She could have prescribed for it. She could not prescribe for the reason.

Below her, through the transparisteel, the hangar was lit for the morning shift. The new deck plating was so clean it shone. The scorch marks were gone. The twisted gantry had been cut away and replaced within days, and two gunships already sat on the pads where the blast had been worst, a squad of clones in white armor running a fuel line between them as if nothing had ever happened on that floor.

That was the part she could not bear. They had rebuilt it as a hangar.

She had thought the war would look different once it came home. For three years she had stood in field hospitals with soldiers' blood to her elbows and listened to Masters she revered talk about acceptable losses and strategic objectives. She had watched Padawans her own age, Ahsoka among them, command battalions of men bred for dying. She had watched the Order that was meant to keep the peace learn to wear a general's rank as though it fit. And she had believed, still believed, that someone had to make the Jedi look at what they had become. The war had to come into the Temple itself, where no one could call it distant.

It had come. They had swept up the dead, laid new plating, parked gunships where Jedi had burned, and called it resilience. And then they had looked for someone to blame, and they had found Ahsoka.

Barriss pushed back her left sleeve. The bruise on her forearm had gone from purple to a sick green at its edges, but its shape was still clear: a long curve, the exact arc of one of Ventress's hilts, where Skywalker's blade had come down on her crossed guard and driven the metal back into her own arm. She had felt that blow to the shoulder. He had not been trying to disarm her. She could have closed the bruise in a breath with the Force, the way she closed a hundred small hurts a week for other people. She had not.

Footsteps in the corridor. She pulled the sleeve down.

"You are up early again," Luminara Unduli said.

Her Master came into the alcove with her hands folded in her sleeves and her hood down, the dark patterns on her chin and cheeks sharp in the candlelight. She did not light anything. She stood beside Barriss and looked at the shelf the way she looked at everything, as though it had something to teach her if she was patient.

"The clinic opens at first light," Barriss said.

"The clinic opens at the second hour. It is barely the first."

"Then I'm early."

Luminara almost smiled. She put a hand on Barriss's left arm, a small gesture of comfort, and Barriss flinched before she could stop herself.

The hand withdrew. "You're hurt."

"The new supply droid stacks the bacta crates too high. One came down on me."

"Then you should tell it to stop."

"I have. It doesn't listen."

"Few things do, lately." Luminara's gaze had gone to the end of the lowest tier, to the flame with no plate. "Who is that one for?"

Barriss looked at it. "Anyone who was missed."

"That is kind of you," Luminara said, and Barriss felt the words go into her like a splinter.

They stood together without speaking. Down in the hangar, someone laughed. Then Luminara drew a breath in the particular way she did before she said a thing she had already decided to say.

"The charges against Ahsoka were filed late last night. The Council was informed at midnight."

Barriss kept her eyes on the candles. "What are they asking for? Prison?"

Luminara was silent long enough that Barriss turned to look at her.

"Admiral Tarkin has asked for death."

The taper in Barriss's hand snapped.

She did not remember picking it up again. She looked down and there it was, two pieces of wax held together by the wick, and her fingers were white around it. The cold went through her from the top down, scalp to spine to the backs of her knees, the way it did in the moment before a patient crashed.

She had seen the shape of it so clearly. Expulsion first, because the Order would never hand one of its own to the Republic, so the Order would cut her loose. A hearing. A conviction, perhaps, and years in some clean Republic facility where they would keep her safe until the war ended and the truth came out of its own accord, and the Jedi would have to live with the knowledge of what they had thrown away. That had been the price. A terrible price, but survivable. She had never once let herself picture the other shape. She had not let it into the room.

"They can't," she said.

"The Republic can," Luminara said quietly. "She is no longer ours to protect. The Chancellor presides. He is a fair man, and Senator Amidala is no fool. We must trust that."

Barriss heard herself say, "Yes, Master."

Luminara touched her shoulder, the right one this time, and left for the Council chamber. Barriss stood alone with the broken taper in her fist and the plateless candle burning at the end of the row, and she found that she could not tell anymore whom it was for.

* * *

The satchel had been under her cot for two nights.

She had told herself the first night that she was too tired, and the second that there were too many people awake in the residential wing. On the third night, after a double shift in the clinic, she took it down.

Her first plan had been the hangar's waste reclamation furnace, which burned hot enough to reduce durasteel to slag. But the hangar had changed since the bombing. There were two Coruscant Guard troopers at every access now, with scanners, and a duty officer who logged every Jedi in and out by name. Barriss had stood at the end of the corridor and looked at them, their red-marked helmets turning to watch the doors, and understood with a kind of sick humor that they were guarding the entrance she had already walked through.

So she went down instead.

The Temple stood on older foundations than most of its residents liked to think about. Beneath the training levels and the archives' deep stacks and the power trunks that fed the spires, there were maintenance galleries no one used anymore, cut when the ventilation had been built along a different plan, then abandoned when it was rebuilt. Barriss had found them as a youngling, on a dare from two crechemates who had lost their nerve at the second ladder. Luminara had found her afterward, filthy and delighted, and had said nothing to her for an entire day. It had been the most effective punishment of her childhood.

She climbed down past the humming trunks, past a service droid that swiveled its photoreceptor at her and went back to its work. The air grew colder and smelled of dust and old lubricant. The light strips here were dim and many were dead; she lit the way with a glow rod from the clinic stores. Three levels below the eastern foundation she found the crawlway she remembered: a square duct mouth at knee height, its durasteel grille stenciled with a faded yellow C-31 and crossed with brittle seal tape from some survey a generation gone.

The latches had rusted shut. She reached into them with the Force and they gave with a shriek that went echoing down the gallery. She froze, listening. Nothing. Only the distant pulse of the power trunks, like a heart in another room.

She knelt on the cold floor and opened the satchel.

The mask came out first. Bone-white, the eye-slits slanted, a crack along one cheek she did not remember making. She set it face-up on the floor and it looked at the ceiling with nothing behind it. Then the sabers, one and then the other. Ventress's hilts were heavier than her own, curved like the neck of some long-legged bird, cold even after days wrapped in cloth.

She had come down here to destroy them. She knew how. Every Jedi knew, in theory, though no Jedi was supposed to do it: you reached into the hilt, found the crystal, and closed the Force around it until it broke.

She held the first hilt in both hands and reached.

The crystal was there at the center, and it was wrong. She had touched her own crystal a thousand times and it had always felt like clear water. This one felt like a wound. It had been bled, forced into red by someone's will, and it hummed with an old pain that had never been allowed to close. Barriss had spent her life closing wounds. She pressed on it, carefully, the way she would probe a fracture, and the crystal answered with something so like a cry that she dropped the hilt in her lap.

It would be easy. One push. The thing was already broken; she would only be finishing it.

She told herself that breaking a crystal would send a ripple through the Force, and that some Master meditating in the spires far above her might feel it and come looking. That was true, possibly. It was also a lie, and she knew it while she was telling it. She could have taken her own blade to the mask in a heartbeat. She did not do that either.

Instead she took a folded surgical drape from the bottom of the satchel, the kind the clinic used for sterile instruments, and laid it open on the floor. She set the two hilts in it side by side, then the mask on top. She folded the corners in the order she had been taught as an apprentice healer, left, right, bottom, top, so that nothing touched the outside. Her hands were steadier doing it than they had been in days. That frightened her more than anything else.

She pushed the bundle into the crawlway at arm's length, then further with the Force, until she felt it settle around a bend into a dry niche where the duct turned. She closed the grille. She bent the latches back with the Force until they bit, and smoothed the old seal tape down with her thumb until it looked undisturbed, or nearly.

Then she sat back on her heels in the cold with the glow rod in her lap and made herself think it all the way through, the way she would make a frightened patient look at a wound.

Those sabers were the only proof. Anyone who found them, anyone who scanned them, would know the red blades in the warehouse and on the Dovrin gallery had not been Ventress's in Ventress's hands. They were the one thing in the galaxy that pointed away from Ahsoka. And Barriss had just carried them past a furnace and three levels down and wrapped them as carefully as a surgeon's tools.

She did not know why. She sat there until the glow rod dimmed, trying to know, and could not.

*Tomorrow*, she thought. She had thought it before, in an alley, with the incinerator chute open in front of her. She climbed back up the ladders in the dark and did not look behind her.

* * *

The Republic Center for Military Operations smelled of floor polish and recycled air. A Coruscant Guard lieutenant at the security desk scanned her identification, then held out his hand without a word.

Barriss unclipped her lightsaber and laid it in the tray. He sealed it in a numbered drawer and gave her a chit. She looked at the drawer and thought of two other hilts, green and yellow-green, laid in a tray like this one at a door like this one, and of the trooper who had logged them and stood back to let Ahsoka walk into a room where a woman was going to die.

"Detention level four," the lieutenant said. "Fifteen minutes. A guard remains present."

"I'm here as a healer," Barriss said. "The prisoner's counsel reported skin damage from the binders."

"A guard remains present," he said again.

The corridor on level four was long and white and freshly resealed. She knew, because the Holonet had shown it, that this was where the three troopers had died. The floor had been replaced. Nothing marked the place at all. She walked over it with the guard's boots ringing behind her and kept her eyes on the cell doors ahead, and her hands folded in her sleeves where they could shake in private.

Ahsoka was sitting on the slab with her back to the wall and her bound hands in her lap. When she saw who was outside the field, her whole face changed. She was on her feet before the guard had keyed the panel.

"Barriss." She sounded so glad. "They let you in?"

"I told them your wrists needed looking at."

Ahsoka held them up. Beneath the edges of the binders the orange skin had been rubbed raw in a band all the way around, cracked in places. "Huh," she said. "Well. Now they do."

The guard lowered the field and stepped back against the opposite wall, and Barriss went in.

She knelt. Up close Ahsoka looked thinner than she had a week ago, the white markings stark on her face, and her montrals bare where the Padawan beads had hung. Barriss had heard about the beads. Everyone in the Temple had heard. She took Ahsoka's wrists in her hands, careful of the binders, and closed her eyes and reached.

Ahsoka's presence in the Force was the same as it had always been: quick and bright and warm, like standing too near a fire. It was battered now, dimmed at the edges with exhaustion and something harder, but it opened to Barriss at once, without any guard at all. Ahsoka trusted her. The trust came through her hands like heat, and Barriss had to hold very still under it while she knit the broken skin closed, cell by cell, the way she had been taught.

"That's better," Ahsoka said. "That's a lot better. Thank you."

"Don't." It came out too sharp. Barriss opened her eyes. "It's nothing. It's what I do."

"It's not nothing to me." Ahsoka flexed her fingers inside the binders. "I saw you, at the Temple. When they took me in. I'm sorry I didn't say anything. I couldn't look at anybody. If I'd looked at anybody I wouldn't have made it into that lift."

"You don't owe me an apology," Barriss said, and the true weight of that sentence sat in her mouth like a stone.

Ahsoka sat back down on the slab. Barriss stayed kneeling on the floor in front of her, because her legs did not feel reliable.

"You heard what Tarkin's asking for," Ahsoka said.

"Yes."

"Padmé says he always asks for the most so that anything less looks like mercy. She says it's a tactic."

"Is that supposed to comfort you?"

"It's supposed to keep me from screaming." A flicker of the old crooked grin. "Mostly it works."

"I thought—" Barriss stopped.

"What?"

"I thought they would only expel you. I thought that would be the worst of it."

"So did I," Ahsoka said. "Turns out that was the good part."

The guard shifted against the far wall. Ahsoka glanced at him, then leaned forward so that her voice dropped beneath the hum of the field emitters.

"Listen. I haven't told them who sent me to the warehouse. Tarkin's going to ask. Padmé's going to ask, probably. I'm not going to say."

Something in Barriss went entirely still.

"You should tell them," she heard herself say. "It might help you."

"It wouldn't. It'd give Tarkin one more Jedi to drag in front of the Senate, that's all. They'd say you helped a fugitive. The Council would do to you exactly what they did to me." Ahsoka's jaw set. "One of us is enough. You only told me what you knew. It's not your fault someone was waiting there. Whoever it was must have been tracking me since the tunnels. I was careless."

"You weren't careless."

"I walked into a trap with a Sith assassin for backup. That's pretty careless, Barriss." She tried to smile again and didn't manage it this time. "But you picked up. When I called. Everyone else in that Temple was hunting me, and you picked up and told me where to look. I'm not going to forget that."

Barriss could not breathe. The cell was very small. The light from the field emitters lay on Ahsoka's face and on the raw new skin of her wrists, healed now, pink, the only good thing Barriss had done in a week.

"Ahsoka," she said, and did not know what was going to come after it.

"Will you do something for me?"

"Anything."

"There's a memorial shelf, by the hangar. For the bombing." Ahsoka looked down at her hands. "The three troopers who died out there. CT-2261. CT-4408. CT-9170. I had the numbers read to me in the charge sheet. Nobody at the Temple's going to light anything for them, not for the men I'm supposed to have killed. Would you? Light one for each?"

The corridor outside. The replaced floor. Barriss had not known their numbers. She had not let herself know them.

"Yes," she said.

"Thanks." Ahsoka let out a long breath. "Anakin's turning the lower levels upside down looking for Ventress. She was there, at the end. She might have seen something. If anyone can find her, he can."

"Time," said the guard.

Barriss got up. Her knees ached from the floor. At the threshold Ahsoka said, "Barriss?" and she turned.

"Will you be there? Tomorrow?"

"Yes."

"Good." Ahsoka's montrals lifted, just slightly. "I'll look for you."

The field came up between them with a soft electric snap. Barriss walked back down the long white corridor over the place where three men had died under her blades, and the guard's boots rang behind her, and she did not look down.

* * *

She lit the three candles that evening, at the end of the lowest tier beside the one that had no plate. She said each number aloud as the wick caught, softly, so that no one passing in the corridor would hear. Twenty-two sixty-one. Forty-four oh-eight. Ninety-one seventy. Four flames now with nothing in front of them, in a row, like a sentence no one else could read.

Then she went to her Master's quarters.

Luminara was kneeling before the long window in meditation, the lights of the city traffic sliding over the floor behind her. She did not open her eyes when the door slid aside. "Come in, Barriss."

Barriss knelt beside her. For a while she tried to meditate, because it was what they did together in the evenings and had done since she was small, and because if she breathed slowly enough she might find the words. The Force moved around her, vast and patient. It did not offer anything. It never did, lately. She had begun to think it was waiting.

"Master," she said. "May I ask you something?"

"Always."

"If a Jedi knew something. Something that might change what happens tomorrow." She kept her eyes on the window. "What should she do?"

Luminara opened her eyes.

"Hypothetically," Barriss said.

"Hypothetically," Luminara said, "she would bring it to the Council at once. Tonight. And if she had kept it to herself until now, she would answer for that, too." She turned to look at Barriss fully. "You understand what is happening in that chamber tomorrow is not only about Ahsoka. Admiral Tarkin and half the Senate are waiting to see the Jedi protect their own. Every newsfeed on the Holonet is waiting for it. The war effort cannot afford a scandal in the Temple, and so the Order cannot afford even the appearance of one. Whatever we do now, we must do correctly."

*The war effort.* Barriss heard the words land and settle, and something cold in her nodded at them, as if they confirmed a diagnosis. Even now. Even with a girl's life on the table, the first thing the Order counted was what the war could afford.

"Is there something you know?" Luminara asked.

Barriss looked at her. Her Master's face was serene and certain, the face that had taught her to read and to heal and to hold a blade, the face that had said nothing for a whole day to a filthy, delighted youngling and made her understand. She tried to imagine that face as it would be after she spoke. She found that she could imagine it exactly.

"I—" she said.

Luminara waited.

"I'm afraid for her," Barriss said.

Her Master's expression softened. "As am I." She reached out and took Barriss's hand, and as she did, Barriss's sleeve slid back from her wrist.

Luminara looked down at the bruise. The green-edged curve of it lay across Barriss's forearm in the window light, plain as a signature.

"That," Luminara said, "is not from a crate."

"Sparring," Barriss said. "A training remote. I got careless."

"A remote did not leave that shape."

"A practice staff, then. I don't remember. I've been tired."

Luminara studied the bruise a moment longer. Then she laid two fingers over it, and Barriss felt her Master's healing gather, gentle and practiced, ready to close it.

"Don't," Barriss said. "Please. I'd like to keep it. For a little while. To remind me."

"Of what?"

"To be more careful."

Luminara took her fingers away. Her eyes stayed on Barriss's face, and for one long moment Barriss saw something move behind them, a question being turned over and not yet asked. Then it was gone, put away somewhere for later, and her Master only said, "That is a strange discipline. But you were always hard on yourself." She squeezed Barriss's hand. "Ahsoka's fall is not your failing. Remember that tomorrow."

Barriss bowed her head so that she would not have to answer.

"We sit in the gallery for the opening session," Luminara went on. "The Council wishes the Order to be seen there. She should have some friendly faces." She paused. "Master Kenobi tells me Skywalker has gone down into the lower levels again tonight. He has not slept either, it seems. He is convinced Ventress survived the fall, and he says he will not stop until he finds her."

Barriss lifted her head.

"Survived," she said.

"So he believes. There was no body." Luminara closed her eyes again and turned back toward the window. "If she lives, she may know who was behind that mask. The Force may yet give Ahsoka a witness. We can hope for that much."

Barriss went back to her quarters and lay down on her cot fully dressed, with the lights off and her hands folded on her chest to keep them still.

Ventress, who had been on that gallery. Ventress, who had felt the attacker in the Force, close enough to touch. Ventress, who had been with Ahsoka when Ahsoka made a call to a friend in the Temple. For three days she had searched the Holonet for word of a body and found none, and told herself it meant the lower levels had swallowed it. She had not let herself think it might mean nothing had fallen far enough.

If Skywalker found her, Ahsoka might live.

If Skywalker found her, Barriss would be the one in the cell.

She lay in the dark and tried to work out which of those two things she was hoping for, and she could not. That was the worst discovery of the whole week: not the bombing, not the troopers, not the girl thanking her for a lie. All of those she had reasons for. This, she had none.

Far beneath her, past the training levels and the archive stacks and the humming trunks, behind a grille she had bent shut with her own hands, Ventress's sabers lay folded in a sterile drape, waiting for someone to come looking.

*Tomorrow*, Barriss thought, and for the first time in her life she did not know what the word meant.

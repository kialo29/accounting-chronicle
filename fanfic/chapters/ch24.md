# Chapter 24: Senator Amidala's Guests

There was a Dathomirian in Padmé's reading chair, and she was eating the last of the pallies.

Padmé stood in the doorway of her own sitting room with her cloak half off one shoulder and the day's datapads under her arm, and for a long moment neither of them spoke. Asajj Ventress peeled the fruit with a short, ugly knife, letting one long curl of skin drop onto the carpet Padmé's mother had sent from Theed. The lights were at their evening setting. The veranda doors stood open to the traffic lanes, and the wind that came through them smelled of rain and hot metal.

"Your security," Ventress said, "is decorative."

Padmé did not reach for the blaster in the side-table drawer. Ventress would be faster, and the knife had gone still the instant Padmé's hand twitched. "The building has a Guard detail," she said. "Doubled, since Narkis."

"Yes. We counted them." Ventress tipped her head toward the veranda. "The one with the shoulder knows their rotation. Apparently it hasn't changed in three years. Your Republic is very loyal to its habits."

A second shape stepped out of the shadow by the veranda doors, and Padmé forgot about the blaster.

Ahsoka was taller. That was the first absurd thing Padmé noticed: five months, and the girl she had sat beside at the defense table had grown. She wore a patched spacer's jacket over grey thermals. A raw pink ring circled her neck where something had been fastened for a long time, and a small healing burn sat under her jaw. The saber at her belt wasn't hers. Her face was thinner, older, very still, and she held herself the way people did when they expected to be sent away.

"I'm sorry," Ahsoka said. "I didn't know where else—"

Padmé dropped the datapads. She crossed the room, took Ahsoka by both shoulders and pulled her in, and felt the girl go rigid and then not rigid at all. Ahsoka smelled of engine coolant and sulfur and someone else's coat. The tips of her montrals brushed Padmé's temple; they hadn't, before.

"You're alive," Padmé said into her shoulder. It came out foolish. She didn't care.

"Mostly." Ahsoka's voice was muffled. "You need to hear the rest before you decide anything. You can still call the Guard. I'd understand."

"Don't be ridiculous."

"She means it, ma'am," said a third voice, and a clone trooper limped in from the veranda.

He wore mismatched civilian layers, his left arm bound against his chest under the jacket, and he had the glassy, hollowed look of a man running a fever and ignoring it. A numeral five was inked at his right temple. Padmé looked at it, and at him, and the floor seemed to tilt a little under her.

She knew that mark. Not from the Holonet, which had shown only a grainy security still and a designation. She knew it because Anakin had sat on the edge of their bed at three in the morning, two months ago, and told her about the trooper who had tried to kill the Chancellor and gone down a refinery shaft. *He used to be one of the best men I had.* He had turned his mechanical hand over and over in the lamplight as though checking whether it was still his.

"You're dead," Padmé said.

"Yes, ma'am." The trooper drew himself to something like attention, which seemed to cost him. "CT-5555. Fives. Officially deceased, recovery not attempted. I'd be grateful if the paperwork stayed that way."

"You attacked the Chancellor."

"No, ma'am. I was drugged, and then I was in a room with him, and then I was a traitor." His jaw worked. "I can tell you the rest. You might not want it."

Ventress bit into the pallie. "Oh, she'll want it. They always want it. Then they never sleep again."

Padmé looked at the three of them standing on her carpet — a convicted bomber, a Separatist assassin, a dead man. Far below, a transport sounded in the lanes. Tomorrow at ninth hour the Judiciary oversight subcommittee would hear Tarkin's people propose a fourfold expansion of the collar program, and she had meant to stand and say that a girl named Ahsoka Tano had been made to wear one for a hundred and eighteen days. On her desk, under the subcommittee briefing, lay a courteous notice from the Admiral's office reminding former counsel of their legal obligation to report any contact from the escaped prisoner.

"Sit down," she told Fives, "before you fall down. Ahsoka, close the veranda. And you." She looked at Ventress. "That was the last pallie."

"I know," Ventress said. "I checked."

* * *

Padmé made tea because her hands needed work. She scalded the side of her right hand on the kettle while Fives was explaining, in a flat report voice, what the Kaminoans had grown inside every clone's skull and what the Chancellor had kept in a small vial after it was cut out of his. She didn't feel the burn until Fives stopped talking and nodded at it.

He opened a battered medkit one-handed. A strip of tape across the lid read ONE EVERY TWELVE HOURS. DON'T BE A HERO. He pressed a square of bacta onto her skin with the care of a man who had been patched more often than he had patched anyone else, and the cool, sharp smell of it spread through the kitchen.

"Rex's," he said, seeing her read the lid.

"Rex knows you're alive."

"Rex knows a lot of things he isn't saying." Fives snapped the kit shut. "That's how he stays alive."

She didn't ask what the thing in the troopers' heads was for. She found she did not want to know tonight, and that cowardice frightened her nearly as much as his story had. Something grown into every one of the men who guarded this building, the Senate, the Chancellor's own door. A report blaming a virus. A vial kept in a desk. It was too large and it had no edges. She put it where she put the worst intelligence briefings, in a locked room at the back of her mind, and turned to the thing she could hold.

"Why Coruscant?" she asked. "Every Guard officer on the planet has your face, Ahsoka."

Ahsoka sat at the kitchen table, both hands around a cup she hadn't touched. "Because I know who did it."

Padmé set the kettle down.

At the trial she had asked a dozen times, a dozen ways. In the cell. At the defense table. In the last hour before the verdict, when there had been nothing left to lose. *Who sent you to the warehouse?* And Ahsoka had looked at her with that stubborn, shuttered face and said, *I promised.* Padmé had built a defense around the hole where that name should have been, and watched Tarkin pour the whole Republic's fear into it.

"Tell me," she said.

Ahsoka's lekku shifted, a small involuntary movement, as though something cold had touched them. "Barriss," she said. "Barriss Offee."

For a moment the name meant nothing. Then it meant too much at once. A hooded Mirialan in the Jedi gallery, every day of the trial, at Master Unduli's side, green hands folded in her lap. During Ahsoka's testimony Padmé had glanced up at that bowed head and thought, *There is one person in this room suffering as much as I am.* She had been grateful for her.

"No," Padmé said. "She's a healer. She went to Narkis. Anakin said she went to Narkis to—"

"She treated me." Ahsoka's voice was very level. "My shin. My shoulder. The nerves in my neck. Her hands shook the whole time, and I thought it was because she was kind." She turned the cup a quarter turn on the table. "She's the one who called me to the warehouse. She visited my cell the day before the keycard. She bought the nano-droids from Dalo Pesk, and Pesk's dead. She was in the mask on the Dovrin platform. Anakin saw her fight. He told me it was Temple forms."

Padmé put her palm flat on the counter. The bacta patch was cool against the stone. "Letta Turmond," she said. "The footage. Your hand was raised."

"Mine was raised because Letta was choking. Hers was raised somewhere else."

"Stars." It was barely a word. Padmé thought of that silent footage played and replayed in the tribunal chamber, Tarkin's dry voice slowing it frame by frame, the senators leaning in. Six Jedi in the hangar. Three troopers cut down with Ahsoka's own blades. And a friend in the gallery, watching the Republic decide whether to kill the girl she had put there. "She sat there," Padmé said. "She sat there and listened to him ask for your death."

"And then she lit candles," said Ventress from the doorway. "Isn't that touching."

"Ventress." Ahsoka didn't look up.

"I'm agreeing with the Senator. It's monstrous. I'm simply impressed."

Padmé made herself breathe, and then made herself think like counsel, because that was the only use she could be. "What proof do you have?"

"Her." Ahsoka tilted her head toward Ventress. "A dead dealer who described green, patterned hands to her and wanted two thousand credits for a recording nobody's found. A bruise I never saw. The way Barriss's hand shook." At last she lifted the cup, then set it down untouched. "A Separatist assassin's word and my memory. You've stood in that chamber. You know what that's worth."

Padmé knew exactly what it was worth. It was worth Tarkin's thin smile.

* * *

She did not go to the subcommittee. She sent a written statement opposing the expansion and a message pleading illness, and spent the next day with the shutters drawn, her staff on leave and Threepio dispatched to the Naboo embassy to inventory a ceremonial archive that would occupy him for a week. By noon her dining table had become a war room.

Fives had brought the recorder. He set it in the middle of the table among the teacups: a phase-two trooper's helmet, scuffed, its white gone grey at the edges, with a deep dent above the brow that someone had never bothered to hammer out.

"Mine," he said. "Rex stuffed it in the bottom of my kit bag at Berth Nine without telling me. I found it on the Gravel Saint, under the bacta." Something moved in his face and was put away. "I put it into a wall the day they took her off the roster. He kept it."

"And it records," Padmé said.

"Every bucket records. Command uses it for after-action reviews and courts-martial." He tapped a seam beneath the left cheek guard. "Hardware timestamp, serial burned into the frame, sealed data chain. You can copy what's on it. You can't change it without the seal showing. Kix explained it to us once when Jesse wanted to erase something embarrassing." A faint ghost of a grin. "Jesse lost."

"Audio range?"

"Good enough across an open floor, if nobody's shouting over a saber."

Padmé turned the helmet toward her. The visor looked back at her, black and blank. "A sealed recording," she said slowly, "of a Jedi confessing in her own words. A recording made by a dead man, held by a fugitive, in the company of a Separatist." She looked up. "Tarkin would call it staged before the first frame finished playing."

"Which is why you're coming," Ahsoka said, and then, a moment too late, "if you'll come."

Padmé had already understood. A Senator of the Republic, standing in that warehouse, seeing with her own eyes and swearing to it afterward — a Senator's sworn statement went into the Senate record the moment she rose to give it, before any office could seal it, before anyone could decide the footage was a forgery. Without her, the helmet was a curiosity. With her, it was a second trial.

"Of course I'll come," she said.

"No," Ahsoka said. "Not of course. Listen to the whole plan first."

The warehouse was the one in the old munitions district, the building with half a roof where Ahsoka had been found among the nano-droid crates. Ahsoka would wait on the floor, in the open, where it had begun. Fives would take the old crane gantry along the north wall, helmet recording, with a line of sight down onto the floor and a stair behind him to the service lane. Ventress would hold the loading doors at the south end, where a person running would run.

"And my sabers," Ventress said, "come home to me."

"After," Ahsoka said. "Not before she's said it. Not while she's still talking."

"And if your friend lights them at you before she's said anything?"

"Then I'll deal with her."

"Will you." Ventress's smile was thin and curved like the hilts she'd lost. "You couldn't say her name for a week."

"I've said it now." Ahsoka's voice didn't rise. "She walks out of that warehouse alive. That's the condition. You agreed on Florrum."

"I agreed to a great many things on Florrum. Most of them involved that Weequay." But Ventress let it lie, and Padmé saw that whatever passed between those two, Ahsoka had won this part of it a while ago.

Padmé herself would come last. Ahsoka was firm: she would not be in the building when Barriss arrived. She would come down by public lift and transit after second hour, cloaked and alone, and climb the service stair to Fives in the gantry. If a red blade lit, she would not move toward it. If Fives said run, she would run.

"I've been shot at before," Padmé said.

"I know. By people who wanted you alive for a ransom." Ahsoka met her eyes. "Barriss doesn't want anything from you. That makes you nothing to her. Please."

Padmé gave her a pair of short-range comlinks from her household security case, old Naboo issue, off every Senate network, and Fives showed her two clicks and a pause — *sent* — and three clicks — *in position*. Ventress watched this with the expression of a woman watching children play at soldiers. Then Fives went to lie down on the couch with his fever shot and his eyes closed, and Ventress went out onto the veranda to glare at the city, and Padmé and Ahsoka were alone at the table with the helmet between them.

"There's one more thing," Ahsoka said. "Please don't tell Anakin I'm here."

Padmé had known it was coming. It still landed like a hand on her chest.

"He's been looking for you for five months," she said. "He's barely slept. Ahsoka, he—"

"He found me." Ahsoka looked at her own hands. "On Florrum. In a canyon. He told me how to get past the Judiciary team, and then he let me go."

Padmé didn't move. Anakin had come back to Coruscant with the Resolute, two days ago now, and had sent her exactly one message: *Back. Can't come yet. Debriefs.* Nothing about a canyon. Nothing about letting anyone go. She thought of him lying to a Council that already distrusted him, and of the Chancellor's patient, interested face, and a coldness climbed her spine.

"When I told him I knew who it was," Ahsoka went on, carefully, "and wouldn't say, the stones came up off the ground around him. He didn't even notice. If he knows it's Barriss, he won't stand in a gantry and wait for a confession. He'll—" She stopped. She did not finish it. Padmé was grateful, and ashamed of being grateful, because she had once sat in a garage on Tatooine and listened to Anakin finish that sentence himself, and she had never repeated it to a living soul.

"Afterward," Ahsoka said. "When it's on the record and she's in custody and nobody can make it disappear. Then he can know everything. Then he can be angry at me. I'd rather he was angry at me than—" Again she stopped. Then, choosing each word as if setting her feet down on ice: "He'll come back here eventually, won't he. When he's on Coruscant. He comes to see you sometimes. To talk. About the war."

The kitchen was very quiet. Padmé heard the couch creak as Fives shifted in his sleep. Ahsoka was not looking at her; Ahsoka was looking with great attention at the dent in the helmet, and her face had the particular stillness of someone handing over a gift wrapped so that neither of them would ever have to open it.

"Sometimes," Padmé said.

Ahsoka nodded once, as if that settled something, and did not ask anything more.

"I won't tell him," Padmé said. Saying it felt like signing a document she hadn't read all the way through.

"Thank you."

"Don't." Padmé stood and took the empty cups to the sink so that she didn't have to look at the girl's grateful face. "Don't thank me for this."

* * *

They left at dusk, over the veranda rail and into a hired air-taxi that Fives had summoned to a public platform two levels down, by the same gap in the Guard rotation they had used to come in. Ahsoka would send her message from the shadow of the Temple. Barriss's old comlink only answered on short range; it had to be close. Then the three of them would go down into the lower levels and wait.

At the rail Ahsoka turned back. The rain had started, a fine haze that blurred the lane lights to smears. "Second hour," she said. "Not before."

"Second hour," Padmé agreed.

Ventress was already gone. Fives sketched her a salute with his good hand and dropped out of sight. Ahsoka lingered one more beat, as if she meant to say something, and then she was gone too, and Padmé was alone in an apartment that still smelled of tea and bacta and coolant.

She cleaned. She was methodical about it, as she had been methodical before every hard vote of her life. Four cups washed and shelved. The pallie peel picked out of the Theed carpet strand by strand. The couch cushions turned over, the fever-shot wrapper burned in the kitchen incinerator, the dining chairs squared to the table. She left the bacta patch on her hand, because the burn was real.

She was sitting in the dark with a datapad she wasn't reading when the door chimed its private tone and Anakin came in.

He looked terrible. That was what she noticed first, before the relief, before the fear. His hair was wet from the landing platform, his tunic creased as though he'd slept in it for three days, which he probably had. The glove on his mechanical hand was new, and the knuckles of it were already scuffed. He stopped just inside the door and looked at her as if she were the one solid thing in a room built of smoke.

"I couldn't stay over there another night," he said.

"Then don't." She went to him, and he held on hard enough to hurt.

He wasn't hungry. She heated soup anyway, and he ate it standing at the counter, and halfway through he lifted his head and went still.

"Bacta," he said.

Her heart did something complicated. She held up her right hand. "The kettle. Last night. I'm an idiot."

He took her hand and turned it over, very gently, with the mechanical one. He looked at the patch for a long moment, and something passed behind his face that she couldn't read — a memory, she thought; something from somewhere else. Then he raised her hand and pressed his lips to the edge of the patch.

"Be careful," he said. "One of us has to be."

He talked, after. Not much, and not in order. The debriefs. The Council's long silences. Master Windu asking the same question four different ways. The Chancellor, who had been kind, who had told him that the offer stood: bring her in and she would be safe, she would be heard, he would see to it personally. Anakin said it as if it were the only rope left over a long drop.

"She's here," he said. "On Coruscant. I can't feel her. She's hiding herself, she's gotten good at it. But I know she's here." He looked at Padmé across the counter. "Has she come to you?"

The question was so simple. He asked it the way he'd ask whether she had eaten. He was too tired even to be suspicious; he was only hoping.

"No," Padmé said.

It was the first time. She had lied to the Senate about where she slept; she had lied to her family about why she didn't come home to Naboo more often; she had lied to the whole galaxy by omission every day for three years, for him. She had never once looked at him and lied *to* him about something that mattered. It went out of her mouth smooth and quiet and believable, like a coin dropping into deep water, and she felt it land somewhere inside her and stay.

"If she does," Anakin said, "you'll tell me."

"Of course."

Then the household comlink in her pocket clicked twice against her hip, and paused.

*Sent.*

Anakin glanced at the sound.

"Bail," she said. "About the subcommittee. He's furious I missed it."

That one was easier. That was the worst thing about it. The second lie slid out after the first as if the first had greased the way, and she understood in that moment how people became the kind of people they had never meant to be. Not with one great choice. With the second small one, and the way it didn't hurt as much.

He believed her. Of course he believed her. He finished the soup and let her lead him to bed and lay down beside her in his clothes, one arm heavy across her waist, and she listened to his breathing roughen and slow and deepen. She watched the chrono on the wall. She counted the minutes past midnight the way she had once counted votes.

At half past first hour, three clicks against her hip. *In position.*

At second hour she slid out from under his arm a fraction at a time. He murmured something without words and turned his face into the pillow. She dressed in the sitting room in the dark — plain trousers, plain boots, the hooded rain cloak she wore when she didn't want to be a Senator — and left her Senate comlink on the counter where it would be found if anyone looked, and clipped the small blaster from the side-table drawer to her belt under the cloak. At the door she stopped and looked back toward the bedroom.

Then she went out, and down, into the rain.

* * *

Anakin had not been asleep. Not really. He had been somewhere underneath it, where he'd lived for five months — a grey place where he could hear everything and rest from none of it.

He heard the door seal.

For a while he lay still, because he was so tired that the sound didn't mean anything yet. Then he reached across the bed and found the sheet cool, and the meaning arrived all at once.

He sat up. The apartment was dark. He reached out with the Force through the rooms, through the walls, the way he'd done ten thousand times without thinking, and found them empty. No Padmé. No Threepio. Only the rain on the veranda glass and a faint, sharp smell still hanging in the kitchen.

Bacta.

He had smelled bacta in a canyon on Florrum, in a stripped bunkhouse with a burn mark on the floor and nobody in it. He had told himself it meant nothing then. He told himself it meant nothing now — a kettle, a careless hand — and the telling didn't take.

He went to the window. Coruscant poured away beneath him, light over light, down into the haze where the lanes thinned out and the old districts lay dark and wet and forgotten. He hadn't let himself reach for her in days. Every time he had, there had been nothing — a closed door, a careful silence.

He reached now.

And she was there.

Not hidden. Not shielded. Far below him, in the deep wet dark of the lower levels, Ahsoka burned like a single lit candle — bright, steady, wide open, and waiting for someone.

Not for him. He knew the shape of her waiting for him. This was something else.

Anakin's mechanical hand closed slowly on the window frame until the metal sill creaked. Padmé's lie came back to him in her own calm voice — *No* — and with it, colder, the understanding that his wife and his Padawan had both looked at him and decided he could not be trusted with the truth.

He reached for his boots.

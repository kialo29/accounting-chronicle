# Chapter 5: The Chancellor's Mercy

The med-kit vendor had a burn scar across both jowls and a stall made of three welded cargo crates, and somewhere in the last ninety seconds he had decided to tell Anakin Skywalker everything he knew, because Anakin's mechanical hand was resting flat on the top crate and the durasteel under it had begun, very quietly, to bend.

"Bacta patches," the Sullustan said. "A sealant gun. Two ampoules of the good painkiller, not the cut stuff they sell down by the outflow. She paid in Hutt coin. Nobody pays in Hutt coin this high up."

Anakin looked around. They were somewhere beneath the old munitions district, under a drainage arch that wept rust-colored water onto the walkway in a steady patter. A sign above the next stall advertised fresh nerf in letters that had not been fresh in a decade. If this was high up, he did not want to know what low looked like.

"What else did she buy?"

"Nothing else. She asked." The vendor's eyes went to the hand on his crate. His name, according to the stall's faded license tag, was Tibbo Nask. "She asked where a person could find a berth-master who didn't scan faces. I told her the Undercroft. Berth Nine. I tell everyone the Undercroft. It's not a secret, General, it's just a long walk."

"When?"

"Two nights ago. Three now, I suppose." Tibbo glanced up at the light-strips along the arch, which had begun their slow shift from amber to white. Somewhere far above, it was nearly morning. "She looked bad. Purple all down the side of her face. She moved like her ribs were held together with wire. I gave her extra patches and didn't charge her. Don't tell anyone that."

Anakin took his hand off the crate. It left a shallow dent the shape of four fingers and a palm. He put a stack of credits beside it, more than the crate was worth, and was already walking before Tibbo could decide whether to be insulted.

The Undercroft was a long walk. He ran it.

He had been running for five nights. He had stopped feeling his feet sometime on the third, and on the fourth he had stopped answering the comlink at all, because the only people who called were Obi-Wan, who wanted him to come back to the Temple and rest, and Rex, who wanted to know if there was anything he could do, and the answer to both was the same and he could not say it to either of them. He had questioned spice runners and dock-rats and a Toydarian who sold forged clearance cards out of a laundry. He had broken one jaw he was not proud of. He had stood on the rim of the Dovrin platform where the fallen hab stack still lay heaped inside its emergency cordon, looking down into the drop where the masked figure had thrown Ventress, and he had stretched out with the Force until his head pounded, searching for one cold, furious presence among a billion others.

Coruscant gave him nothing. It never did. The planet was so loud with life that finding one person in it was like listening for a single voice in a stadium, and Ventress had spent years learning how not to be heard.

But a med kit was a fact. A berth-master was a fact. He could hold facts.

Berth Nine was a pit cut into the side of a dead foundation pillar, wide enough for a light freighter and not much else. The landing clamps were open and empty. A Pantoran woman in a stained work coat sat in a booth at the edge of the pit, eating noodles out of a carton and watching a podrace replay on a cracked screen. She did not look up when his shadow fell across her.

"The ship that was here," Anakin said. "Two nights ago, maybe less. Who flew it, and where did it go?"

"Lots of ships are here." She slurped. "That's what a berth is for."

He took out his lightsaber. He did not ignite it. He only set it on the counter between them, the way he had set his hand on Tibbo's crate, and let her look at it.

The Pantoran put down her noodles.

"The *Marrow Lark*," she said. "Corellian hauler, older than me. Captain's a Nikto named Grell. He took one passenger. Pale, bald, tattoos on her chin, face like somebody had used it to stop a speeder. She paid for the whole cabin." She tapped her screen and the podrace vanished, replaced by a departure log. "Lifted thirty-one hours ago. Filed for Ord Mantell."

Thirty-one hours. He had been in a cantina six levels up, thirty-one hours ago, holding a Rodian against a wall.

"Is that where they're going?"

The berth-master laughed, one short breath through her nose. "Everybody files for Ord Mantell, General. It's the flight-plan version of 'I was home all night.' Grell could be halfway to the Corporate Sector. He could be dumping her out an airlock over Nar Shaddaa. Could be anywhere that isn't here."

"Did she say anything? Leave anything?"

"She said if anyone in a Jedi tunic came asking, I should tell him—" The Pantoran stopped. She looked at the lightsaber on the counter and seemed to reconsider the exact wording. "She said to tell him she had better things to do than die for his Padawan."

Anakin stood very still.

Then he picked up his lightsaber and walked to the edge of the empty pit, and looked down at the scorched clamps and the oil-stained duracrete, and the stillness broke. He drove his right fist into the support beam beside him. The beam boomed the length of the pit. When he pulled his hand back, the glove had split across the knuckles and the metal underneath gleamed, unhurt, and the beam had a crater in it, and none of that changed the fact that the only witness who could say a second person had stood in that warehouse was somewhere in hyperspace, going anywhere that wasn't here.

His comlink chimed. Not Obi-Wan this time. Text only.

*Motion to delay denied. They reconvene at the eighth hour. Where are you?*

He looked at the chrono. It was the sixth hour and a half. The Senate district was the better part of a thousand levels above him.

He started running again.

* * *

He reached the tribunal chamber with lower-level grime still on his boots and the smell of the drainage arch in his clothes, and the Coruscant Guard sentries at the doors took one look at his face and stepped aside without asking for his credentials. The doors parted on a silence so complete he could hear the hum of the ceiling lights.

The clerk was already reading.

"—on the first count, conspiracy in the bombing of the Jedi Temple hangar, resulting in the deaths of Jedi, clone personnel, and civilian workers, the tribunal finds the accused guilty."

Anakin stopped in the aisle.

Ahsoka stood at the defense table in binders, her back to him, her shoulders squared. She did not move at the word. Beside her, Padmé had one hand flat on the table, as if she were holding it down.

"On the second count, the murder of Letta Turmond while in Republic military custody: guilty."

The gallery was full. The families of the hangar dead sat together on the right in their deep blue mourning, a band of dark color like a bruise across the seats. Someone in that band made a small sound, not quite a sob. On the left, in the section reserved for the Order, Anakin saw Master Windu with his arms folded and Ki-Adi-Mundi with his eyes half-closed, and Plo Koon beside them with his masked face tipped toward the floor. Obi-Wan sat at the end of the row. He was looking at Anakin. Anakin did not look back.

"On the third count, the murders of Coruscant Guard troopers CT-2261, CT-4408, and CT-9170: guilty. On the fourth count, escape from lawful custody and assault upon officers of the Republic: guilty."

The clerk lowered the datapad. "Guilty on all counts, Supreme Chancellor."

On the raised bench, Palpatine inclined his head, slowly, the way a man bows at a graveside. The senators at the curved desk below him sat like carvings. Riyo Chuchi had her eyes shut. Bail Organa had his hands folded and was studying them as if they belonged to someone else.

Ahsoka turned her head. Not much. Just enough that Anakin, still standing in the aisle like an idiot, could see her profile, and she could see him.

She looked at his boots first. At the filth on them. Then at his face. He saw her read it there, the whole night, the Undercroft and the empty pit and the *Marrow Lark*, and he gave her the smallest shake of his head because there was nothing else to give.

Her chin came up a fraction. Then she faced the bench again.

Tarkin rose from the prosecution table. He had not even put on a show of waiting.

"Your Excellency. The Republic is at war. Its enemies are not only beyond our borders but, as this tribunal has now found, within our most trusted institutions. The accused used the training, the trust, and the weapons given to her by this Republic to murder those who served it. Leniency in such a case would not be mercy. It would be an invitation." He folded his hands behind his back. "The prosecution asks that the sentence be death."

The word went through the chamber like cold air through an open hatch.

Anakin heard his own breath. He heard Padmé's chair scrape as she rose to object, and Palpatine lift one hand to still her, gently, the way one quiets a restless child.

"Senator Amidala, you will have your opportunity to speak to the sentence." The Chancellor's voice was soft and carried to every corner. "And I will not pronounce a judgment of this weight without reflection. This tribunal stands in recess until the fourteenth hour."

The chamber came apart into noise. Anakin was moving before the gavel finished sounding, down the aisle against the current of bodies, and Padmé met him at the rail with her eyes too bright and her voice very low.

"Did you find her?"

"She's gone. Off-world. Thirty-one hours ago."

Padmé closed her eyes for exactly one breath. When she opened them, she was a senator again. "Then I'll argue sentence with what we have. Service record. Age. The war. Anakin, your hand—"

He looked down. The split glove, the bare knuckles. He closed his fingers over them. Across the room, the guards were taking Ahsoka out through the side door, and she did not look back this time, and somehow that was worse.

"I have to go," he said.

"Go where?"

He was already walking. He did not answer, because he was not sure she would want to hear it, and he was not sure he wanted to say it aloud: that there was only one man in the chamber who could change the word Tarkin had said, and that man had looked down at Anakin from the bench, just for a moment, before the gavel fell, and his face had been full of sorrow.

* * *

The Chancellor's office was red and warm, too warm, the way it always was. The heat pressed on Anakin's face after the chill of the lower levels and made the grime on his skin feel thick. The tall windows looked out over the Senate district, all glittering spires and orderly traffic lanes, so far above the Undercroft that it might have been a different planet.

Palpatine did not keep him waiting. That was the first thing that undid him a little. The Chagrian aide at the outer door had barely spoken his name before the inner doors opened and the Chancellor himself was there, robes rustling, holding out both hands.

"Anakin. My dear boy. Come in, come in. You look as though you haven't slept in a week."

"Five days," Anakin said, and then, because his mouth had decided to say true things without consulting him: "Six, maybe."

"Sit. Please."

He didn't sit. He stood in the middle of the carpet with his ruined glove and his filthy boots, and Palpatine went to the window and stood beside it, half-turned toward him, patient as stone.

"She didn't do it," Anakin said.

"I know that is what you believe."

"It's not what I believe. It's what *happened*. There was someone else in that warehouse. There was someone else on the Dovrin platform. I fought them. I saw them." He nearly said it then: *they fought like us*. The words were right there, a Soresu turn on a catwalk, a parry he had seen a hundred times in the Temple training halls. He swallowed them. If the figure had been trained in the Temple, then the Temple had produced the bomber, and Tarkin would only say that Ahsoka had trained them. Every truth he had was a weapon that cut her. "I almost had the one witness who could prove it. She's gone. Thirty-one hours. I was thirty-one hours too slow."

"And so you have come to me." Palpatine's voice was not unkind. It was the opposite of unkind, which was harder to bear. "You understand what you are asking, Anakin. You are asking me to set aside the finding of a lawful tribunal on the strength of a witness who cannot be found and a fight that no one else saw."

"I'm asking you not to kill her."

The words came out much too loud for the room. Palpatine did not flinch. He only studied Anakin in silence, and then he crossed to him and laid a hand on his arm, just above the mechanical wrist, as if he knew precisely where flesh ended and metal began.

"Do you think I want to?" he said quietly. "I have known that child since she first came to you. I have watched her grow up in the reports from Christophsis, from Ryloth, from a hundred places whose names the public will never learn. I do not want to sign this. But I am not a king, Anakin. I am a servant of the Senate and of the law, and the law is a blunt instrument even in the gentlest hands."

"Then don't use it."

"If only it were so simple." Palpatine sighed and let him go. He went to his desk and stood behind it, touching the edge of it with his fingertips. "Tell me. Where were the Jedi this morning?"

Anakin stared at him.

"I saw Master Windu in the gallery," Palpatine went on, in the same mild tone. "I saw Master Mundi, and Master Koon, and your own Master Kenobi. They were very still. Did any of them come to this office to plead for her? Did any of them petition the tribunal, or speak to the senators, or offer so much as a character statement?" He paused. "You are the only one, Anakin. The only one who came."

Something in Anakin's chest tightened and burned. He thought of Windu's hand at Ahsoka's montral, unclasping her beads. He thought of Plo Koon's face turned to the floor.

"They expelled her so you could try her," he said. "They washed their hands."

"I will not speak against the Order. They have their reasons, I am sure." Palpatine's mouth moved in something that was not a smile. "But I will say that loyalty is a rarer thing than it ought to be, and that I have always admired it in you."

Anakin's knees were shaking. He realized it with a kind of distant surprise, the way he sometimes noticed a wound only after a fight. He thought about kneeling. He thought he might do it, here on the red carpet, if it would help.

"Please," he said. "I'll do anything. Whatever you need. Just—not this. Not her."

The Chancellor's face changed. For a moment it looked almost stern.

"Don't say that, my boy. Not to anyone. Not even to me." He came around the desk again and rested both hands on Anakin's shoulders. They were light hands, an old man's hands, and they held him perfectly still. "Men in my position remember such things, whether we wish to or not. And you should never put yourself in anyone's debt for something they ought to do freely."

"Then will you?"

Palpatine held his gaze. The window light was behind him, so that his face was in shadow and only his eyes were clear, and they were wet.

"I can promise you nothing," he said. "I wish I could. I can promise only that I will listen to my conscience, and that I will remember everything you have said to me today. Now go back. Wash your face. Let her see you there when it is read." His hands tightened, briefly. "Whatever happens, she should not be alone for it."

Anakin went. He did not wash his face.

* * *

At the fourteenth hour the chamber was fuller than it had been in the morning, as if word had spread through the Senate that something worth watching was about to happen. Holonet drones hovered at the back, their lenses glinting. Anakin took the seat in the third row behind the defense table, the one he had left empty during closings, and gripped the rail in front of him with both hands.

Padmé spoke to the sentence for twenty minutes. She spoke about a girl who had been given to a war at fourteen and had never once refused an order that would save lives. She spoke about Christophsis and Ryloth and an ice world where Ahsoka had kept younglings alive. She spoke about the Republic's long tradition of distinguishing between justice and vengeance. It was the best speech Anakin had ever heard her give. He watched the senators while she gave it. Mon Mothma looked at Padmé with something like grief. The others looked at their hands.

Tarkin rose and said four sentences and sat down. He did not need more.

Then Palpatine stood.

"Ahsoka Tano," he said. "Please rise."

She rose. The binders clinked once. She was so much smaller than the bench, Anakin thought. She had always been small, and she had always moved so that nobody noticed, and now she was standing still and there was nothing to hide it.

"This tribunal has found you guilty of crimes against the Republic, against the Jedi Order you once served, and against the soldiers who defended you. The law of the Republic in a time of war is clear. For crimes of this nature, committed in this manner, the sentence prescribed is death."

Ahsoka did not move.

Anakin's mechanical hand closed on the rail. He felt the metal give under his fingers and could not make them stop.

"However."

The word was very quiet. The whole room leaned toward it.

"The Senate, in its wisdom, has entrusted this office with certain emergency powers for the duration of the present conflict. Among them is the power of clemency." Palpatine looked down at Ahsoka, and his voice softened until it was almost tender. "I cannot ignore that the accused has bled for this Republic since childhood. I cannot ignore that she is young, and that the war has asked of her what it should ask of no one. Let it never be said that the Republic forgot those who served it, even in their darkest hour. By the authority vested in me, I commute the sentence of death."

A sound went through the gallery, part gasp and part protest. In the deep blue band on the right, someone stood up and was pulled down again.

"In its place, the condemned will serve a sentence of imprisonment at the Republic Penal Facility on Narkis, for the remainder of her natural life."

Anakin was watching Ahsoka. He would remember that afterward, that he had been watching her and not the bench, and so he saw it.

At *death*, she had not moved at all. She had been ready for death. He had seen her ready for it a dozen times, on a dozen battlefields, the stillness she wore like armor when the odds had stopped mattering. But at *life*, something went out of her. Her shoulders did not drop; they locked. Her lekku, which had been still, drew in tight against her back. Her lips parted, and he saw them shape the word without sound, *life*, as if she were testing whether it would hold her weight.

She was seventeen.

Then she turned her head and found him in the third row, and for one long moment she looked at him the way she used to look at him across a battlefield when the plan had gone wrong and she needed to know what came next. He did not know what came next. He had no plan. All he had was the relief, which had arrived all at once and was so enormous that he could barely see around it.

She was alive. She was going to stay alive.

He looked up at the bench. Palpatine was gathering his robes to sit, and for an instant, just before he did, the Chancellor's eyes met his across the chamber. There was no triumph in them. Only a small, tired nod, as if to say: *I listened. It was all I could do.*

Gratitude went through Anakin like heat into frozen hands. It hurt the way that heat hurt, sharp and total, and he did not care. Someone had done something. After five nights of nothing, after a Council vote and a stolen set of beads and a gallery full of Jedi sitting with their arms folded, someone had finally reached out and caught her before she fell.

He looked at the Jedi gallery then. He made himself.

They were standing to leave. Windu's face was unreadable. Ki-Adi-Mundi was speaking quietly to Shaak Ti. Plo Koon had not risen; he sat with his clawed hands on his knees and his head bowed, and Anakin wanted to cross the room and ask him whether it had been worth it, whether it had felt like wisdom when he raised his hand in the Council chamber, whether it felt like wisdom now. Of all of them, only Obi-Wan was looking at Ahsoka, and he looked as if he had swallowed something sharp.

None of them had come to the Chancellor's office. None of them had gone into the lower levels. They had expelled her and sat down to watch.

The guards took Ahsoka out through the side door. She went without looking back. The evidence case with her lightsabers went after her on the hover-cart, #417 and #418, and Anakin watched it all the way to the door.

Obi-Wan caught him in the corridor outside. He had the look he wore before a negotiation, careful and tired.

"Anakin. You weren't at closings. Padmé—Senator Amidala—said you were following a lead."

"It went off-world."

"I'm sorry." And Obi-Wan sounded it, which made it worse. "For what it's worth, I believe the Chancellor did the best that could be done."

"The best that could be done." Anakin heard his own voice from a distance, very calm. "He did *something*, Master. That's more than I can say for the people who sent her to him."

Obi-Wan's face tightened. "The Council was in an impossible position."

"She was in an impossible position. You were in a gallery."

He walked away. Obi-Wan did not follow, and Anakin told himself he was glad.

* * *

The Chancellor did not watch the Holonet coverage. He had no need to. The commentators would be saying what he had arranged for them to say: that the verdict was just and the mercy was greater, that Palpatine had shown the galaxy the Republic still had a heart. By morning, a dozen systems that had wavered toward the Separatists would be reminded that the Supreme Chancellor was a kind man. That was useful. It was not the point.

He sat alone in the red office with the lights low and turned a small object over in his fingers, a stylus he had used to sign the commutation order. Narkis. Tarkin had recommended the place himself, and its warden, a careful woman of his own training who would keep the girl very cold and very quiet. A Jedi without the Force, in the ice, at the edge of nothing. Let her be forgotten for a while. Things that were forgotten could be remembered later, when remembering became convenient.

But the girl had never been the point either.

He thought of the boy standing on the carpet in his torn glove, shaking, offering *anything*. He had refused it, of course. A debt that was demanded could be resented. A debt that was refused could only grow, and the one who owed it would keep paying in installments of his own invention, for years, and call each payment love. Gratitude was the finest chain ever forged, because the prisoner made every link himself and polished it.

And the Jedi had helped. They always helped. They had given him the boy's anger as neatly as if they had wrapped it.

He set the stylus down and touched a control. Above the desk, the tribunal's internal recording bloomed in blue light: the gallery, frozen at the moment the word *death* was spoken. He let it run forward slowly, frame by frame, studying faces the way other men studied cards. The mourning families. The senators. The Jedi, so very still.

And there, at the end of the Order's row, half-hidden behind her Master's shoulder: a young Mirialan, her hood fallen back. At *death*, every other Jedi face in the gallery had gone smooth. Hers had come apart. Her mouth was half open, her eyes wide and wet, and they were not on the bench, and not on the condemned, but on the floor beneath her own feet, the way the guilty look at the place they expect to open.

The Chancellor stopped the recording. He looked at that face for a long time.

None of it was news. Days before the tribunal convened, an auxiliary maintenance camera in the upper service passage of the Military Operations Center had given him a hooded healer and a raised hand, in the very second Letta Turmond began to die. The camera had given him everything but a face. The gallery had just supplied it.

Then he saved the frame, and smiled for the first time all day.

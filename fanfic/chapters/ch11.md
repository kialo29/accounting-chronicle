# Chapter 11: Visitors

Sergeant Holt pulled Ahsoka off terrace nine in the middle of a cut, which never happened, and told her she had a visitor, which could not happen at all. She had been on Narkis fifty-three days. The rule was ninety. She had learned the rules here the way she had once learned saber forms, by repetition and by the bruises that came when she got them wrong, and so she walked up the Yard tunnel behind him with her gloved hands loose at her sides and her shoulders set for the Well.

"Is it the Commandant?" she asked at the second checkpoint.

Holt keyed the lift. He was a broad, tired man who smelled of caf and heat-strip ozone, and in fifty-three days he had never once told her anything he did not have to. "Commandant's going to be in the room," he said. "That's all I know."

The lift climbed out of Ring Two and the smell of the mess, leaving the long curved galleries of cells somewhere below in the blue ice. On Ring One the walls stopped being ice and became plated durasteel, painted a pale Judiciary grey, and the air was warm enough that her eyes stung. Holt stopped at a door with no marking on it and looked at her for a moment, as if there were something in his face he meant to put into words, and then he only said, "Two hours. She'll count them."

He opened the door.

The room was small and bare, a table bolted to the floor, two chairs, a ring welded into the tabletop for binders. Commandant Vorne stood against the far wall with her hands clasped behind her back, her uniform as sharp as a cut edge, her iron-grey hair pinned flat. And at the table, half out of his chair before the door had finished opening, was Anakin.

Ahsoka stopped in the doorway. Holt had to touch her elbow to move her forward.

She had asked him not to come. She had said it on Coruscant, in a detention cell, with her voice steady because she had practiced it, and he had looked at her the way he looked at an order he had decided to disregard later. She had known then. She had told herself he would listen.

"Sit, prisoner," Vorne said. "General Skywalker, you will remain seated as well. The restraints stay on. There will be no physical contact. If either of you stands without my leave, the visit ends."

Holt sat her down and ran her binders through the ring. The metal was cold even through the gloves. Then he went out, and the door sealed, and it was the three of them.

Anakin sat back down slowly. He moved as if something on his left side was holding a grudge against him, a stiffness through the ribs that he was trying not to show and showing anyway. He was thinner. The bones of his face had come forward. His hair was longer than he usually let it get, and there was a shadow under his eyes like a bruise that had settled in to stay.

"Hey, Snips," he said.

"I asked you not to come."

"You did." He tried a smile. It came out lopsided and tired. "You also asked me not to land on that dreadnought, and not to take the shortcut through the asteroid field, and not to call you Snips. My record's not great."

"I was serious."

"So was I." He looked at her neck. She watched his eyes find the collar, the grey band of it, the little blue status light that sat in the hollow of her throat. His jaw moved. "Does it hurt?"

"Sometimes." She lifted her chin a fraction, as if that would make it less visible. "Headaches. You get used to it."

"You shouldn't have to get used to it."

"There are a lot of things I shouldn't have to get used to." She heard how that sounded, sharper than she meant, and softened it. "It's fine. Really. I can still breathe. That's more than some people get."

He reached for her across the table. It was pure reflex, the same reflex that had pulled her out of a hundred bad places, and his gloved fingers were nearly on her wrist when Vorne said, "General," in a voice like a door closing. He stopped. He laid his hand flat on the tabletop instead, the right one, the metal one, a few centimeters from her binders. He left it there.

That was when Ahsoka noticed what was missing.

All her life Anakin had been loud in the Force. Not in a way most Jedi would have liked, but she had grown up beside it, the heat and pressure of him, a sun she could have found with her eyes shut on any battlefield in the galaxy. She had always known what he was feeling a half-second before his face did. Now there was nothing. He was a man sitting across a table. She would have to read him the way anyone else did, with her eyes, and she was out of practice.

"How did you get in here?" she said. "There's a rule. Ninety days."

"There's a permit," Anakin said. He glanced at Vorne. "The Commandant has it."

"I do," Vorne said. "Signed by the Supreme Chancellor, overriding the standing order of this facility. The Chancellor's signature supersedes my rule. It does not supersede my room."

"She's been very clear about that," Anakin said. "Several times. On the landing pad. In the lift. Twice in the corridor."

"Repetition is cheaper than incident reports, General."

Ahsoka almost laughed. It surprised her, and she looked down at the table to hide it, and when she looked up Anakin was grinning at her, the old grin, and for the space of a breath they were on the bridge of the *Resolute* and he had just done something reckless and gotten away with it.

Then it went out of his face, the way everything seemed to go out of it now, quickly, like a light someone was saving power on.

"The Chancellor," she said. "He did this?"

"He's been the only one." Anakin leaned forward. "Snips, listen. He's working on it. He can't say anything officially, not with the war, not with the Senate the way it is, but he as good as told me. When this is over, there's going to be an amnesty. A general one. He'll make sure your name is on it. He doesn't have to do any of this. He's doing it anyway."

She remembered the Senate chamber. Death, and then the long silence, and then *life*, and how she had come apart on that word in front of everyone because it was somehow worse. She remembered Palpatine's kind, sorrowful face above the bench. She owed him her life. That was true. It was also true that she did not know what to feel about a man who had pronounced her death in one breath and taken it back in the next, and she had not let herself think about it for fifty-three days, and she did not want to start now.

"That's good of him," she said.

"It's more than good. It's more than anyone else is doing." Anakin's voice dropped. "You know what the Council did? While I was trying to get a permit? Windu wrote to the Chancellor. Personally. Asked him to deny anything I applied for. Not 'consider.' Deny." He laughed, short and ugly. "They expelled you so they wouldn't have to look at you, and now they don't want me looking either."

"Anakin."

"They had you in front of them. They had you *standing there*, and they voted, and Windu took your beads off with his own hands like he was doing you a favor—"

"Anakin."

"—and Obi-Wan comes to me on Ordaine with a message from the Council, a *message*, like I'm some Padawan who needs to be told to go to bed—"

The cup on the table moved.

It was a plain steel cup of water, set out for the visitor, sitting a hand's width from his elbow. Nobody touched it. Ahsoka watched its rim dimple inward, a small dent, then another, the metal giving under a pressure that came from nowhere, folding with a thin, patient crackle. Water ran out across the table toward her binders.

Against the wall, Vorne's hand went to her belt, to the small black cylinder there. Ahsoka saw it, and saw Vorne realize in the same instant that it was useless, that it was keyed to one collar in this room and that collar was not the problem. The Commandant did not move further. Her face did not change. But her hand stayed where it was.

Anakin looked down at the crumpled cup as if somebody else had done it.

"Sorry," he said.

Ahsoka did not say anything. She could not feel him. That was the terrible part. A year ago she would have felt the anger rising in him like weather and known its shape and known it would pass. Now she only had the cup, and the water creeping across the table, and his face, which was not ashamed. It was only surprised. As if the anger was a fact he had stopped noticing, the way you stopped noticing cold on Narkis after the first week. She did not know what he was becoming. She did not know if he did.

"Who sent you to the warehouse?" he said quietly.

It was not what she had expected. "You asked me that before."

"I'm asking again. Because something's wrong, Snips. There was a broker in the lower levels, a Rodian, the kind of man who might have known who bought nano-droid crates out of an old munitions store. I found him. He was dead. Not a mark on him. Everything he had was gone, his records, his cam core. Someone cleaned up behind him like they were tidying a room." He spread his flesh hand on the wet table. "Whoever framed you is still out there, and they're careful, and they're not done. Whoever sent you to that warehouse knew where you'd be. I need to talk to them."

She had thought about that, in the Well. She had thought about very little else, lying on the cold shelf under lights that never went out, listening to Merrick Tal breathe through the vent. *Who knew where you would go.* She had followed the thought exactly as far as she could stand to, and then she had stopped, because the answer it wanted was impossible and she would not be the kind of person who thought it. Her friend had told her where the crates came from. Her friend had been trying to help. And if someone had known where she would be, it was because someone had been listening to that call, or watching the Temple, or watching her—and if that someone was cleaning up now, then her friend was not a suspect.

Her friend was in danger.

"I made a promise," Ahsoka said.

"To who?"

"That's the promise."

"Snips—"

"They helped me when no one else would. When you were *hunting* me." That landed. She saw it land and was sorry and did not take it back. "If I give you a name, you'll go after them the way you went after Ventress. And whoever killed your Rodian will see you do it."

Anakin was quiet for a long moment. The water had reached the edge of the table and was dripping onto the floor, one drop at a time.

"Then you be careful," he said finally. "In here. Somebody knew to put you on that terrace with the broken camera. You think that was the Trandoshan's idea?"

She had thought about that too. She had not come up with anything she liked.

"I'm always careful," she said.

"You have never once been careful in your life."

"I learned from the best."

That got a laugh out of him, a real one, small and cracked, and they talked after that about smaller things because there was nothing else to do with the time. She told him about Tess and the heat-packs and the rule about bare skin on metal, and he told her Rex had sent a message he'd been made to memorize word for word, and then recited it in a flat, gravelly voice that was so exactly Rex that Vorne's mouth twitched once against the wall. He told her the 501st had painted something on a gunship and refused to say what. He did not mention Padmé, and she did not ask, and both of them noticed.

At an hour and fifty-two minutes Vorne said, "Eight minutes," and Ahsoka felt the room tilt.

She leaned forward until the binders pulled against the ring.

"I need you to promise me something."

He looked at her. "Anything."

"Don't do anything stupid."

"You'll have to narrow that down."

"I'm serious, Skyguy."

The name went into him. She watched it, watched his face open and then close again around the hurt of it, and she was sorry for that too, and she had used it anyway because she needed him to hear her. "Don't do anything stupid," she said again. "Not for me. Not about the Council. Not about anyone. Whatever's going on in your head right now, whatever you're thinking about Windu, or—any of them. Don't."

"I'm not—"

"Promise me."

"I promise." He said it at once. He meant it. She could see that he meant it. He had always meant everything completely, right up until the moment he didn't. "I promise, Snips."

"Time," Vorne said.

Holt came in. Anakin stood when he was told he could and not before, which she understood was costing him something. He stopped at the door and looked back at her with his gloved right hand closed at his side.

"I'll come back," he said.

"Don't," she said, and found that she was smiling, and that her eyes were wet, and that she could not tell anymore which of those things she meant.

* * *

The chain went at the forty-first minute of second shift, thirty-nine days later, and it went with a sound like a blaster bolt.

Ahsoka heard it before she understood it. A sharp metallic crack from the top of the ramp, then a scream of links running loose over a pulley, and then the block—a full-cut slab of glacier ice the size of a speeder cockpit, bound for the refinery sleds—came loose from its harness and started down the chute toward the terrace below.

Toward Pell.

Pell was nineteen, a Rodian kid from Ring Five with a cough he wouldn't get checked, eight days into his sentence and still walking the terraces like they were floors. He was standing in the chute with his back to it, bent over a jammed sled runner, because nobody had told him yet that you never stood in the chute, or because someone had and he'd forgotten. There was no time to tell him now.

Before the collar she would have pulled him clear from twenty meters away without thinking. Her hand actually started to lift, and the old reflex reached for the Force and found the silence where it used to be, a step that wasn't there in the dark. She lurched.

Then she ran, which was against the rules.

The ice was slick and pitted. She took the slope in four strides, hit Pell in the back with her shoulder—the right one, the bad one, and something in it lit up white—and the two of them went sprawling across the terrace. The block came down the chute behind them with a grinding roar, clipped the lip of the terrace edge, and spun. A corner of it caught her left leg below the knee as it went past.

She felt the impact more than the pain at first. A blow like being kicked by a bantha, and then she was lying on her side on the ice, and the block was gone over the edge, and somewhere below there was a long, heavy crash and shouting from the lower crews.

Pell was saying something in Huttese, over and over, high and fast. Tess's face appeared above her, white cropped hair, eyes narrowed.

"Don't move."

"I'm fine."

"You're bleeding into your boot. Don't move." Tess pressed down above her knee with her good hand, the one with all its fingers. "I told you never to run."

"You did," Ahsoka said. The pain was arriving now, a hot, wet throb in her shin, and a second, deeper one in her shoulder. "He was in the chute."

"Everybody's in the chute sooner or later. That's why there's a rule about the chute." Tess looked up and bellowed for a guard, and then looked back down, and for a moment her expression was something Ahsoka had not seen on it before. "Stupid, brave kid," she said, more quietly. "Lie still."

The guards came. The medic on duty was a Judiciary corpsman with a field kit and very little interest, and he cut her boot away, looked at the gash, packed it, and told her it wasn't broken. It was deep, though. A long split down the outside of her shin where the block's corner had opened her up, and the bone beneath it was bruised black already, and when they got her on a stretcher and the stretcher tipped going into the lift, her shoulder made her see sparks.

"Medical," the corpsman told the lift. "Ring Two." And then, to her, with a sort of bored generosity: "You're in luck. The circuit healer's still on station. She was supposed to ship out at the end of this shift."

"Circuit healer?"

"Comes through every few months. Jedi, this time." He snorted. "Wasn't a lot of call for one until you showed up."

Ahsoka closed her eyes. A Jedi. The lift hummed. She thought about Plo Koon, who had looked at the floor. She thought about Windu's hands on her beads. She thought about which Jedi of all of them would agree to come to the edge of Wild Space and stand in a room with her, and could not think of a single one, and decided that whoever it was, she would be polite to them, because that was the only thing in this situation that was still hers to decide.

* * *

The medical bay on Ring Two was the warmest place on Narkis, which was not saying much. Six cots, a scrubbed steel counter, a bacta cabinet with a lock on it, one narrow window looking out into the blue twilight of the Yard. They put her on the cot nearest the window and ran a restraint strap across her right wrist to the frame. The guard went to stand by the door.

Someone in a dark hood was washing her hands at the counter, her back to the room.

"I'm told it's a leg," the healer said, without turning around. "And a shoulder. And that you ran."

Ahsoka knew the voice before the woman turned. She knew it the way she would have known her own name, and she pushed herself up on her good elbow so fast the shoulder screamed at her.

"Barriss?"

Barriss Offee turned from the basin. She was drying her hands on a cloth, carefully, finger by finger, and she pushed her hood back. The same green skin. The same diamond pattern across the bridge of her nose and beneath her eyes. She looked tired. More than tired—worn thin, the way Anakin had looked, as if something had been taking a little from her every night. But she was here. She was standing in a prison infirmary at the end of the galaxy, and she was looking at Ahsoka, and she was here.

"Hello, Ahsoka," she said.

Ahsoka could not think of a single thing to say. Her throat had closed. She lay back down on the cot because her arm would not hold her anymore, and stared at the ceiling, and blinked several times, hard.

"You came," she managed.

"It's a healer circuit." Barriss came over and set her satchel on the end of the cot. "Four facilities. Narkis is the last stop. Someone has to do it." She paused. "I asked for it."

"You asked."

"Yes."

Barriss knelt beside the cot and peeled back the corpsman's packing from Ahsoka's shin, and drew a breath through her teeth at what she found there. Then she put her hands on either side of the wound, not quite touching, and closed her eyes.

Ahsoka had been healed by Barriss before. On Geonosis, after. In the detention cell on Coruscant, her wrists, the night before the trial. Every time she had felt it in the Force first: a cool, clean current, like a hand drawn through water. Now there was nothing at all. Only warmth, spreading slowly from Barriss's palms into the torn meat of her leg. The throbbing eased. She felt, rather than saw, the edges of the wound drawing in, a strange crawling tightness, and she watched Barriss's face instead because she could not watch her leg.

Barriss's lips were pressed together. A thin line of sweat had broken out at her hairline, even in the cold.

"You can't feel it," Barriss said, without opening her eyes. It was not a question.

"No. It's—I can't feel anything. Not you. Not anyone." Ahsoka tried to make it a joke. "I'm a terrible audience."

Barriss's hands did not move, but something passed over her face. "I'm sorry," she said.

"It's not your fault."

Barriss opened her eyes then and looked at her, and it was such a strange look—long, and level, and very far away—that Ahsoka almost asked what was wrong. But Barriss only nodded and moved up to the shoulder, and laid two fingers along the collarbone, and frowned.

"This was injured before."

"A while back. There was a Trandoshan."

"There's always a Trandoshan." Barriss's hand moved, and the warmth sank in under the joint, and Ahsoka let out a breath she had been holding since the chute. "You should have had this seen to properly. It's healed crooked." Her fingers drifted higher. Paused at the edge of the collar, where the skin was red and swollen and had been for weeks. "And this."

"That's just the collar."

"I know what it is." Barriss's voice was very quiet. "The nerves under it are inflamed. They'll keep getting worse. Headaches. Eventually, seizures." She laid her palm flat against the side of Ahsoka's neck, just below the band of grey metal, and held it there.

For the first time in ninety-two days the ache in Ahsoka's skull went quiet.

She hadn't known how loud it was until it stopped. She lay on the cot with her eyes stinging and felt it ebb out of her like a tide going down, and Barriss's hand stayed where it was, warm and steady, and neither of them said anything for a long time.

"Do you remember the supply ship," Ahsoka said eventually. "After Geonosis."

Barriss's hand twitched. Just once. "Yes."

"I keep thinking about it. Here." She looked toward the window, the endless blue dusk of the Yard. "The cold. Everyone in the Deep hates the cold. I hate it. But I keep thinking—it saved us. Those things in your head couldn't stand it. I opened the coolant lines and froze us both half to death and it was the only thing that worked." She smiled at the ceiling. "So I tell myself it's on my side. The cold. It's stupid."

"It isn't stupid," Barriss said.

"You tried to kill me that day, you know."

"I know."

"You weren't yourself."

Barriss did not answer that. When Ahsoka looked, Barriss had turned her face toward the satchel, and was looking into it as if there were something in there she had lost.

"I never told them about you," Ahsoka said. "I want you to know that. Not Tarkin. Not Padmé. Anakin came here, a few weeks ago—he asked me twice. I didn't tell him either."

"Anakin was here?"

"He had a permit from the Chancellor."

Barriss went still. "Of course he did," she said, very softly, and then, before Ahsoka could ask what she meant: "The Chancellor arranged my transport, too. A courier from his own office. He asked to be remembered to you."

"He's been kind," Ahsoka said, and heard her own uncertainty, and let it go. "Anakin says someone's been cleaning up behind the bombing. A man who might have known something turned up dead. So if anyone ever asks you—about the warehouse, about what you told me—you be careful. Promise me. I don't want you anywhere near this."

Barriss laughed. It was a very small sound and there was no humor in it at all, and it stopped quickly. "I'll be careful," she said.

Her hand was still on Ahsoka's neck. Ahsoka could feel a faint tremor in it now, a fine shiver running through the fingers. The cold, she thought. Barriss had been on Narkis since the morning, in robes made for the Temple, not for ice.

"Do you still light candles?" Ahsoka asked. "For the three troopers. You said you would."

"Every morning." Barriss took her hand away at last and began packing her satchel, one item at a time, with great care. "I light four."

"Four?"

"There was—someone else. Who shouldn't have died."

Ahsoka thought of Letta Turmond, choking in a room with no one near her, a woman who had helped kill six Jedi and all those workers and clones, and who had still been a person, and who had died before she could say the one thing that would have saved Ahsoka's life. She thought how like Barriss it was to light a candle even for her, when no one else in the Temple would have. She swallowed.

"That's good of you," she said.

Barriss's hands stopped on the satchel's clasp.

"Ahsoka," she said, without looking up. "Can I ask you something?"

"Anything."

"Do you hate them? The Council. The Order." She did look up then, and her eyes were very bright. "After what they did. Do you hate them?"

It was the question Ahsoka had been asking herself on the cold shelf in the Well, in the dark, in the cut with the pick in her hands. She had never said the answer out loud. She found that she wanted to say it to Barriss, because Barriss would understand. Barriss had always been the one who asked the real questions, about the war and what it was doing to them, while everyone else asked about tactics.

"I'm trying not to," she said.

Barriss looked at her.

"It would be easy," Ahsoka went on. "That's the thing. Hating them would be the easiest thing in the galaxy. Every morning in this place I wake up and it's right there, waiting, and it would keep me warm. But I keep thinking—if I let it in, it won't stop with them. It'll be the guards next. Then the people in the Senate. Then everyone who believed it. And one day I'll look up and I'll be exactly what they said I was." She shook her head against the thin pillow. "So I'm trying not to. Some days I'm better at it than others."

Barriss did not move at all. Then she stood, and put the satchel over her shoulder, and her hands were shaking openly now—Ahsoka could see it from the cot, a tremor in the fingers as she fastened the strap, missed the clasp, fastened it again.

"You should rest," Barriss said. "The leg will hold if you keep off it two days. Tell them I said so. Tell them it's a healer's order." She hesitated. "I've left a note for the medical officer. About the collar. I don't know if anyone will read it."

"Barriss." Ahsoka reached for her with the hand that wasn't strapped down, and Barriss looked at it, the open grey-gloved palm, and after a second took it. Her fingers were ice-cold. "Thank you. For coming all this way. You don't know—" Her voice went, and she waited for it to come back. "You don't know what it means. That somebody from home still—"

"I know," Barriss said. "I know."

She let go. At the door she drew her hood back up, and the guard stood aside to let her pass, and she went out without looking back, very straight, very quickly, like someone walking against a strong wind.

Ahsoka lay on the cot by the window and watched the blue dusk of the Yard. An hour later she saw the courier lift off the landing pad beyond the dome: a small, sleek ship, white and red, nothing like the grey Judiciary hulls that came and went with the prisoners. It climbed through the twilight and was gone.

Her leg was whole under the dressing. Her shoulder moved freely for the first time in weeks. Her head was quiet.

The guard came eventually and unbuckled the strap from her wrist and said she could stay the night, healer's orders, and turned the lights down to half. Ahsoka lay in the dim warmth and thought about Barriss's hands shaking on the satchel clasp. She thought about the cold, and the long journey, and how hard it must have been to walk into this place in the robes of the Order that had sent her here, knowing what everyone in the Temple must be saying about her—*that she would go*—and doing it anyway.

That was what kindness looked like, she decided, when it cost something.

She fell asleep before she could finish the thought, and slept without dreaming for the first time since the hangar. In the morning, the medical officer found the note Barriss had left on the counter, read it, frowned, and carried it straight up to Ring One—and an hour after that, for the first time since the transport, Commandant Vorne came down to Ring Two and stood at the foot of Ahsoka's cot with the collar control in her hand.

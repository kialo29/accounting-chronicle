# Chapter 18: Pirates

The first speeder bike came over the canyon rim trailing black smoke, and Ahsoka knew three things before its skids touched the rock: there were more behind it, the rider had already seen them, and she had not felt a single one of them coming.

Four months ago she would have known about the bike before she heard it. She would have known the rider's mood, whether his finger was on the trigger, whether he was the kind who shot first. Now she had only her ears and her eyes and the stiff grey collar under her jaw, and the whisper beneath it told her nothing at all.

"Down," she said, and pulled Tess behind a spur of red stone.

They had been walking since first light, following the canyon floor away from the crash because it was the only direction that wasn't straight up. The air stank of sulfur. Every so often a geyser somewhere ahead coughed and hissed, and the ground shivered under their boots. Tess had been limping for the last hour and pretending she wasn't. Her breath came in short, careful gasps, though the air was warm; four months of ice had taught both of them to breathe like it was still cold.

A second bike crested the rim. A third. Then the low, grinding hum of something heavier, and a battered cargo skiff slid into view above the canyon, its hull patched in six different colors of plate, a pair of rotary cannons swinging lazily on its bow.

"Friends of yours?" Tess said.

"Maybe," Ahsoka said. "Depends what kind of day he's having."

The bikes dropped into the canyon and circled them, close enough that grit stung her face. The riders were Weequay, leathery and grinning, in mismatched armor and goggles, rifles slung forward. One of them shouted something in a dialect she half knew. She stood up slowly with her hands open and stepped out from the rock so they could see her, and so Tess could stay behind it.

The skiff came down last. A figure stood at the rail with one boot up on the gunwale like a man posing for a holo he'd commissioned himself: long coat, goggles pushed up on a patterned head-wrap, a face like weathered stone split by an enormous, disbelieving smile.

Hondo Ohnaka spread his arms wide.

"No," he said. "No, no. I refuse. This is a trick of the fumes. I have been breathing the geysers for too long and now I am seeing ghosts. Little Jedi?" He leaned so far over the rail that one of his crew grabbed the back of his coat. "Is that you, or is it a very sad impostor?"

"It's me, Hondo."

"It *is* you!" He vaulted the rail, landed badly, made a show of it being on purpose, and strode toward her with his hands still spread. He stopped an arm's length away and looked her over the way a dealer looks over a speeder he suspects has been in a crash. "Look at you. Grey. Everything grey. Who dressed you? And so thin. They don't feed people where you have been, I think." His gaze dropped to her throat, and the smile changed shape without leaving his face. "Ah. And that is not jewelry."

"No," she said.

"No." He clicked his tongue. "You know, it is a very strange thing. Only this morning I am sitting in my chair, drinking something I stole, and the Holonet tells me that the famous Temple bomber has been rescued by her friends the Separatists and is now sitting at Count Dooku's right hand, eating his little cakes. And I think, well, good for her, she always had spirit. And then my scouts tell me a pod has come down in the Red Narrows, and I come to see what I can take off it, and—" He gestured at her with both hands, as though presenting her to an audience. "Here is the Temple bomber, in my canyon, eating no cakes at all."

"I didn't bomb the Temple."

"I did not ask." He held up a finger. "But I notice that if you were with Dooku, you would not be here. So someone has lost you. And when someone loses a thing, someone else is looking for it, and when someone is looking for a thing, the thing has a price." He began counting on his fingers, brows lifting with each one. "The Republic wants you back. They have not even posted a number yet, which frankly I find insulting on your behalf. Count Dooku, apparently, wanted you in the first place. And whoever put you in that pod and then failed to keep you in it is now very embarrassed and very angry, and embarrassed people pay the best of all." He looked at his three raised fingers, then raised a fourth with great ceremony. "Then there is the fourth party."

"Who's the fourth party?"

"Me!" He beamed. "I am the one who has you."

"You're thinking about selling me."

"Of course I am thinking about it. I am a pirate. I think about selling everything. My boots. This canyon. My crew, frequently." Behind him, one of the riders made a rude gesture, and Hondo waved it off without looking. "Thinking is free. Selling is a matter of timing, and I never sell before I know what a thing is worth. Before that, it is just a guest."

Tess stepped out from behind the rock. Two rifles swung toward her. She looked at them the way she looked at guards who got too close in the mess line, which was to say like they were a weather condition.

Hondo's eyebrows went up. "And who is this? Your grandmother?"

"Her neighbor," Tess said. "Next cell over. Four months. And before you start counting your fingers again, I'm worth nothing to anybody, so save yourself the arithmetic."

"Everyone is worth something," Hondo said generously. "Some people are only worth very little."

"Bane put us in that pod," Ahsoka said.

Hondo went still.

It lasted less than a heartbeat. Then the smile came back, wider and with more teeth in it. "Cad Bane."

"He's the one who lost me. He said he'd be back for me in a day or two."

"In *my* canyon. Without asking." Hondo laid a hand on his chest as if wounded. "This changes things. Now it is personal, and personal is always more expensive." He turned and snapped his fingers at the skiff. "Water! Blankets! Something to eat that is not still moving! Our guests have come a long way." Then, quieter, to her, almost an afterthought: "You are shaking, little Jedi."

She looked down. She was. She hadn't noticed.

He took off his long coat and put it around her shoulders. It smelled of engine grease and sweet smoke and something fermented. She did not say anything, because if she said anything she might do something embarrassing, and he did not say anything either, and she was more grateful for the silence than for the coat.

"This is a loan," he said, walking her toward the skiff. "I will want it back."

* * *

Hondo's base had been rebuilt since the last time she'd seen it—or rather, it had been patched, which was not the same thing. New plating sat over old scorch marks. A gun emplacement Grievous had flattened had been replaced by two smaller ones, both painted a cheerful orange for reasons nobody explained. Pirates lounged on crates and argued over sabacc in the shade of the landing pad. Everything smelled of fuel and sulfur and cooking oil.

They fed her first. She ate a bowl of something spiced and brown with her hands trembling, then made herself slow down when her stomach cramped. Tess ate beside her with the steady, unhurried efficiency of someone who had eaten every meal for nine years as if it might be taken away.

Then Hondo brought them down a ramp into a workshop that ran the length of a hangar bay, crowded with half-stripped swoops, droid torsos hung from hooks like smoked meat, and a long bench under a ring of work lamps.

The mechanic's name was Juko. He was a Weequay older than Hondo, his skin creased deep as dry riverbed, with a jeweler's loupe strapped over his right eye on a band of cracked leather and fingers stained black to the second knuckle. He wore a heavy welding apron scorched through in places and chewed slowly on something that he never swallowed. He did not say hello. He looked at the collar, then at her, then back at the collar, and held out his hand for her to sit.

She lay back on the bench with a rolled tarp under her neck. The lamps were hot on her face.

Juko ran a hand-scanner around the collar twice, peering through the loupe at the readout. His chewing stopped.

"Judiciary," he said. "New. Ugly work. Good work, but ugly."

"Can you get it off?" Ahsoka asked.

"Off, yes." He tapped the band near the seam, just beneath the root of her left lekku, and she felt the vibration in her teeth. "There is a charge cell inside. Here. Runs both ways around. If someone cuts the band without the proper key, the cell empties into you."

"Into me," she repeated.

"Into the neck." He tapped his own, thoughtfully. "Maybe you sleep for a day. Maybe your heart forgets what it is for. Depends on the charge. This one—" he squinted at the scanner, "—this one is full."

Tess, standing at the foot of the bench, said, "Hells."

"The key's in Bane's coat," Ahsoka said. "The warden's terminal was keyed to it too, and he shot that to pieces."

"Then no key." Juko shrugged. "So we drain the cell first. Small hole, here, to reach the line. A clip to ground it. Then I cut both sides, quickly, while it is cold, before it notices." He said it like the collar was an animal.

"And if the clip slips?" Tess said.

Juko considered the question with real care. "Then she will have a very bad afternoon."

From the doorway, Hondo said, "He has done this before. Many times. Almost never on necks, but the principle is the same."

"That's not helping, Hondo."

"I am not trying to help, I am trying to reassure. They are different arts." He came in and dragged a stool over, sat on it backward, and folded his arms on the backrest so his chin rested on them. "If it helps, I will not let him kill you. You are far too valuable dead—no. Alive. I meant alive. You are far too valuable alive."

She almost laughed. It came out as a breath.

Tess pulled up a crate and sat by her head. After a moment Ahsoka felt Tess's left hand find hers—the one with the two frostbitten fingers, stiff and dark to the second joint—and grip it hard.

"Don't break my good one," Tess said. "I only have the one."

Juko packed cold gel around the band, so cold it bit, and set a thin bar of chilled metal against her throat to keep the heat off her skin. Then he lifted a drill no thicker than a stylus.

"Do not move," he said. "Do not swallow if you can help it."

The drill whined. She felt it as pressure first, then a high thin vibration in the bones behind her eyes. She fixed her gaze on the ring of lamps and counted them. Eight. One flickered. She counted them again.

The whine changed pitch.

The status light under her jaw, the small hard blue bump she had felt every day since the transport, flickered. Then it turned amber.

A tone sounded from somewhere inside the collar—two short pulses, thin and polite, like a door chime.

"Juko," Hondo said, not theatrical at all.

"I know." The mechanic's hands did not speed up. A tool clicked. "I know. Hold."

Something bit down at the base of her skull.

It was not pain at first. It was white—a white sheet thrown over the world, every lamp blooming at once, her whole spine snapping straight like a cable pulled taut. She heard Tess swear from very far away. Her hand clenched so hard on Tess's fingers that something in them popped. Her heels drummed once against the bench and she could not make them stop.

"*Hold her*," Juko said, and hands—Hondo's, she thought, rings and all—pressed her shoulders flat.

Then a clip snapped home, and the white drained out of the world as fast as it had come.

She was lying on the bench breathing in short tearing gasps, tasting metal. The amber light was dark. Somewhere a cooling fan ticked.

"Grounded," Juko said. He did not sound relieved. He sounded like a man noting the weather. "The cell is empty now. Mostly. You are fine?"

"I don't know," she said. Her voice came out cracked. "Ask me later."

"Later is good. Later means alive." He lifted a cutter with a blade like a silver fingernail. "Now we cut. This part is only loud."

It was loud. The cutter screamed against the band on the left side and she felt heat bloom through the gel, sharp and sudden, and something stung beneath her jaw like a spark had landed there. She hissed. Juko muttered an apology to the collar, not to her. He switched sides. The cutter screamed again. She stared at the lamps. Tess's fingers were still in hers, and Tess had not let go even after the pop.

Then Juko set down the cutter, slid two flat bars into the cuts, and pried.

The collar opened like a jaw.

For half a breath, nothing happened. She lay there with cold air on the raw skin of her neck, waiting, afraid to want anything.

And then the Force came back.

Not a whisper. Not an echo. It came the way morning comes over a ridgeline, all at once and everywhere, sunlight through a door someone had kicked open. It poured into her through every crack she hadn't known she had. She felt the stone of the base around her, ancient and warm and patient. She felt the geysers beneath the canyon, slow pulses of heat like the breath of something sleeping. She felt Juko beside her, careful and dry as old leather, already thinking about the next job. She felt Hondo, a big bright untidy blaze of greed and curiosity and an affection he would have denied at blasterpoint. She felt every pirate in the yard above, every swearing card player, every bored lookout, the small quick heartbeat of some creature nesting in the hangar beams.

She felt herself. She had forgotten how that felt—to be a shape in the light instead of a hole in the dark.

She did not decide to cry. It simply happened, the way breathing had happened in the Well. Her shoulders shook and the tears ran sideways off her face into the tarp, and she put her free hand over her eyes and could not stop, and for once she didn't try.

Nobody said anything for a while.

Then Hondo cleared his throat loudly and stood up. "The fumes in here are terrible," he announced. "Juko, you must do something about the ventilation. My eyes are very irritated." He wiped them with the heel of his hand and glared at a pirate in the doorway until the pirate found something else to look at.

When she could sit up, Juko smeared burn gel across her neck. Where the collar had been, the skin was red and rubbed raw all the way around, a ring the width of two fingers. Under the left side of her jaw was a small round burn the size of a credit chip where the cutter's spark had landed. He pressed a patch over it with his thumb.

"Scar, maybe," he said. "Small one."

"I don't mind."

She reached for Tess with the Force, without meaning to, the way you'd turn your head toward a familiar voice. What she found surprised her. Tess was knotted up tight, a fist of a person, and underneath the relief there was something heavy turning over and over, like a coin being worried between fingers in a pocket. Fear, Ahsoka thought. Of course it was fear. Bane was coming, and Tess had nothing to fight him with but a cracked sounder and a sharp tongue.

"I hurt your hand," Ahsoka said.

Tess flexed it. One of the frostbitten fingers was already swelling. "Not the first time somebody's broken something of mine." She managed a crooked smile. "Worth it. You look like somebody turned the lights on in you, kid."

Hondo had picked the collar up off the bench with two fingers, the way someone might lift a dead thing by the tail. He turned it over in the lamplight, studying the seam, the dark status light, the neat black hole Juko had drilled.

"This," he said, "I keep."

Ahsoka looked at it in his hand. Four months of her life, broken open in a pirate's workshop.

"Why?"

"Because somebody will want to know how it works. Somebody always does." He tucked it into his belt. "The Republic, to get it back. The Separatists, to build their own. A third person I have not thought of yet, who will pay the most of all."

"Don't ever put that on anyone."

He looked genuinely offended. "On whom? I have no Jedi." Then he looked at her, the raw ring at her throat, and something in his face went briefly quiet. "Except, it seems, one. And she is a guest."

* * *

The trophy room was at the top of the base, behind a door with three locks, two of which Hondo opened with keys and one of which he opened by kicking it in a particular place.

It was not a large room. It was crammed with things: a Mandalorian helmet with a blaster hole through the visor, a crate of crystal statuettes from some looted palace, a painting of a Hutt that looked like it had been used for target practice, and, along one wall in a glass case lined with dark red cloth, lightsaber hilts.

She stopped in front of the case. There were seven of them. Each one was laid on its own little cushion like a jewel in a shop window.

"You've been busy," she said.

"I am a collector," Hondo said with dignity. "It is a hobby, and like all hobbies, it is expensive and occasionally gets people killed." He unlocked the case and stood back, hands clasped behind him. "Choose one. No—I will choose. You would pick the most valuable, because you have good taste, and then I would be sad."

He reached past two ornate hilts and lifted out a third.

It was a plain thing: a brushed steel body, a ridged black grip worn shiny where a hand had held it for years, an emitter shroud with a small dent on one side. Near the pommel was a rough patch of metal where something—a mark, a name, a symbol—had been filed away.

He held it out to her across both palms.

"This," he said, his voice dropping into the rich, dramatic register of a man who had told a story many times, "I took from a Jedi Master. A terrible one. Huge. Very old, very fierce. We fought for an entire day atop the great geyser at Kesh Falls, and at the end, as the steam rose around us, he knelt and handed me his blade, and he said, 'Hondo Ohnaka, I have never met your equal.' And then he wept."

"He wept."

"Copiously."

She took the hilt.

It was heavier than her own main blade and longer in the grip. She turned it over, feeling the balance, and then, carefully, she reached into it with the Force the way she would have reached into her own crystal on a long night in the field.

The crystal was there. It was quiet, and it was blue, and it had been asleep a long time. There was nothing in it of a duel on a geyser, nothing of rage or surrender. What she felt instead was something almost gentle: long hours of stillness, a patient hand, the faint echo of a single person who had carried it for many years and had put it down, at the end, without any violence at all. Whoever had owned this had not lost it to Hondo. Whoever had owned this had probably never met Hondo.

She looked up at him. He looked back at her, beaming, innocent as a child.

"A terrible Jedi Master," she said.

"The worst."

"Huge."

"Enormous." He held up a hand well above his own head. Then he seemed to consider something and lowered it a little. "Well—big in spirit. Physically, he was actually quite small. Very small. That is what made it so frightening."

"Hondo."

"What?"

"You bought this from a salvage dealer."

He put a hand over his heart. "You wound me. I would never pay for a lightsaber." He paused. "I traded for it. A very fair trade. Some of the people involved are still alive."

She turned away so he wouldn't see her smile, thumbed the activator, and the blade sprang out with a snap-hiss that she felt in her chest.

Blue light filled the little room, washing over the cracked painting and the shot-through helmet and Hondo's delighted face. The hum was a fraction lower than her own sabers, and the color was wrong—every instinct she had expected green—but it was alive, and the crystal stirred a little under her attention, like something rolling over in its sleep.

She shifted her grip, flipping the blade into a reverse hold, and took two slow steps through a form she'd practiced a thousand times. The weight pulled at her wrist. A single blade felt lopsided after years of two. She would have to learn it again.

That was all right. She had learned harder things lately.

"Blue," Hondo said, admiring. "It suits you. Very dignified. Very respectable. Nobody will ever believe a girl carrying a blue saber blew up a temple."

She deactivated it. The room went dim and orange again.

"Why are you doing this?"

"Doing what?"

"You could sell me to anyone. You said so yourself. Bane's coming. You could just hand me over when he lands and spare yourself the trouble."

Hondo pursed his lips and looked at the ceiling for a while, as if the answer might be written on it.

"Bane," he said at last, "would not pay me. Bane would shoot me, take you, and steal my good chair on the way out. This I know from experience. The Republic would pay, but slowly, with forms. Count Dooku—" he wobbled a hand, "—Count Dooku and I have a history, and it was not a friendly history, and he still owes me for a ransom that went very badly for everyone." He shrugged expansively. "And you. You are a guest. You brought me a mystery and a reason to annoy Cad Bane, and that is worth something too."

"That's not a real answer."

"It is the answer I am giving." He waved at the saber in her hand. "That is a loan. I will want it back, with interest. The interest is that you do not die, because the dead are terrible at returning things."

She looked at him a moment longer, and through the Force she felt it again: under all that noise and gold-plated nonsense, a stubborn, unreasonable warmth that he would have sold his own mother rather than admit to.

"Thank you, Hondo."

"Yes, yes." He was already ushering her toward the door. "Do not thank me. Thanking is how people start expecting things."

On the stair down, they passed Tess coming up.

She'd changed out of her prison greys into a stained pirate's jacket two sizes too big, and she had a cup of something steaming in her good hand. Her swollen finger had been splinted to its neighbor with a strip of tape.

"Ohnaka," she said. "That big dish on the north tower. That reach off-world?"

"It reaches everywhere," Hondo said. "For a price."

"What's the price?"

He looked at her with fresh interest. "Who are you calling, grandmother?"

"Nobody yet." Tess sipped her drink. "Just like to know where the exits are. Old habit. Prison does that." Her free hand was in her jacket pocket. Ahsoka noticed it there and thought nothing of it; Tess had always kept one hand hidden, in the cut, in the mess, in the cell. It was where she kept whatever she was trading that week.

"There are no exits on Florrum," Hondo said cheerfully. "Only very long walks."

* * *

By evening the sky had gone the color of old brass, and the geysers out in the canyons threw up plumes that caught the last of the light and turned it orange.

Ahsoka sat cross-legged on the edge of the landing pad with the borrowed saber across her knees and her eyes closed.

She was not meditating, exactly. She was just listening. She had spent all those months in the ice learning to sit with nothing, to find her breath when there was no Force to lean on, and now that it was back she found she did not want to rush into it. She let it come to her a little at a time. The warm stone. The pirates arguing over cards behind her. The engines cooling. A lookout up on the north tower humming badly to himself.

The raw ring around her neck stung in the evening wind. She touched the patch under her jaw and let the sting be what it was.

Footsteps behind her, uneven. Tess lowered herself onto a crate nearby with a grunt.

"Hondo's people say the last ship through here was three days ago," Tess said. "Smugglers. Gone again."

"Bane said a day or two."

"Bane says a lot of things. He likes to hear himself drawl." Tess pulled her jacket tighter, though the evening was warm. "What'll you do, kid? Now you've got your little torch back."

Ahsoka opened her eyes and looked out at the canyon.

"Find out who did it," she said. "Who killed Letta. Who killed those three troopers with my sabers. Who was in that warehouse." She turned the hilt over in her hands. "The Republic convicted me because they needed someone. Tarkin as good as told me. If I can find whoever it really was, then maybe they don't get to need me anymore."

"And then what? You go back to your Temple?"

The question landed somewhere she hadn't expected. She thought of the Council chamber. Windu's hand at her montral, lifting away her beads. Plo Koon looking at the floor.

"I don't know," she said.

Tess was quiet for a while. When she spoke again her voice was rougher. "You know what I've learned in nine years of being guilty? Nobody gets cleared. Not in this galaxy. You get bought out, or you get forgotten, or you get lucky. That's the list. There's no fourth thing."

"There was a fourth thing in the ice," Ahsoka said. "Four minutes. I could have walked out."

"I know. I was there." Tess looked at her strangely. "You didn't."

"No."

"That's the difference between you and me, kid." Tess's hand moved in her pocket. "I'd have walked."

Ahsoka reached for her through the Force, gently, not prying. She found that same tight knot, the same coin turning over and over. It felt sadder than it had in the workshop. She took it for fear, and for the particular weariness of an old woman who had been locked up for nine years and was now loose in a galaxy that had moved on without her.

"You did walk," Ahsoka said. "You walked out of Bane's hold. You opened my cuffs. I wouldn't be here without you."

Tess didn't answer. She drank from her cup and looked away, out at the geysers, and her jaw worked as if she were chewing on something that wouldn't go down.

Then the Force *changed*.

It was not loud. It was a single cold note threaded through the warm evening, like a blade drawn slowly from a sheath somewhere just out of sight. Ahsoka's head came up. The hair along her lekku prickled. She knew that feeling. She had felt it in a lower-level alley with the rain coming down, beside her in the dark of a stairwell, across the width of a burning warehouse. Sharp, bitter, and wound tight around something hurt.

She was on her feet before she knew she'd moved, the borrowed hilt in her hand.

"Kid?" Tess said.

Up on the north tower, the humming stopped. The lookout shouted something. Then another voice in the yard, and another, and the clatter of rifles being snatched up off sabacc tables.

Down at the end of the landing pad, where the canyon road came up through the outer gate, a single figure was walking out of the dusk.

Cloaked. Hooded. Unhurried. She didn't run and she didn't raise her hands. She walked up the middle of the road as though Hondo's base was a public street and the dozen blasters swinging toward her were somebody else's problem. A pirate stepped into her path and she simply looked at him, and he stepped back out of it.

Hondo appeared on the balcony above the hangar in a borrowed coat that didn't fit him, a pistol in each hand. "Whoever you are," he called down, "you are trespassing, and I charge for that!"

The figure stopped where the floodlights began. Pale hands rose and pushed the hood back.

Bald head, ghost-white skin. Dark tattoo lines down the chin. The purple bruise Ahsoka remembered from the night of the warehouse was long gone, but the eyes were exactly the same: cold, amused, and very tired.

At her belt hung two empty clips where a pair of curved hilts used to be.

Asajj Ventress swept her gaze across the circle of pirates, past Hondo on his balcony, past Tess on her crate, and let it come to rest on Ahsoka standing at the edge of the pad with a stranger's blue saber in her fist.

"I heard," Ventress said, in a voice like silk dragged over a knife, "that there was a Jedi for sale on Florrum." Her lip curled. "I see the merchandise has been damaged."

Ahsoka's thumb found the activator.

"I'm not for sale," she said.

"Everyone's for sale, little girl." Ventress smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes. "The only question is who's buying, and what they've already paid."

# Chapter 16: Bane

The heat strips died at the start of third watch, and the cold came up through the floor of cell 4-17 like water filling a hold.

Ahsoka was already awake. She had not really slept in two nights, not since Tess had tilted that cracked green readout toward her in the tool shed and shown her three marks that did not move. Every shift since, Tess had checked again and given her the smallest shake of the head. *Still there.* And every night the whisper of the Force, thin as it was under the collar, had stayed flat and listening, as if it were holding its breath along with her.

Now the strips along the ceiling faded from orange to grey to nothing. The blue twilight of the corridor flickered once and went out. For three heartbeats the Deep was completely dark and completely silent, which in four months she had never known it to be.

Then the emergency lamps came on, a dull amber, and somewhere far below her the ice shook.

It wasn't a collapse. She knew the sound of a collapse now; it came from everywhere at once and had a long tail. This was short and flat and deliberate, and a second one followed it exactly as long after the first as it took to walk a corridor and set another charge.

"Told you," Tess said through the left wall. Her voice was very calm. "Didn't I tell you."

"You told me." Ahsoka was on her feet, coat on, boots sealed. She didn't remember doing any of it. "Where?"

"Ring One, from the feel of it. Maybe the Yard doors." A pause. "Kid. Whatever they're here for, it isn't the tibanna."

The alarm started, a rising two-note howl that bounced up the central shaft and set every prisoner on Ring Four shouting at once. Under it Ahsoka heard boots on the gantry, running, and a voice she knew giving orders in short barks. Sergeant Holt came around the curve of the ring with two guards behind him and a blaster rifle held across his chest, and he stopped in front of her cell.

He was breathing hard. Frost had already formed in his moustache.

"Twenty-two fourteen. Hands through the slot."

She put her hands through. The binders were cold enough to sting. "What's happening?"

"Commandant wants you in Ring One. Secure room." He keyed the door, and it rolled aside, and he took her by the upper arm the way he always did, not hard, just firm. "Somebody's cut through the outer hull of the Yard tunnel. Lost contact with the hangar crew. That's all I know and more than you need."

"Sergeant, it's three ships. They came in on the far side, low, two days ago—"

Holt stared at her. Then at the wall of Tess's cell. Then back.

"Two days," he said.

"I didn't have anyone to tell."

He let out a breath through his teeth and didn't argue. "Move."

They took the gantry at a jog. The lift was dead, so Holt turned them toward the service stair that ran up the inside of the shaft, and they were halfway to the landing when every cell door on Ring Four opened at once.

It was a single sound, forty locks releasing together, a clack like a giant cracking its knuckles. For one moment no one on the ring moved. Then the prisoners came out into the amber light, slowly at first and then not slowly at all.

"They've sliced the block," one of the guards said. He was young, younger than Holt by twenty years, and his voice cracked on it. "Sarge, they've got the whole block—"

"I can see that, Dannick." Holt put his back to the stair rail and his rifle up, not aimed at anyone yet. "Everybody stay where you are! Back in your cells and nobody gets hurt!"

Nobody went back. Nobody came at them, either. Most of the Deep's prisoners were too cold and too old and too tired to riot; they milled on the gantry, shouting questions down the shaft, and a few of the quicker ones started for the stairs below. Ahsoka saw Tess step out of 4-18 with her hands in her coat pockets, take one look at the stair, and walk the other way along the ring—toward the maintenance hatch, toward the tool shed, toward the scanner hidden behind its loose panel.

And she saw Krasso, three cells down, duck his notched brow under the doorframe and come out limping on his bad left leg, and look straight at her.

"Up," Holt said, and pushed her up the stair.

They reached the Ring Three landing. Smoke was rolling up the shaft from below now, thin and bitter, smelling of cut durasteel. The second guard went first through the hatch to Ring Two, and Ahsoka heard him say "Clear—" and then heard the sound of a single blaster shot, very close, and the word didn't finish.

The guard came back through the hatch backward, sat down against the landing wall as though his legs had simply decided they were done, and didn't move again.

Holt swore and shoved Ahsoka behind him so hard she hit the rail.

A figure stepped through the hatch into the amber light. Wide-brimmed hat. Long coat. A face the color of old jade, ridged and hairless, with red eyes that caught the lamp and gave nothing back, and two breathing tubes that ran from his cheeks down under his collar and made each breath a soft mechanical hiss.

He held a pistol in each hand. Neither of them was pointed at Ahsoka.

"Evening, Sergeant," Cad Bane said.

Holt fired. He was fast for a man his age, faster than Ahsoka would have guessed, and he didn't waste anything on words. Bane had already moved half a step to the side, the way a person moves to let someone pass in a doorway. The bolt scorched the hatch frame. Bane's left-hand pistol answered it once.

Holt went down on one knee. Then onto his side, on the grating, with his rifle under him and his eyes still open and surprised.

"No—" Ahsoka was on her knees beside him before she knew she'd moved, her bound hands on his chest. Under the thermal coat there was no rise and no fall. The frost in his moustache wasn't melting anymore.

Dannick, the young one, threw down his rifle and put his hands up and started to cry.

Bane looked at him, then at the rifle on the grating, then back at Dannick, and seemed to make a decision about how much trouble he was worth. "Smart," he said. "You stay smart. Sit down against that wall and count to a thousand." He turned the red eyes on Ahsoka. "And you're the reason I came all this way. You're smaller than the holos."

She hit him.

She hadn't planned to. There was no plan, there was only Holt's open eyes and the binders and a stair rail, and her body did what three years of Anakin's training and four months of ice had made it into. She came off the grating low, used the rail to swing her weight, and drove both bound fists up under his chin.

It should have put him down. It moved him back a step. Bane's head snapped up, the hat stayed on, and he made a sound through the tubes that might have been a grunt or a laugh.

She went for his right-hand pistol. She got her fingers on the barrel and twisted, and for a moment it was almost hers—and then something hit her in the ribs with no more fuss than a door closing, and she was against the wall with Bane's forearm across her collarbones and the barrel of the other pistol resting gently against her collar, just below the blue light.

"Now that," he said, "I didn't see coming. Somebody told me you'd be docile without your Jedi tricks." He leaned in. His breath through the tubes smelled faintly of machine oil. "Somebody was wrong. I'm going to remember that."

"You killed him." Her voice came out raw. "He was doing his job."

"So am I." He tipped his head toward the hatch. "Contract says I bring you back alive, in one piece, with that pretty necklace still switched on. It doesn't say anything about anybody else. You keep that in mind before you get creative again, because the next one who steps in front of you is going to be a prisoner, not a guard, and I'll still be on schedule."

From the hatch behind him came a wet, clicking voice. An Aqualish in patched grey armor ducked through, a heavy rifle in his hands and a coil of cable over one shoulder. "Ring Two's clear. The Separatist's in the Well, they say. Krinn's gone down after him with two of the boys."

"Then let's not keep the gentleman waiting." Bane stepped back, and the pistol came off her collar, and he gestured at the stair with it like a host showing a guest to the table. "After you, Commander. Oh—sorry. That's not right anymore, is it?"

She went up. There wasn't any choice. The Aqualish fell in behind her with his rifle, and Bane behind him, and on the landing below them Dannick sat against the wall beside Holt and the other dead guard, counting under his breath.

They were three steps from the Ring Two hatch when Krasso came up the shaft.

He didn't come up the stair. He came up the outside of it, hand over hand on the support struts, bad leg dragging, and he swung over the rail directly onto the Aqualish's back and bore him down onto the steps with a sound like two crates colliding. The rifle went off into the ceiling. Ice and dust rained down.

"*Go*," Krasso snarled at her, and that was the whole of it.

She went. Not up. Down—over the rail, the way Krasso had come, dropping to the Ring Three gantry and landing on her bound hands and rolling. The Ring Three prisoners scattered from her. She ran along the curve of the ring toward the far stair with her lungs burning in the cold and her ribs shrieking where Bane had hit them, and she heard two shots behind her, and a roar from Krasso that was all fury and still alive, and then a third sound she didn't know.

A hiss. A roar. Light.

Bane came over her head in a shallow arc on two jets of blue flame, coat flaring, and landed on the gantry ten meters in front of her as lightly as if he'd stepped off a curb.

"I'll admit it," he said, "I've had worse evenings."

She skidded and turned and he fired, not at her, past her, and the bolt took the gantry rail beside her hand and turned it white-hot. She flinched away from it. He was already there.

The stun bolt didn't hurt. That was the strangest thing. It was only a weight that settled over her whole body at once, and the amber lamps tipping sideways, and the grating coming up very slowly to meet her face.

* * *

She surfaced twice before she properly woke.

The first time she was being carried over someone's shoulder through the Ring One corridor, and the walls were scorched black in long streaks, and there was a guard lying across the threshold of the records office who did not look as if he'd ever get up again. Commandant Vorne stood at the end of the corridor in front of her own office door. Her uniform was still impeccable. Her sidearm was in her right hand and the collar control, the small black cylinder Ahsoka had watched hang from her belt for four months, was in her left.

"You will put the prisoner down," Vorne said. There was no tremor in it at all. "She is the property of the Republic Judiciary, and I am the—"

Bane shot her through the right shoulder. Vorne turned halfway around with the force of it and went down against the door, and the sidearm skittered away across the floor.

Bane crouched and took the cylinder out of her hand. She didn't let go of it easily; he had to bend her fingers back one at a time.

"It's keyed to the office terminal as well," Vorne said through her teeth. Her face was grey. "It won't do you any good to take it. I'll shut her down from my desk the moment you're gone."

"Appreciate the warning, Commandant." Bane stood, stepped past her, and put four shots through her open office door into something that sparked and then burned. "There. Now there's only the one." He tucked the cylinder into an inner pocket of his coat and patted it. "I'd get that shoulder looked at. Cold place like this, you don't want it setting wrong."

Vorne looked at Ahsoka. Not at Bane. Her iron-grey hair had come loose on one side, and there was something in her face that Ahsoka had never seen there in a hundred and eighteen days, and it took her a long, swimming moment to recognize it as fear—not for herself.

"Twenty-two fourteen," Vorne said. Then, quieter, as if it cost her something: "Tano."

Then the dark came back.

The second time she surfaced, it was to cold so total it felt like being struck. She was outside. She had not been outside the dome since the day she arrived. The sky over the Yard was black and enormous and full of stars, and the wind off the ice field cut through her thermals like they were paper. Three ships squatted on the landing pad with their ramps down and their running lights dark: two blunt grey haulers with their identification plates scraped off, and a third, longer and meaner, all angles and dark hull.

Men were carrying crates up the haulers' ramps. Loot, she thought dimly. Medical stores. Tibanna canisters. Anything that would make it look like pirates and not a contract.

And between two of Bane's crew, being walked up the ramp of the nearest hauler with his hands bound in front of him and a hood held in one man's fist, ready to go over his head, was a thin man in a high-collared tunic stripped of its insignia.

Merrick Tal turned his head. He had not seen open sky in fifteen months; she watched him look up at it, just for a moment, before he looked at her.

"It seems we are both useful after all," he called across the pad. His voice was steady. "Remember what I told you, Tano. Someone always pays."

"Merrick—"

The hood went over his head. The crew pushed him up the ramp, and the hauler swallowed him, and its ramp began to rise.

She tried to turn toward it. Her body wouldn't do it. Whoever was carrying her shifted her weight with a grunt, and in the jolt her eyes swung across the pad, across the crates stacked at the foot of the dark ship's ramp—a pallet of grey insulated heat-pack cases, Narkis stores, the kind she had watched Tess trade a hundred times—and for a single heartbeat she was certain she saw a figure with cropped white hair crouched in the shadow behind them.

Then the stars went out again.

* * *

When she woke for real, she was sitting up.

She was on a narrow durasteel bench in a holding cage at the back of a ship's hold, and the binders had been replaced with heavy magnetic cuffs locked to a ring set into the deck between her boots. Her ribs ached in a long hot line. Her mouth tasted of metal. The deck under her hummed with the deep, steady vibration of a hyperdrive, and through it, very faintly, she could feel the ship's age: the uneven pulse of a motivator that had been repaired more times than it had been serviced.

The collar was still on. She knew without touching it. The world was still muffled, and the faint whisper she had lived on since Vorne turned it down was still there, and still silent.

"She's awake! She's awake, she's awake." A small skeletal droid on spindly legs clattered up to the bars of the cage and peered at her with two round photoreceptors. "Hello! I am Todo 360, I am in charge of you. Are you going to hit anyone? The boss says you hit him. Nobody hits him. I'm not supposed to open the door."

"I wasn't going to ask you to," Ahsoka said.

"Good. Because I wouldn't. Probably." Todo bobbed. "Would you like water? I am also in charge of water."

"Todo." Bane's voice came from the front of the hold. "Go count something."

The droid went, muttering. Bane walked down the hold toward her without hurry. He'd taken the hat off; it hung on a hook by the hatch, and without it his head looked longer and stranger, the ridges over his eyes deeper. He was turning Vorne's collar control over and over in his long fingers, the way a gambler turns a coin he hasn't decided to bet yet.

"Sleep well?" he said.

"How many?"

He knew exactly what she meant. That was the worst part. He didn't pretend not to. "Didn't count. Five or six. A few more if your lizard friend makes a habit of jumping on people."

"Is he alive?"

"He was when I left him. Vosh wasn't happy about it." He leaned against the bars. "I'll tell you something for free, since I've got time and you've got nowhere to be. Ninety percent of this job is getting people to stop fighting before they get hurt. Your sergeant didn't stop. He made a choice. I respected it."

"You murdered him."

"I *ended* him. Murder's a word for courts." The red eyes considered her. "You'd know more about that than me. You've got a conviction. I just have a reputation."

Behind him a console on the bulkhead chimed. He glanced at it, and something in his posture altered very slightly—not respect, exactly, but the attention a professional gives to the person signing his payment.

"Speaking of which," he said, "you've got a call."

He keyed the console. Blue light spilled across the hold and resolved into a tall figure, standing very straight, with a cape over his shoulders and a neat white beard and hands folded before him. Even flickering and translucent, he filled the hold as if he had built it.

Ahsoka had seen Count Dooku once in person, from a distance, across a battlefield. She had seen his face on a thousand briefings. She had never had him look at her the way he was looking now: with interest, and something that could almost have passed for sympathy.

"Miss Tano," Dooku said. "I am glad to see you unharmed. I gave strict instructions."

"Your instructions killed six people tonight."

"Mr. Bane's methods are his own." Dooku did not glance at Bane. Bane did not appear to mind. "I would not have chosen them. But I did not have the luxury of asking politely, and neither did you. You were not going to leave that place by any other door."

"I was serving a sentence."

"For a crime you did not commit." He let that sit a moment. "Oh, I know you didn't. Half the Senate knows it, if they are honest with themselves late at night, which they rarely are. The Republic needed a face for its grief, and yours was conveniently nearby. The Council needed to show the Senate that the Order would sacrifice one of its own, and you were conveniently small." His voice was beautifully reasonable. It was the voice of a man explaining a long-settled thing to a promising student. "They tried you in a room you once guarded. They took your beads with their own hands. And then they locked you in the ice with a collar on your throat and congratulated themselves on their mercy."

Ahsoka said nothing. Her hands, in the cuffs, had closed into fists, and she made them open again.

"I left the Order myself," Dooku went on. "Not as you did. I walked out of the Temple when I saw what it had become, and I have never regretted it. You were not given that choice. I am giving you one now. Come to Serenno. Not as a prisoner—as a guest, and in time as something more. I can teach you things the Jedi were afraid to teach. I can take that collar off. And I can give you the one thing the Republic never will." He paused, and when he spoke again it was softer. "The chance to make them understand what they did."

For a moment—only a moment—she felt the pull of it. Not the offer. The *anger*. It was real, and he had found it without effort, the way a healer finds a broken bone by pressing exactly where it hurts. Tarkin in the inspection room, saying guilt had never mattered. Windu's hand at her montral. The Council chamber, and Plo Koon looking at the floor.

And then Holt sitting down against the wall. And Merrick's hood going over his head. And four minutes and nine seconds in Cut Five, with the whole weight of the ice in her hands and the door to the stars right there, and choosing to set it down.

"No," she said.

Dooku's eyebrows rose a fraction. "You haven't heard the terms."

"I don't need to. You're right about some of it." It came out steadier than she expected. "They got it wrong. They got *me* wrong. But you're offering me the same thing they did—you've decided what I am, and you want me to agree. I've done four months of being whatever somebody else needed. I'm not starting over with you."

"That is pride talking. It does you credit, and it will not keep you warm."

"Neither did the ice. I lived."

Dooku studied her for a long moment. Then he smiled, very slightly, as though she had confirmed something he'd hoped to find.

"You'll reconsider," he said. "People generally do, once they have been on Serenno a while and the Republic has finished telling the galaxy what you are. Mr. Bane. Proceed as arranged."

The hologram folded into itself and was gone.

Bane switched off the console. In the sudden quiet the hyperdrive hum seemed louder.

"Well," he said, "that's his problem, not mine. I get paid on delivery, not on enthusiasm. You can sulk all the way to Serenno for all it matters to my account." He tucked the collar control back inside his coat, against his chest, and gave the pocket a pat. "But I'll give you some advice, since you turned his down. Don't try anything on my ship. I like my ship."

"And if I do?"

"Contract says alive." He took his hat off the hook and settled it on his head. "Alive covers a lot of ground."

She counted.

Not the way Dannick had been counting. She counted the way Tess had taught her to count blocks on the terrace: steadily, without hope, so that hope couldn't run out. Bane's crew—only two aboard, from the voices: Bane himself, and the Aqualish called Vosh, whose rasping complaints about his back came down the corridor at intervals. The others had gone with the haulers. With Merrick. Todo clattered in and out of the hold, counting crates aloud and losing his place.

She had counted to nineteen hundred when the lid of a heat-pack case at the bottom of the stacked Narkis loot lifted two centimeters and stopped.

Ahsoka went absolutely still.

The lid rose further. A frostbitten hand came out, two of its fingers stiff and dark. Then a cropped white head. Tess Varro unfolded herself out of a crate the size of a footlocker one joint at a time, wincing at every one of them, and sat back on her heels on the deck and looked around the hold with the expression of someone who had just woken up in a cheap hotel.

"Stars," she whispered. "I'm too old to be cargo."

"Tess—" Ahsoka kept her voice to nothing. "How did you—"

"Same way I always get anywhere. I walked in while everybody was looking at something else." She crept to the cage and crouched beside the bars, and up close Ahsoka could see how blue her lips were, how badly her hands were shaking. "They were loading anything that looked like it was worth stealing. I looked worth stealing."

"You could have stayed. Tess, you could have walked out of that block with every door open and nobody—"

"And gone where? Out on the ice in a thermal coat?" Tess's mouth twisted. "There's no road out of the Deep. There's ships, and there's dying. I picked a ship." She was already digging in her coat. Out came the cracked sounder, and then the leak-detector coil, its wires bent into a crude loop. "Hold still. Mag-lock's a mag-lock. Same as the tool shed."

She pressed the coil against the cuff housing and thumbed the sounder. It whined, a thin needle of sound. Nothing happened. She adjusted something with her thumbnail and tried again. Ahsoka watched the corridor hatch and listened to Todo, two compartments forward, explaining something to Vosh at great length.

The cuffs clicked and fell open.

Ahsoka caught them before they hit the deck. Tess let out a long breath.

"Cage door's on the same circuit," Tess said, and did it again, faster.

Then they were out, crouched together behind the stacked crates, and Ahsoka's mind was moving faster than it had in months—not the Force, she didn't have the Force, but everything else. Three years of Anakin. Three years of watching him crawl into the guts of every ship they'd ever flown, swearing at couplings, explaining things to her she'd pretended not to listen to.

"That hyperdrive," she breathed. "Hear it? The motivator's out of true. He's been running it hard and patching it." She pointed down the hold, past the cage, to a low access panel near the deck. "Main power feed to the motivator runs back along the keel. If we pull the regulator coupling, the safeties will drop us out of hyperspace before the drive tears itself apart."

"And then we're sitting in the dark with a bounty hunter."

"And then we find the escape pods. Every ship that size has two." She was already moving. "Watch the hatch."

The access panel was held with four bolts, and she had no tools. Tess silently handed her a flat steel spike from the inside of her boot—a guard would have given her thirty days in the Well for that—and Ahsoka used it as a lever and then as a driver, and the panel came away. Inside was a tangle of conduit, heat shimmering off it. She found the regulator by the warning stencils, the same ones on every hyperdrive she'd ever seen, and got both hands on the coupling and thought of Anakin, for one sharp instant, lying under a fighter in the hangar with grease to the elbows, saying *the trick is not to care what it costs you*.

Beside the panel, a cargo repeater scrolled the nav plot in dull amber so the loaders could see how long the run would be. The lane bent out past the Florrum system. She knew exactly who lived on Florrum, and what he would do for a profit, and what he might do for her. She kept her hands on the coupling and watched the marker crawl along the plot until it sat level with the system, because if the safeties were going to drop them somewhere, it would be next to Hondo.

She pulled.

The coupling came free with a shriek of sparks. The hum of the deck changed pitch, climbed, stuttered. Every light in the hold went red. Somewhere forward an alarm began to whoop, and the whole ship lurched as if something enormous had grabbed it by the tail—and then the long streaked light beyond the hold's single viewport snapped back into stars.

And a planet. Close. Huge, and sickly yellow, its sky hazed with sulfur, its surface cracked with dark canyons.

She knew that yellow. She had fought for her life down there, with a crate of younglings behind her and a pirate who couldn't decide whether to sell her or applaud her.

"Florrum," she said.

"*Intruder! Intruder!*" Todo came clattering through the hatch, arms waving. "The prisoner is out! The prisoner is out and there are *two* of her!"

"Pods," Tess said.

They ran. Through the forward hatch, down a cramped corridor washed in red, Todo screeching behind them. Ahsoka saw the pod hatch ahead, recessed into the port bulkhead, with its yellow-and-black frame—and saw Bane come around the corner at the far end of the corridor at the same moment, hat low, a pistol already rising.

He didn't fire at her. He fired at the pod controls, and missed only because Vosh blundered into him from behind. The bolt scorched the bulkhead a hand's breadth from the release lever.

Ahsoka hit the lever. The hatch cycled open. Tess dove in. Ahsoka turned in the doorway, and for one half-second she and Bane were looking straight at each other down the length of the corridor. She saw the outline of the collar control under his coat, over his heart. Six meters. It might as well have been six systems.

He saw her see it. He touched the brim of his hat.

She threw herself into the pod and slammed the inner seal, and the launch charge went off with a bang that knocked the breath out of her, and the *Justifier* fell away above them.

The pod's comm crackled almost at once.

"That was my ship," Bane's voice said, perfectly calm. "I told you I liked my ship." The breathing tubes hissed. "You know what's down there? Pirates. Bad weather. Things that eat pirates. I'll give you a day or two to discover how much you miss my company. Then I'll come and collect. I always collect."

Tess reached past her and turned the comm off.

Then Florrum's atmosphere caught them, and the pod began to scream.

There was nothing to do but hold on. Ahsoka strapped Tess into the second crash couch with hands that wouldn't stop shaking, and strapped herself in, and the viewport went orange and then white with heat, and the whole pod bucked and spun until she couldn't tell which way was the planet. The retros fired late and not enough. A canyon wall flashed past the viewport, close enough to see the striations in the rock. A plume of steam—a geyser—enveloped them, and the pod struck something and bounced and struck again and skidded, on and on, a long grinding roar of rock against durasteel that went through her teeth.

Then it stopped.

Silence. A tick of cooling metal. The smell of hot plating and, seeping in through some crack in the hull, the sharp rotten-egg reek of sulfur.

"Tess?"

A cough beside her. "Still here. Don't ask me what's broken. I'll let you know when I find out."

Ahsoka popped her harness and fell against the side of the pod, which was now the floor. She got the hatch open by kicking it. Yellow light poured in, thick and hot after four months of blue, and she climbed up into it and stood on the warped hull of the pod at the bottom of a red-walled canyon. A geyser hissed somewhere upstream. The sky was the color of an old bruise healing. The air burned her throat.

It was the first real warmth she had felt since the day they put her on the transport, and she stood in it with her eyes closed and let it.

Then she lifted her hand to her throat, and her fingers found the grey metal and the small hard bump of the status light, still glowing blue, and the Force beneath it still a whisper too faint to use.

Bane had the only control. And Bane was coming.

Below her, Tess was hauling herself out of the hatch. As she came up, her shaking hand closed around something small she'd taken from the pod's emergency locker—or from somewhere—and slipped it into her coat before Ahsoka could see what it was.

"Power cell," Tess said, catching her look. "For the sounder. We'll want to know who's coming." She squinted up at the yellow sky. "Kid. Where in all the hells are we?"

"Somewhere I know," Ahsoka said. "Which isn't the same as somewhere safe."

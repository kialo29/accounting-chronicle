# Chapter 22: The Canyon

For one heartbeat, before she slammed the door on him, Anakin Skywalker was happy.

It hit him over the open airspeeder like the sun clearing a ridge. Ahsoka. Not the faint pressure he'd felt days ago in his bunk on the *Resolute*, thin as a held breath at the far end of the galaxy. Her, whole and loud and close, the quick bright stubborn shape of her that he had felt every waking hour for three years and had not felt once since a technician sealed a grey collar around her neck. And she felt him. He knew it the way he knew his own pulse. The startle. The reach. And then gladness, so plain and so young that it went through his chest like a blade.

Then a wall where she had been.

The speeder dipped. Obi-Wan's hand closed hard on the edge of the dash.

"Anakin."

"I've got it." He hauled the nose up. A rib of red rock went past close enough to scrape paint, and the slipstream threw grit across the windscreen. His mechanical hand had locked on the yoke. He made the fingers open, one at a time, the way he'd had to on the Dovrin platform with twenty stories of hab stack on his back. "I've got it."

"You felt her."

"She felt me." He heard his voice crack on it and didn't care. "She was glad, Obi-Wan. For a second. Before she remembered."

Obi-Wan said nothing to that, which was kinder than anything he could have said.

Below them Florrum's canyons opened and closed like the folds of a burned hand. The sky was the color of old brass, dirty gold, streaked with geyser steam. The air stank of sulfur even at this speed. Anakin banked east, toward the place where she had been, and kept his eyes on the rock because if he looked at Obi-Wan he might say something he couldn't take back.

It had been thirty-one hours since the intercept reached the *Resolute*. Tarkin's office had sent it "as a courtesy," six hours after it went to Major Rusk: a cargo homing tag of the kind Cad Bane liked to hide in shipments he meant to collect, pinging off-world through a pirate comm dish on Florrum, and then, abruptly, dead. Anakin had turned the cruiser around in the Narkis approach lanes before the message finished playing. He'd pushed the hyperdrive past anything the chief engineer liked. They had reached orbit in the dark.

Hondo's dish had been a blackened ruin on its tower. Hondo himself had come out to meet the gunships wearing a dressing gown and an expression of deep personal injury, and had spent forty minutes explaining, in a great many words, that he knew nothing, had seen nothing, and was in fact the victim here. Anakin had left Rex and two platoons to search the base. Rex had looked at him a beat too long when he gave the order, and Anakin had put it down to the same thing everyone was carrying: the lethal-force authorization, signed by Windu, sitting in every trooper's orders like a live grenade.

That was why he'd taken an airspeeder and only Obi-Wan. No one with that order in their pocket was getting anywhere near her.

Up in orbit, Barriss had asked to come down. She'd been waiting in the hangar with her healer's satchel already on her shoulder, very still, very composed. *If she's hurt—*

*If she's hurt,* he'd told her, *I'll bring her to you.* Luminara had laid a hand on her Padawan's arm and kept her aboard, and Barriss had nodded and said nothing, and Anakin had been grateful that somebody besides him still cared.

The comlink on his wrist chirped.

"Sir." Rex. "Judiciary corvette just dropped out of hyperspace. Major Rusk is asking for your coordinates. She's asking in the Admiral's name."

Anakin thought of the case Rusk carried. Sealed, grey, the size of a dinner tray. A spare collar, already calibrated.

"Tell her we're still searching the base."

A pause. "She'll know that isn't true, sir."

"Then tell her slowly."

"Slowly, sir." Another pause, shorter. "Good luck, General."

Obi-Wan was watching him. Anakin dropped the speeder into a side canyon, then another, following the ache where Ahsoka had been, and five minutes later the walls fell back and he saw the outpost.

* * *

It clung to the canyon wall on rusted stilts: a crusher house with a sagging roof, a dead conveyor running down to a cluster of prefab huts, a bunkhouse with its door shut. Someone had painted a large, cheerful *9* on the crusher's flank. A geyser vent at the base of the slope breathed out a lazy yellow plume and sucked it back.

There was a figure on the crusher roof. Not Ahsoka. Too tall, too pale, the hood thrown back from a bald head.

"Well," said Obi-Wan. "There's a face I'd hoped to see only in my nightmares, where at least I'm expecting it."

Ventress raised a rifle.

The first bolt took the windscreen. Anakin threw the speeder sideways and set it down hard in the scree, and Obi-Wan was already out, his blade blue and humming, turning the second and third bolts into the dirt. Anakin went over the side the other way, boots skidding on loose stone, and reached out with everything he had.

She wasn't in the outpost.

She was below it, and moving. Down a slot canyon that cut off south-east from the geyser vent, fast and sure-footed, and she wasn't hiding now. She'd opened herself again, deliberately, a lantern held up in the dark. *Here. This way. Follow me.*

Away from the outpost.

The wind turned, and over the sulfur he caught something else, sharp and clinical, drifting from the bunkhouse's open vent. Bacta. A lot of it, recently. Someone was hurt. His stomach dropped — and then steadied, because the presence in the canyon below was running too fast to be wounded. Ventress, then. He filed it and let it go.

Obi-Wan reached out with his free hand and the rifle tore from Ventress's grip, cartwheeled off the roof, and smashed against the conveyor housing. She let it go without a glance. She dropped from the roof to the top of the conveyor and from the conveyor to the ground, light as ash, and came up with a knife in her hand. Long, plain, single-edged. Not a saber. Not even close.

"Kenobi," she said. "They still let you out without a leash."

"And they've let you out without your lightsabers. I had heard." Obi-Wan's blade dipped politely toward the knife. "I'd offer my condolences, but I don't think either of us would believe them."

"Keep talking. I've been wanting something to cut for a week."

"With that? I've seen better cutlery in a Senate canteen."

"It's sized for small men."

Anakin was already moving toward the slot canyon. Ventress pivoted to block him, and her free hand came up, and a stack of rusted ore carts beside the conveyor slid screaming across the yard into his path. He went over them with a jump that wrenched the Ordaine burn along his ribs. Behind him he heard the snap of Obi-Wan's blade meeting nothing, and Ventress's laugh.

"Anakin!" Obi-Wan called. "Go. Our old friend and I have a great deal to catch up on."

"He'll be dull company," Ventress said. "He always was."

"And yet you keep coming back for more."

Anakin didn't hear her answer. He was over the lip of the slope and sliding down loose scree toward the geyser vent, and then into the narrow red throat of the canyon, and the sound of the yard fell away behind the rock.

* * *

The canyon twisted and narrowed until he could have touched both walls at once, then opened suddenly into a pocket of pale mineral crust, flat as a floor, crazed with white cracks where some old geyser had died. A warm wind breathed up through the cracks. The sky was a single gold seam far overhead.

She was standing in the middle of the crust with her hands empty.

Anakin stopped at the edge of it. Ten paces. He'd closed bigger gaps than that in one jump. He didn't move.

She was thinner. That was the first thing, and it was stupid that it was the first thing, but he couldn't stop seeing it — the hollows under her cheekbones, the way the patched pirate's jacket hung off her shoulders over prison greys. There was a raw ring around her neck where the collar had been, pink and angry against her skin, and a small burn under the left line of her jaw. No beads hung from her montral. He'd known that. Windu had taken them himself. Knowing it was different from seeing the empty place.

A saber hung at her belt. Brushed steel, worn black grip, a dented emitter. Not hers.

"Snips," he said.

For a moment she didn't answer. Her montrals tilted, just slightly, the way they did when she was listening to something underneath what was said.

"You found me," she said.

"You let me."

"I didn't let you. I was careless." Her mouth moved, not quite a smile. "I let you find *this* part."

He looked at the hilt on her belt because he couldn't keep looking at her neck. "Whose is that?"

"Hondo's. He says he won it from a Jedi Master." A breath. "He's told me how four times. It's a different Master every time."

The laugh came out of him before he could stop it, short and painful, and her eyes went bright and she looked away, up at the seam of sky, and blinked hard. He took a step onto the crust.

She took a step back.

It was a small thing. One step on a flat of cracked white stone. It hurt worse than the plasma burn on Ordaine. He stopped where he was.

"Are you hurt?" he said. "I smelled bacta at the outpost."

"It's not mine."

She said it flatly, a door closing, and he let it close.

"Ahsoka." He didn't know what to do with his hands. The left one opened and closed. The right one, the metal one, had found its way to his belt and was resting just above the saber hilt, and he hadn't told it to. He saw her see it. He saw her own hand stay exactly where it was, loose at her side, nowhere near the borrowed steel. Something in him went cold and ashamed, and he moved his hand away and hooked his thumb in his belt instead.

"I had them," he said. "On the platform. The one in the mask. I was this close, and the stack started to go, and I stopped." The words came out in the wrong order, too fast, the way they had in his head for five months. "I could have caught them. If I'd caught them, none of this—"

"Fox told me. Nobody died."

"*You* did. Near enough."

"Not yet." She looked at him then, straight on, and her voice gentled. "If you'd kept running, there'd be a mother and a kid under that stack. I've had a long time to think about it. I wouldn't trade them. You wouldn't either. That's the whole problem with you, Skyguy — you can't help stopping."

It wasn't the name. It was how quietly she said it, like setting down something that might break. He had to look at the rock for a moment.

Then he made himself say what he'd come to say.

"Come back with me." He took a breath. "Listen to me. Just listen, before you say no. The Chancellor asked me to bring you to him. Him, not the Council. He's been talking about an amnesty since before the visit. If you come in to *me*, if you come in on your own, he can pardon you. He's the only one who can. He's the only one who's lifted a finger for you since this started."

She didn't say anything. He pressed on, because if he stopped he'd hear how it sounded.

"Rusk's corvette is in atmosphere right now. She's got a collar in a case, Snips. A new one. Windu signed lethal force. Do you understand what that means? Every trooper on this planet has an order that says if you lift that saber they can put you down. I can't stop that. Obi-Wan can't stop that. But the Chancellor—"

"The Chancellor already made his choice once."

She said it quietly. The warm wind breathed up through the cracks between them.

"He saved your life."

"He sat above me and said *death*." Her voice didn't rise. Somehow that was worse. "I heard him say it. I was still standing there with that word in me when he said the rest. And then he gave me the rest of my life in the ice, and everybody in that room stood up and called it mercy. And you thanked him."

"He didn't have to do anything."

"He could have done *anything*, Anakin. That's what I'm telling you. He could have said there wasn't enough proof. He could have sent it back. He's the Chancellor. He looked at everything there was to look at, and he decided what my life was worth, and he picked a number." She touched the raw ring on her neck, once, as if to check it was still only skin. "I wore his mercy for a hundred and eighteen days. I know exactly what it weighs."

He wanted to tell her she didn't understand, that she hadn't been in that red office, that she hadn't seen the old man's face when Anakin begged. But she had been in the ice. He hadn't.

"A pardon isn't the same as innocent," she said. "A pardon is somebody important deciding to be kind to a guilty person. I'm not guilty. I won't let him be kind to me."

"Then prove it." It came out hoarse. "Let me help you prove it. That's all I've wanted, from the first night. Let me *help*."

"I'm going to prove it."

And there it was. Not in her words. In the Force, where she'd stopped hiding from him: a hard, cold, settled certainty, like a stone at the bottom of clear water. Grief all around it. He knew that feeling. He'd felt it in himself, kneeling in the sand of a Tusken camp. The feeling of knowing exactly who.

"You know," he said. "You know who did it."

She didn't deny it. She didn't need to.

"Tell me."

"No."

"Ahsoka—"

"No."

"*Tell* me." He took two steps before he knew he'd moved. "Give me a name, and I'll end this tonight. Tonight. You'll never have to run again, you'll never see the inside of a cell again, you'll—"

The gravel around his boots was rattling.

He heard it before he understood it. A fine dry chatter, like rain on a hull. Pebbles along the rim of the crust were lifting, shivering, a finger's width off the ground. A crack under his left foot spat a puff of warm steam and widened with a sound like snapping bone. Along the canyon wall, a fist-sized stone pulled loose and hung in the air beside his head.

Ahsoka was looking at the stones. Not at him. At the stones.

He let them fall. They clattered down all at once, and the canyon was very quiet.

"At Narkis," she said, "you crushed a cup and didn't notice. Vorne noticed. I noticed. I had a collar on and I couldn't feel a thing, and I still noticed." She drew a breath that shook a little at the top. "This morning I felt you before the sun came up. All of you. And there's something in you that wasn't there when they took me away. It was small at Narkis. It isn't small now."

"That's not—" He stopped. He didn't know what it wasn't.

"If I give you a name," she said, "you won't take it to the Council. You won't take it to Master Kenobi. You won't take it to a court. You'll take it to *them*. Alone. Tonight, like you said." Her eyes were wet and steady. "And then whatever's left after, you'd have to live in it. For the rest of your life. I've seen what you do when you're carrying something you can't put down. I won't hand you this one."

"You're protecting them." He couldn't believe it. He *could*; that was the worst part. "After what they did to you, you're protecting them."

"I'm protecting you."

The wind moved her lekku. She didn't move at all.

"Both," she said, more quietly. "Both of you. Somebody has to. You were never going to let it be you."

He had nothing. Five months of things he had planned to say, and nothing.

Somewhere up at the rim, far off, he heard the high thin whine of a repulsor engine that wasn't a pirate's. His comlink chirped against his wrist.

"Sir." Rex's voice was very level. "Major Rusk stopped asking. She's tracking your speeder's transponder. Judiciary gunship inbound to your position. I make it twelve minutes. Maybe ten."

Anakin looked at Ahsoka. She'd heard. Her chin lifted a fraction, the way it used to lift on the flight deck before a drop, when she was afraid and had decided not to be.

He thought of the spare collar in its grey case. He thought of the Chancellor's hand on his shoulder in the red office, the warmth of it, and the voice saying *bring her to me*. He'd promised. He'd told himself, standing in the Temple hangar, that this time he wouldn't stop for anything. That this time he'd run the bomber down and not look back.

He had stopped anyway. He'd been standing still for five minutes.

"Rusk will come down the Narrows from the north," he heard himself say. "It's the only cut wide enough for a gunship. Go south-east. Keep the walls high."

"I know."

"Of course you know." He had to swallow. "How are you getting off-planet?"

"Better if you don't know."

"Hondo."

A flicker at the corner of her mouth. "He's calling it a loan. I think I'll be paying it back until I'm a hundred."

"He'll charge you interest on the interest."

"He already has."

She crossed the crust then, the ten paces he hadn't, and before he understood what she was doing she'd put her arms around him and her forehead against his chest, right over the healed burn, hard enough to hurt. He felt her in the Force all at once and all the way through, the way he used to every day without thinking about it — tired, frightened, furious, and so stubbornly, impossibly *her* — and he got his arms around her and held on. His metal hand was against her back. He was careful with it. He was always careful with it, with her.

"Don't be grateful to him on my account," she said into his tunic. "Please. Don't let anyone own you. Not the Council. Not him. Promise me that one."

"Snips—"

"Promise."

He didn't. He couldn't make his mouth do it. She felt that too; he knew she did. She stepped back, and her hand brushed the hilt at her belt — not to draw it, only to settle it — and his own saber stayed dark on his hip, and hers stayed dark on hers.

"I'll prove it," she said. "In front of everybody. And then nobody will ever have to pardon me for anything."

"And then you'll come home?"

She looked at him for a long moment. Her eyes were very clear.

"Go on, Skyguy," she said. "They'll be looking for you."

Then she turned and ran, light and quick over the white crust and into the south-east slot, and the red rock took her. He felt her for twenty heartbeats more. Then the wall went up again, gently this time, and there was only the canyon, the warm breath of the cracks, and the whine of the gunship growing louder overhead.

Twice now, he thought. Twice he had stood still and let the one he was chasing get away. The first time had cost her everything.

He had no idea yet what this one would cost. He stood there until he could make his face do what he needed it to, and then he climbed back toward the outpost.

* * *

Obi-Wan was sitting on an overturned ore cart in the middle of the yard, his cloak torn at the hem and a long smear of red dust down one side of his face. A heap of crushed ore lay beside him where none had been before. The crusher's discharge chute hung open above it, still dribbling gravel.

"She dropped most of a hopper on me," Obi-Wan said, before Anakin could ask. "Then she left, by way of the roof, with a remark about my beard I'd prefer not to have entered into the record."

"Which way?"

"Up. Over. Gone." Obi-Wan dabbed at the dust on his cheek and considered his fingers. "She's slower without her sabers. Not as slow as I'd like."

The bunkhouse door stood open now. Anakin went to it and looked in. Bunks stripped to the frames. A scorched patch on the floor where someone had burned something small and quickly. The bacta smell was still there, fainter, under the sulfur. Nothing else. Whoever had been hurt had been well looked after and was gone.

He stood in the doorway longer than he needed to. Then he came back across the yard.

"Well?" said Obi-Wan.

"She wasn't there." It came out steadier than he'd expected. "She knew I was coming. She opened up just enough to drag me down three side canyons and lost me in the vents. I never got closer than a kilometer."

Obi-Wan looked at him.

It was not a long look, by Obi-Wan's standards. It took in Anakin's face, and his hands, and the pale mineral crust caked white on his boots — the only white ground Anakin had crossed all morning, a kilometer and a half down the south-east slot. Anakin had felt Obi-Wan's attention in the Force while he stood on that crust: steady, distant, waiting for the flare and roar of two blades meeting. It had never come. Obi-Wan would have felt nothing at all. Nothing but quiet, and then a grief so sharp it must have reached him even through the fight.

"Ah," said Obi-Wan. "That's unfortunate."

"Yes."

"Three side canyons."

"At least."

Obi-Wan nodded slowly, as if Anakin had said something reasonable about the weather. He stood, brushed off his knees, and looked up at the gold seam of sky, where the Judiciary gunship was dropping toward them, grey and unmarked, its engines kicking a storm of grit across the yard.

"For the report, then," he said.

"For the report."

And that was all. Anakin waited for the rest of it — the sigh, the *Anakin*, the quiet question he'd asked on Coruscant that had cut deeper than any blade. It didn't come. Obi-Wan simply let it lie there between them, a lie with both their names on it now, and turned to face the gunship.

Rusk came down the ramp first, in grey armor without insignia, the pale scar through her left brow pulling her eyelid very slightly askew. Behind her a trooper carried the grey case. Anakin made himself not look at it.

"General Skywalker." Her voice was pleasant and exact. "You went off-transponder for thirty-eight minutes."

"I was chasing a fugitive through a canyon system. It doesn't have great reception."

"And?"

"She led me away from the outpost and lost me in the geyser vents. Ventress was here. She's gone too."

Rusk's eyes went to Obi-Wan. "General Kenobi. You were here."

"I was rather occupied with Ventress, Major," Obi-Wan said. "General Skywalker has given his account."

"So he has." She let that sit a moment. Then she touched her ear and listened, and something in her face sharpened. "My sensor officer tells me an unregistered vessel lifted from these canyons nineteen minutes ago. South-east of here. Ore-scow profile, one engine running hot. It made orbit and jumped before the *Resolute* could get a tractor on it."

"Florrum's full of pirates," Anakin said.

"It is." Rusk turned her head and looked south-east, along the line of the canyon, as though she could see the white crust and the place where two people had stood. "The jump vector was Core-ward, General. Toward the Corellian Run." Her gaze came back to him, unhurried. "I'll be reporting that to the Admiral. I expect he'll pass it to the Chancellor's office. They both like to know where things are going."

She went back up the ramp. The trooper with the case followed her.

Anakin stood in the settling grit with Obi-Wan silent at his side and stared at the gold sky, at nothing, at the place where the scow must have climbed and vanished.

Core-ward. She wasn't running away. She was going *back*. Back to Coruscant, to the city that had already decided she was guilty, to the Senate and the Temple and the Chancellor's red office. Back to whoever it was she'd stood on that white crust and refused to name — someone she'd rather protect than save herself with. Someone she still loved.

She was going to prove it in front of everybody, she'd said.

He had just made very sure she would do it alone.

# Chapter 29: The Steps

The fifth candle would not light.

Ahsoka struck the igniter a third time. The flame jumped, caught the wick, shivered, and drowned in its own wax. Behind her the rebuilt hangar was still dim with the hour before dawn, the starfighters asleep in their cradles, a single maintenance droid ticking along the far wall. In front of her, on the low shelf beneath the wall of names, four candles were already burning in a row. Those had lit on the first try.

One for Jackar Bowmani, who had carried a bomb into this room and never known it. Three for CT-2261, CT-4408 and CT-9170, who had died outside a cell door with her lightsabers in someone else's hands.

They were Barriss's candles. For five months Barriss had come down here before the Temple woke and lit four flames without names, and no one had asked her who they were for. Ahsoka had wondered once, at Narkis, and gotten the answer wrong.

*Will you light them?* Barriss had asked, in the voice the choke had left her. *Someone should.*

*Ask me tomorrow.*

Twelve minutes. Then a corridor, a medical bay, a narrow bed in Temple guest quarters, and Obi-Wan in the doorway at first light with a face that had already said it before he did.

It had been tomorrow for a full day now. She had spent most of that day unable to come here.

She pinched the drowned wick straight and tried again. This time the flame took, small and stubborn, and stood up.

There was nothing to write beside it. Barriss's name would never go on the wall above; she was not one of the honored dead, and no one in the Order would ever argue that she should be. So Ahsoka left the fifth candle the way Barriss had left hers. Unmarked. Burning.

She sat back on her heels. Her left forearm still pulled where the saber burn was healing. Her neck didn't hurt at all anymore, which was the strangest part — five months of cold metal, and the skin had nearly forgotten. Her fingers went to it anyway, every few minutes, checking for something that wasn't there.

A woman in the deep blue of the hangar families came in while the light was still grey. She was human, older, a scarf over her hair. She saw Ahsoka and stopped, and Ahsoka watched her recognize the face from five months of Holonet bulletins. The woman stood very still. Then she walked the length of the shelf, lit a candle of her own beneath one of the names at the far end, and stood with it a while. When she left, she did not look at Ahsoka. She had not left early, either.

Ahsoka found she could breathe around that.

She felt Plo Koon before she heard him: the old shape of him in the Force, steady as weather she had grown up under. He came down the line of fighters without hurrying and stopped a respectful distance away.

"I came to light one for her," he said. The antiox mask gave his voice its faint, breathing hiss. "I see I am late."

"There's room."

He lowered himself to one knee beside her, a long way down for a Kel Dor, and lit a sixth candle from her fifth. For a while neither of them spoke. The flames leaned together in the draft from the hangar mouth.

"Before the vote," Plo said, "I told myself that the Republic's law deserved its chance to be fair to you. I said so to Master Windu. I said it very wisely." His clawed hands folded and unfolded on his knee. "Then I sat in my chair and looked at the floor while they took you out of the room. I have thought about that floor every day since. I know its pattern better than my own hands."

Ahsoka didn't answer. She didn't trust what would come out.

"I found you once," he said, "in the grass on Shili. You bit me."

"I was three."

"You had excellent teeth." Something behind the mask might have been a smile. Then it was gone. "I found you when you were too small to know you needed finding. And when you were old enough to know, and you needed it, I did not come. I am sorry, Little 'Soka. There is nothing I can put behind the word to make it larger. I am only sorry."

*Little 'Soka.* No one had called her that since she'd grown taller than the training remotes. In the Deep, in the dark of the Well, she had not let herself remember voices like his. Remembering had been a kind of warmth she couldn't afford.

She put her hand over his. The claws were cool and careful under her palm, the way they had always been careful.

"I tried to be angry with you," she said. "At Narkis there was a lot of time to try." She looked at the six small flames. "It never fit. You were the one person I could never make it fit on."

"You may yet."

"Maybe." She pressed his hand once and let go. "Not today."

He bowed his head. When he rose it was slowly, one hand on the shelf. "The Council will send for you at the third hour. You should know what they mean to offer before you walk in. It would not be fair to let you be surprised in that room twice."

"I know what they're going to offer," Ahsoka said.

Plo looked down at her through the dark lenses for a long moment. Whatever he felt in her then, he didn't ask about it. He only touched the top of her head between her montrals, the way he had when his hand could cover her whole crown, and left her with the candles.

* * *

The Council chamber had not changed. That was the thing she hadn't prepared for.

The same ring of high-backed chairs. The same tall windows full of Coruscant, the traffic lanes stacked into the haze like thread on a spindle. The same circle of floor at the center, worn pale by generations of Padawans standing exactly where she now stood. Five months ago she had stood there in binders. Her wrists remembered. She kept her hands loose at her sides so no one would see them wanting to find each other.

Most of the chairs were filled in person this time. Yoda, small and very still. Windu, upright as a pillar. Ki-Adi-Mundi, Saesee Tiin, Shaak Ti. Plo, who had his head up today. Obi-Wan, with his hands folded and his eyes on her and not, for once, on the door. Anakin was not there. He wasn't on the Council and no one had asked him to come, and after the silence in this room yesterday morning, she understood that hadn't been an accident.

"Ahsoka Tano." Windu let her name sit in the air a moment before he went on. "The confession of Barriss Offee, the recording entered at tribunal, and the depositions of Commandant Vorne and her staff establish that you were innocent of every charge brought against you. This Council expelled you on the strength of those charges. We were wrong."

He didn't soften it and he didn't dress it. He set it down like a man setting down something heavy that he fully intended to carry again later.

"The Council has voted to restore you to the Order," he said. "In light of your conduct on Narkis and since, we have also voted to waive the trials. You would be raised to the rank of Knight."

She felt it go around the room in the Force. Warmth — real, and from more than one chair. Shaak Ti's, open and kind. Plo's, steady. Obi-Wan's, carefully contained and almost hopeful. A year ago, that word would have been the whole sky.

She thought of what a Knight was, in this war. Knights got battalions. She thought of the image Fives had shown them in Padmé's sitting room, the small pale shape grown into every clone's head since before they could open their eyes. She thought of Tup with his hand pressed to his temple, and three hundred men standing to attention in front of her, waiting for orders, and her not knowing who else could give them orders, or what those orders would be.

And she thought of a ruined voice in a cell three doors from her old one. *He knew. From the very beginning.*

"Thank you," she said. "I mean that. But I'm not going to accept."

The warmth in the room went very quiet.

"May we know why?" Ki-Adi-Mundi asked.

"If it's the vote—" Windu began.

"It isn't." She met his eyes. "You took my beads yourself, Master Windu. You said someone who'd voted for it should have to be the one. I hated you for that for about a month." She let out a breath. "Then I decided it was the most honest thing anybody in this room did that day. I'm not refusing so you'll feel it. I don't think it would work on you anyway."

Something moved at the corner of Windu's mouth and went away again.

"Something you carry." Yoda's ears were low, his hands crossed on the head of his stick. "Since the night the healer died, I have felt it. Heavy. And you have decided to carry it alone."

"Yes, Master."

"Tell us, will you?"

"Not yet."

Obi-Wan stirred. "Ahsoka—"

"Five months ago I stood in a room a lot like this one and told the truth," she said. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "I didn't have anything to hold it up with. It fell over, and it took me with it. Now I have something I think might be true. If it is, it's bigger than me. Bigger than Barriss. And all I have to hold it up with is the word of a dead woman who lied to my face for five months. If I bring it in here like that, you'll weigh it, and you'll be right to, and it'll fall over again." She looked around the ring. "I'd rather bring it to you when it can stand."

"And you cannot find that proof as one of us?" Shaak Ti asked gently.

Ahsoka thought about how to say it so it would be true without being cruel.

"A Knight reports to this Council," she said. "This Council answers to the Senate and the Chancellor's office for every battalion and every ship, every week. Anything I found, I'd find from inside that. I need to look at it from outside first." She hesitated. "And some of what I'd be asking about — I don't think you could hear it yet. Not because you're bad. Because you're tired, and at war, and the war needs the answers it already has."

That landed. She felt it land. She felt Windu go still in a way that was not anger, and something in Yoda turn toward her and stay turned.

"That," Windu said, "is a dangerous thing to say in this room."

"I know. I said it anyway." She almost smiled. "That might be the part of me you'd want back, if you wanted any of it."

Obi-Wan cleared his throat. "In fairness, it has been a difficult year for listening."

"One more thing." Ahsoka made herself say it before she lost the nerve. "Whatever this Council is thinking about Anakin. He didn't kill her."

The room cooled by a degree she could feel on her skin.

"The inquiry has been closed," Ki-Adi-Mundi said.

"By the Chancellor," said Windu. Only that. Three words, flat as a blade laid on a table. Ahsoka looked at him, and for one heartbeat she thought — *you feel the shape of it too.* Then he looked away toward the windows, and the moment closed.

"I hope you're right," Obi-Wan said quietly. "About Anakin. I would like very much for you to be right."

"I usually am," Ahsoka said. "It just takes everyone a while."

Plo made a sound behind his mask that might, in a Kel Dor, have been a laugh.

Yoda tapped his stick once against the floor. "Go, then. Not as Jedi. Not as stranger, either." His great eyes held hers, and they were sadder than she had ever seen them. "Outside the walls, you may see further than we can. Come back, and tell us what you find. Listen, we will try."

She bowed to him — properly, the full Temple bow her body still knew better than her mind did — and walked out of the circle on her own feet.

In the corridor outside, a Padawan runner of perhaps twelve was waiting with a grimy square of flimsi held at arm's length, as if it might bite.

"This came to the public gate by relay courier," he said. "From the Undercroft. The Temple Guard scanned it four times. Master Windu said to give it to you before you left, because if it was going to explode he'd prefer it did that outside."

The outside of the fold said, in a sharp, slanting hand: TO THE JEDI WHO ISN'T.

Ahsoka opened it.

*So they asked you back. Of course they did. They always want the knife back once they've finished cutting themselves with it.*

*Say no. Not for my sake. You'd hate it, and you'd be terrible at hating it quietly, and eventually I'd have to hear about it.*

*I have my sabers. I have no further use for you. We're even, which is generous of me. Somewhere there's a Jedi Master still combing ore out of his beard. Tell him I think of him often.*

*Don't come looking for me. I'll know.*

*If someone hurts you again, I may come looking for them. Don't read anything into that.*

*— V.*

Ahsoka laughed out loud in the middle of the Temple corridor. The runner flinched. She folded the flimsi twice and put it inside her coat, against her ribs, where it crackled every time she breathed.

* * *

Kix's hands were shaking.

Rex watched them from the surgical couch, because there was nothing else worth watching in the Resolute's medbay at twenty-three hundred, with the ship cold in its cradle at the fleet yards and most of her crew on leave. The overheads were down to a third. On the tray at Kix's elbow lay a hypo of local anesthetic, a micro-probe, a laser scalpel no longer than a finger, and Rex's old cadet datapad, the cracked screen still showing the last image Kix had pulled from Fives's files: a scan of a clone's skull and, inside it, small and neat as a seed, a shape that had no business being there.

"Say it again," Kix said.

"It's in me," Rex said. "It's in you. It's in Jesse. It was in Tup."

Kix's jaw worked. He'd read the files for three hours without a word — the embryo records, the scan logs, the droid's notes — then played the forty-second video once, gone into the head, and been sick. Rex had heard it through the hatch. Kix had come back with his face washed and his eyes hard and said, "On the couch, Captain," and that had been twenty minutes ago. Since then he'd scanned Rex twice, checked the result against Fives's images three times, and not touched the scalpel.

"A droid did this," Kix said. "A Kaminoan medical droid with a surgical arm and fifty years of their own research. Not a field medic with a cutting laser in the middle of the night."

"Fives is walking around."

"Fives is lucky."

"Tup wasn't," Rex said.

Kix looked at the scan, then at the side of Rex's head, then at his own hands. "If I go a hair wide—"

"Then you write down what you did wrong, and you do Jesse better." Rex kept his voice flat, the voice he used on a comm channel when the line was coming apart. "That's an order. The writing it down part."

"That's not funny."

"Wasn't trying."

He hadn't done this at the Seventh Refinery. Fives had all but begged him, shivering in the outflow chamber with his shoulder gone bad, and Rex had said *not yet*. He'd told himself he needed proof, needed time, needed the Commander cleared first. Then the tribunal. Then Barriss Offee dead in a cell whose door had never opened, and the Chancellor's aide on the Holonet closing the matter before the Council could sit down to discuss it. Something about the speed of that had gone into Rex like cold through the seams of his armor.

He had stood in a gallery five months ago and said nothing while they sentenced her. He had knocked Fox's arm and then let his report say nothing about it. He was done waiting to find out what he would do with an order until after it came.

"Kix," he said. "Do it, or give me the scalpel and a mirror."

Kix picked up the hypo.

The anesthetic went in cold above Rex's right ear and spread until that side of his head felt like it belonged to someone in the next room. Kix lined up the scanner, muttered numbers, and set the probe. There was no pain. There was pressure, and then a sound Rex heard from inside his own skull, small and gritty, like a boot shifting on gravel. He fixed his eyes on a rivet in the overhead and counted its edges.

The probe trembled against him.

"Steady," Rex said.

"I am steady."

"You're shaking."

"I'm steady on the inside, Captain. Shut up."

Rex shut up. He listened to Kix breathe — ragged, then slower, then slower again, until the breathing was something a man could set a chrono by. The trembling at the side of his head stopped. Kix was counting under his breath now, very low, the way he counted compressions on a battlefield. Rex had heard him count like that on Umbara. Over Tup. Over men who lived and men who didn't.

A click, small and precise.

"Got it," Kix said, and his voice cracked straight down the middle.

He held up the forceps. Pinched in the tips was a thing no bigger than a fingernail paring, pale grey shot faintly with pink, organic and somehow neat. It didn't look like much. It didn't look like anything that could make a man shoot a Jedi who was kneeling to check on him.

Kix dropped it into a specimen vial, sealed the cap, and then sat down on the deck rather suddenly, with his back against the cabinet.

Rex waited to feel different.

He didn't. That was the worst of it, and maybe the best. The medbay looked the same. The rivet looked the same. Somewhere far back in his head there was no sudden silence, no lifting weight, nothing at all to tell him what the thing had been doing all these years — whether it had ever leaned on him, or only waited. He couldn't tell which of his old orders had been his. He decided, lying there with half his head numb, that it didn't much matter anymore. The next ones would be.

Kix got up eventually, sealed the incision, and smoothed a bacta patch over it with his thumb. "How do you feel?"

"Like I've got a hole in my head."

"You do."

"Then the medic's accurate. Good to know."

Kix almost laughed. He labeled the vial in his small square print — CT-7567 — looked at it, scratched the number out, and wrote REX instead. Then he hid it at the back of the refrigerated cabinet behind a rack of expired fever shots, where nobody on this ship would ever look.

"Who else knows?" he asked, with his back still turned.

"A dead man," Rex said. "A Senator. A bounty hunter who'd prefer you never mention her. And the Commander."

Kix turned around. "The Commander. Then she's—"

"Leaving tonight."

"With him." Kix didn't say the name. He didn't have to. Something in his face eased, and then set. "I'm next."

"Not tonight."

"No. Not tonight." Kix looked down at his hands, which were quiet now. "Somebody with steady hands has to do me, and it isn't going to be you."

"We'll find someone."

"You keep saying *we*, Captain."

"Get used to it," Rex said.

At midnight his comlink chirped on the dead Guard fault channel: a routine power-cell error, a fault class retired years ago, signed at the bottom in the old cipher. Rex read the signature twice.

*5555.*

All four digits. None of them flipped. Whole.

Rex winced at it anyway, the way he did every night. Fives was still using the Guard's own band to tell him he was alive. A dead fault class was a good hiding place right up until some bored tech in Fox's comm section ran an audit and wondered why a retired error kept turning up at midnight.

Rex keyed the acknowledgment. Then, against every rule he'd set for this channel, he added a line of plain text and sent it before he could think better of it.

*Tell her her sense of direction is still terrible. Tell her I said so.*

He lay back on the couch with the numbness fading from the side of his head and, for the first time he could remember, listened to the inside of his own skull, and found it quieter than he had expected. Quieter, and entirely his.

* * *

Anakin found her on the Temple steps at the second hour of night, with a grey Judiciary evidence case under his arm and its seal already broken.

She had been sitting halfway down for a while, her pack beside her, watching the lanes stream past the Processional Way. The wind up here came off the city in long warm gusts that smelled of ozone and engine heat. Far across the haze, the Senate district glittered. She had been trying not to pick out one particular window high in the Executive Building, glowing a deep, comfortable red. She kept finding it anyway.

Anakin sat down a step above her without being asked, the way he always had.

"Fox made me sign for these four times," he said. "Four. I think he enjoyed it."

"Fox doesn't enjoy anything."

"He enjoyed that." He opened the case across his knees.

They lay in grey foam, side by side, each still wearing its yellow tag. *#417. #418.* She hadn't held them since the night Fox took them from her at a cell door.

Ahsoka lifted the green one out first. The hilt settled into her palm like a word she'd been trying to remember. Through it, faintly, the crystal knew her — and beneath that, like a cold spot in deep water, it remembered what someone else's hand had made it do outside her cell. Three men. She held it until the cold spot was just a part of it, the way a scar is part of skin. Then she took the shoto, and the two of them together were the right weight at last, and she clipped them on her belt and could not, for a moment, say anything.

"There's one more." Anakin reached into the case's lid and drew out the brushed-steel hilt with the dented emitter and the worn black grip. "You handed me this one in the rain. I'm told it belongs to a pirate."

She took it from him. It was heavier than hers. It always would be.

"I'll keep it a while," she said. "Someone gentle carried this before Hondo got his hands on it. He's going to tell me who, even if I have to sit through six versions first. Then I'll give it back."

"You're going to Florrum."

"Eventually. It's a big galaxy." She slid the blue saber into the inner pocket of her coat, next to Ventress's letter and the datacard Padmé had pressed into her hand at the door. One string of characters on it, copied out of Vorne's deposition: the off-world authorization code on the order that had sent her to Cut Three in her first week, to a terrace whose camera nobody had fixed in two years. Padmé's people could not trace it. Neither, apparently, could the Judiciary. *Somebody traded somebody something,* Tess had said, that first week, and Ahsoka still did not know who had done the trading, or for what. Florrum meant Hondo's, and Hondo's meant Tess Varro, still mending in one of his back rooms; Ahsoka found she didn't yet know what she would say to her. "I've got time now. That's new."

He was quiet. Below them an air-taxi idled at the foot of the steps, its running lights pulsing amber. She had asked it to wait. It was costing her credits she had borrowed from Padmé, and the driver had stopped looking patient some time ago.

"They offered you Knighthood," Anakin said.

"They did."

"Obi-Wan told me. He said it to the wall behind my head, but he told me." Anakin turned his mechanical hand over on his knee, the glove creased at the knuckles. "I would've stood up there with you. You know that."

"You'd have cried."

"I would not."

"You'd have cried in front of Master Windu, and he'd have pretended not to see it, and it would've been the best day of his life."

That got a laugh out of him, a short broken one. It didn't last. "So where?"

"Somewhere I can think." She picked her words. "Somebody's waiting for me."

"Your friend who smells like bacta."

She looked at him. He looked back, steadily, and she saw that he had known for days and had chosen, every one of those days, not to pull on the thread.

"Don't ask," she said.

He didn't.

"Tell Senator Amidala thank you for me," Ahsoka said. "For the tea."

Something passed behind his eyes, quick as a shutter. "I'll see that she gets it."

She didn't ask how he planned to reach a Senator at this hour. She let it go by, the way he had let the bacta go by. It seemed fair.

The wind gusted. Across the city the red window burned on, patient as a coal. She had carried it for two days in her mouth, unsaid, and it had grown heavier with every hour, and she had come out here tonight knowing she would not leave Coruscant with it still there.

"Barriss told me something," she said. "The night she died."

"She told you she was sorry. You said."

"That's what I told you. It wasn't all she said." She made herself look at him. "She said the Chancellor knew. From the beginning. That he had a recording of her killing Letta before my trial ever started, and he sealed it and sat on it. That he used her. That he's the one who sent her to the warehouse to make sure I never testified."

At first he didn't move at all. Then he laughed — not the broken laugh, something worse, a sound with nothing behind it.

"No," he said.

"Anakin—"

"No. Snips. Don't." He shook his head, slowly, as if something had struck him there. "He's the only one who did anything. The Council threw you out. The Senate wanted you dead. He's the one who stood up in front of all of them and—"

"Said *death*," Ahsoka said, "so he could say *life*. So you'd be grateful."

"He gave you your life."

"He gave me my life back after he watched them take it. He forgave me for something he knew I didn't do." It came out quieter than she meant. "That's not a gift. That's a leash, and it isn't even on me."

"She was lying." His voice rose, then caught. "She lied to you for five months. To your face. She healed your shoulder while she was letting you rot. That was the last thing she had left to do to anyone, and she did it to you."

"Maybe."

"She *was*."

"Maybe," Ahsoka said again. "I don't know. That's why I'm going." She turned on the step so she was facing him. "I'm not asking you to believe it. I'm asking you to be careful of him. Watch what he asks for. Watch what it costs, and who pays. That's all. Just watch."

He stared past her at the city. She felt him in the Force the way she had in the canyon, stones lifting at the edges of him — but nothing lifted tonight. He was holding all of it down, and it was costing him.

"I can't hear this," he said at last. "Not about him. Not from you. Don't ask me to."

"I know you can't." She kept her voice gentle. "Hold onto it anyway. Put it somewhere you don't look. Someday you might need it."

He didn't answer.

But somewhere in it he had taken her hand. She wasn't sure when. He'd taken it with his right, the metal one, which couldn't feel her at all; she could feel the servos in his knuckles shift and tighten. He didn't believe her. He didn't let go.

She let that be enough. It was more than she'd had in a long time.

They sat like that while the lanes ran on overhead, two small figures on the long white steps, until the air-taxi driver leaned on his horn. Ahsoka stood and shouldered her pack. Anakin stood with her, and still didn't let go, and so they went down together — one step, and the next, and the next, the way you walk someone home. He didn't speak, and neither did she. There wasn't anything left to say that the hand didn't.

"Look after Rex," she said at the bottom.

"Rex looks after me."

"Then let him."

He nodded once. His grip tightened, and for a heartbeat she thought he wouldn't loosen it at all — and then she stepped off the last stair onto the plaza, and the distance between the Temple and the city did what neither of them could.

Somewhere below, at Berth Nine, a scrap hauler with a dead man aboard was warming its engines. Somewhere above, a red window glowed. She was free, and she was not in exile, and she had chosen both. And she knew, with a certainty that sat in her as calm and cold as ice, that the galaxy was darker than anyone behind her on those steps believed.

Twice now the Republic had given its verdict on Ahsoka Tano — guilty, and then forgiven — and both times it had been wrong. With her own sabers at her hip and his grip still warm in her palm, she walked into the city and gave her own.

*Not guilty. Not forgiven. Not theirs.*

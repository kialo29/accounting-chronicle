# Chapter 12: Ringo Vinda

Ringo Vinda Station turned once every six minutes, and every six minutes the local sun swung around through the shattered dome of the transit hub and blinded half the 501st.

Rex had learned to count it. Six minutes of grey half-light: emergency strips, droid optics, the red stutter of blaster bolts across the deck. Then the white came rolling over the plates like surf, his visor dimming a beat too slowly, and for ten seconds every trooper in the hub was firing at memory. Then grey again.

"Sun in thirty," he said on the squad channel. "Heads down when it comes."

"Copy," Jesse said, from behind a toppled cargo sled. "Somebody tell the clankers."

The hub was a wheel inside the wheel, a round chamber two hundred meters across where six freight spokes met and crossed. Before the war it had moved ore and tibanna and passengers around the station's rim. Now it was the only thing holding the station together, and Admiral Trench's forces had held it for nine days. His flagship sat off the station's dark side, out of reach of the Republic guns and close enough to pour fresh droids down the spokes faster than the 501st could cut them down.

So the plan was simple, the way the bad ones usually were. General Skywalker and most of the legion would keep Trench busy at the docking ring on the far side. Two companies and two Jedi would take the hub and seal the spokes one by one, until the droids had nowhere left to come from.

The two Jedi were already doing their part.

Rex had served with Generals Tiplar and Tiplee twice before, and he still couldn't always tell where one of them stopped. They were Mikkian sisters, twins, head-tendrils bound back with plain cord, and they fought as if they had been issued one mind and four arms between them. Their blades crossed through each other's space and never touched. Tiplar talked through every fight—to her sister, to the troopers, to the droids, once memorably to a tank. Tiplee almost never spoke at all, and when she did, Tiplar usually laughed.

They said thank you, too. Rex had noticed that. Not every general did.

"Spoke four!" Tiplar called now. Her blade swept a volley back up the tunnel mouth and three droids folded. "Captain, I need that door shut before the next lot comes down it!"

"Fives," Rex said.

"On it." Fives was already moving, low and fast along the curve of the wall toward the spoke-four seal controls. Rex went with him, laying fire up the tunnel.

The sun came.

White everywhere. Rex dropped to one knee behind a support column and kept shooting at where the tunnel mouth had been, short bursts, counting. Beside him a trooper from Bravo Company was swearing steadily. Somewhere to the left a spider droid's legs clattered on the plates, and then a lightsaber hummed and the clattering stopped.

Grey again.

Fives had the seal panel open, wires in his teeth. Across the hub, Tiplee had driven the droids out of spoke two alone and was standing in its mouth with her blade up, still as a carving. Rex swept the line out of habit. Jesse, behind his sled. Kix, dragging a wounded trooper toward the medical point by his drag-strap. Tup, on the left at the spoke-three junction—

Tup had his hand against his helmet again. The heel of his palm pressed hard into the right side, where the temple would be. Third time in the last hour.

Three months. The headaches had started, as far as Rex could tell, the night they'd been ordered to strike the Commander off the roster. Kix had nagged him about it every week since. Tup always had an answer: the lights, the recycled air, the bunk under the vent. He'd skipped two scans. Rex had let it go, because Tup kept his rifle clean and made his marks, and because Rex had had other things on his mind in those months. He had told himself a headache was a headache.

"Tup," he said on the channel. "You good?"

The hand came down. "Fine, sir. Just the glare."

"Stay low."

"Sir."

"Got it," Fives said, and the spoke-four seal came down with a long shriek of durasteel, cutting a battle droid neatly in half on the way.

Two spokes left. They took spoke five in the next turn of the station, and spoke one in the turn after, and Rex lost two troopers doing it. He would write their numbers down later. He always did.

Then the hub was quiet.

Not silent. Silent never happened. Smoke curled out of a burning sled. A wounded droid's vocabulator repeated half a word. Somewhere in the ducts, the station's air system coughed and caught. But nobody was shooting, and the troopers began to straighten up from behind their cover the way men do when they don't quite trust the quiet yet.

"Good work," Tiplar said, to everyone. She deactivated her blade and pushed a loose tendril back under its cord. "Very good work. Captain, my sister and I will hold here until the General—"

She stopped. She was looking past Rex, to the left.

Tup was standing at the spoke-three junction with his head bowed. His rifle hung from one hand. The other hand was pressed flat against his temple again, and he wasn't moving at all.

Tiplar went to him. She didn't hurry; there was no reason to hurry. She went the way she'd go to any trooper standing wrong after a fight, and Rex watched her do it and thought, *good*, because Tup listened to her. Tup liked her. He'd said once she was the only general who'd ever asked his name twice to make sure she had it right.

"Trooper." She crouched a little, to see under the brim of his helmet. "Did you take a knock? Let me look."

The sun came around.

Rex would remember afterward that he saw it clearly, even in the white. That the light behind the twins was so bright they were only shapes, but Tup was turned the other way and Rex could see him perfectly. He saw the hand come away from the helmet. He saw it come away smoothly, without hurry, the way a man lowers his hand after a salute. He saw the rifle come up the same way.

One shot.

It was not loud. There had been louder all day.

The white rolled past. In the grey that followed, Tiplar was lying on the deck at Tup's feet, and Tup was standing over her with his rifle at his shoulder and his head tilted, as if he were listening to something a long way off.

"It was the order," he said.

Nobody moved. Rex could not afterward have said how long that lasted. A breath. Two.

Then Tiplee's blade lit across the hub.

She did not cry out. She came across the deck in the silence like a thrown thing, and Rex was moving before he knew it—not toward Tup, but in front of him, between a Jedi General and his own trooper, his blaster pointed at the floor and his free hand up.

"General!" His voice cracked on it. "General, we have him. We *have* him."

Fives hit Tup from the side, low, the way they'd been taught in the cadet rings. Tup went down without resisting. The rifle skidded across the plates and Jesse stamped on it. Fives was on Tup's back with one knee, wrenching his arms behind him, and he was saying, "Tup, it's me, it's Fives, Tup, look at me—" and Tup's helmet was turned sideways against the deck, and Rex could hear him through the open channel, quite calm.

"It was the order. It was the order. It was the—"

Tiplee stopped an arm's length from Rex. Her blade was so close he could feel the hum of it in his teeth. Her face was not angry. That was what he'd remember. It wasn't anything at all.

Then she looked down at her sister, and the blade went out, and she knelt.

Rex lowered his hand. Behind him, Tup kept saying it, over and over, in the voice he used for roll call, until Kix got a sedative through the seal of his armor and the words slowed and blurred and finally stopped.

The sun came around again. Nobody bothered to duck.

* * *

They put Tup in the isolation room off the *Resolute*'s medbay, because the brig was full of captured tactical droids and nobody wanted to argue about where a clone belonged.

Six hours after the hub, he was strapped upright in a restraint chair behind a pane of transparisteel, out of his armor, in a grey undersuit with his head shaved for the electrode pads Kix hadn't been allowed to attach. His eyes were open. Every so often his lips moved. The room was soundproofed, and Rex was grateful for that, and ashamed of being grateful.

"No scans," Kix said. He was standing at the glass with a med scanner in his hand, holding it the way a man holds something he's been told to put down. "No samples. No neural workup. Not even a blood panel, Captain. I asked."

"Who said no?"

"Came down from the medical officer. Who got it from Fleet. Who got it from Kamino." Kix's jaw worked. "Patient's to be kept sedated and transferred as-is. Kaminoan specialists will conduct the examination. Field medics are not to—" he made the quote marks in the air with two fingers, which Rex had never seen him do "—*compromise the subject*."

"The subject," Rex said.

"That's what it says."

The *Resolute*'s legal liaison had come and gone already: a Judge Advocate lieutenant, a young human with a pale careful face, who'd taken statements in the corridor and used words like *preliminary* and *deliberate act* and *no indication of coercion at this time*. He'd said there would be a formal inquiry. He had also said, in the tone of a man reading a weather report, that the possibility of Separatist conditioning could not be excluded, and that the trooper's service record would be annotated pending findings.

Annotated. Rex had thought about that word for a long time after the lieutenant left. He knew what an annotation was. It was the first line of a verdict.

By the time the troopers' mess sat down for the evening meal, the word going around wasn't *annotated*. Rex had heard it in the lift and in the armory and in the line for caf. Tup had turned. Tup had been bought. Tup was a clanker in a clone suit. A sergeant from Bravo who had never met him said he'd always thought there was something off about that one. Rex had stopped and looked at the sergeant until the sergeant found something else to do.

It had taken less than a day. That was the part that sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone. Less than a day, and men who had shared a barracks with Tup for three years were saying *that one*.

He'd felt this before. He'd been standing in the back of a Senate chamber three months ago, helmet under his arm, while a clerk read out *guilty* four times, and he had felt exactly this: the floor not quite where he'd left it. The sense of something enormous and certain moving through the room, and everyone stepping aside to let it pass.

He'd stepped aside, that time. He'd hunted her through the lower levels on orders, and he'd stepped aside.

The medbay doors opened, and General Skywalker came in.

He was still in the scorched armor from the docking ring, a long black scar across one shoulder plate where something had come too close. He'd lost weight since Ordaine—since Narkis, maybe; he never said much about either—and in the medbay lights his face was all hollows. He stopped next to Rex at the glass and watched Tup without speaking.

"General Tiplee?" Rex asked finally.

"With her sister." Skywalker's voice was flat. "She won't leave. I didn't ask her to." His mechanical hand closed slowly on the edge of the observation console and opened again. Rex watched the knuckles move under the dark glove. "What happened, Rex? Not the report. I read the report."

So Rex told him. The hand on the temple. Three months of headaches, the skipped scans. The way the rifle came up—not like a man who'd snapped. Like a man doing a drill.

"He didn't know her, sir. Not really. He *liked* her." Rex heard his own voice go rough and pulled it back. "There's no reason in it. I've been turning it over for six hours and there's no reason in it anywhere."

"The Judge Advocate seems to have found one."

"The Judge Advocate found a word."

Skywalker looked at him then. And Rex saw that the General knew exactly what he meant, had known it before Rex said it, had been standing in the same Senate chamber with the same stone in his stomach.

"*Deliberate*," Skywalker said quietly. "I've heard that one before."

Neither of them said her name. They didn't need to.

"Sir." Fives was in the doorway. He'd been there for a while, Rex realized; he'd come off the hangar deck still half in armor, his dented helmet under his arm. He'd never had the dent hammered out of it. "Sir, permission to speak."

"Go ahead, Fives."

Fives came to the glass. He didn't look at Tup. He looked at Skywalker, very straight.

"I've known Tup since we were cadets, General. He apologized to a training droid once. Out loud. In front of the whole squad, and we never let him forget it." Fives swallowed. "Whatever that was in the hub, it wasn't him. Something's wrong in his head. Something *physical*. And they've just told our medic he's not allowed to look."

"Kamino will look," Skywalker said.

"Yes, sir. That's what I'm afraid of."

It came out too fast, and too loud, and the medical officer at the far station glanced up. Fives didn't take it back.

"They'll call it a defect," he said, lower. "Or a Separatist trick. Or they'll call it a rogue trooper and write him off and send the rest of us a nice memo about vigilance. I want to go with him, General. I want to be in the room. Somebody who knows him should be in the room."

Skywalker was quiet for a moment. Rex watched him look from Fives to the glass, and from the glass to Rex, and he wondered what the General was seeing. A trooper begging to stand next to the accused. Rex had a guess at whose face he was seeing instead.

"Medical transport leaves at oh-nine-hundred," Skywalker said. "You're the escort. I'll clear it with Fleet. If anyone argues, tell them to call me." He paused. "And Fives. Be polite to the Kaminoans."

"I'm always polite, sir."

"Be *more* polite."

The corner of Fives's mouth moved. It wasn't quite a smile. "Yes, sir."

Skywalker left. On his way out he put his hand on Rex's shoulder, briefly, the way he used to do in the old days after a bad fight, back when there had been three of them on the command deck instead of two. Then he was gone, and Rex was alone with Kix and Fives and the glass, and behind it Tup's lips were moving again.

Rex walked down to the morgue before he turned in. He didn't know why. He stood in the doorway and did not go in.

General Tiplee was sitting on a low stool beside the slab, her hands folded on her knees. She had loosened the cord from her sister's tendrils and set it on the slab beside her, neatly coiled. She didn't look up.

"Captain," she said. "Did he hate her?"

It was the first thing Rex had ever heard her say without her sister there to laugh at it.

"No, General," he said. "He liked her. She learned his name."

Tiplee was silent for a while. Then she nodded, once, as if that were worse, and Rex left her there.

* * *

The medical shuttle was Kaminoan, long and white and smooth as a riverstone, and it sat at the far end of the *Resolute*'s main hangar looking as though it had been set down there by mistake.

Rex got there at oh-eight-thirty. Fives was already at the foot of the ramp in full kit, checking the charge on his sidearm for what was probably the fifth time. Two Kaminoan medical attendants had wheeled Tup aboard in a sealed pod an hour ago. Rex had watched from the gantry. Through the pod's small window Tup's face had looked very young and very peaceful, and Rex had not gone down.

"Captain."

"Fives."

They stood together at the bottom of the ramp. Around them the hangar went on with its morning: a gunship being hosed down, a load lifter beeping in reverse, two pilots arguing over a cracked canopy. Normal noise. It was a long time since anything had felt normal to Rex. He wasn't sure he'd notice anymore.

"Report every twelve hours," Rex said. "Standard check-in. I want to know what they find."

"Yes, sir."

"I mean it. Not just the findings. I want to know who you talk to. Who's in the room. What they say when they think you're not listening."

Fives turned his head and looked at him properly. "You think I'll need that."

"I think a field medic was ordered not to take a blood sample from a trooper who just killed a Jedi General," Rex said. "I think that's an odd thing to order. And I've been keeping count of odd things lately."

Fives didn't answer right away. He looked down at the dented helmet in his hands, rubbed his thumb across the crease in the brow.

"You know what I keep thinking about?" he said. "The lower levels. All those nights after she was sentenced. Every stall we went into, every broker, every lead. And the one time we got close—"

"Pesk."

"Pesk." Fives's mouth flattened. "Dead before the General got there. Not a mark on him. Hands folded. Cleaned out." He turned the helmet over. "Somebody tidied up after her, Captain. Somebody made sure the story stayed the story. And now Tup goes strange in the head and a day later there's an order from Kamino that nobody's allowed to look inside it. Maybe it's nothing. Maybe that's just the way they do things."

"Maybe."

"But you've been keeping count."

"I have."

Up the ramp, one of the Kaminoan attendants appeared in the hatch, tall and grey-white and patient, and inclined her long neck toward Fives. Time.

Fives put his helmet on. Then he took it off again. He stood there with it in both hands, and for a moment he looked less like an ARC trooper than like the stubborn cadet who used to ask the instructors *why* until they assigned him extra laps for it.

"Captain," he said. "If something happens on Kamino. If it goes wrong—if they decide it's easier to write Tup off, and anyone who argues with them along with him—"

"Fives."

"Let me say it." His voice was low and very steady. "I watched them take the Commander apart. In that chamber, in front of everyone. And she stood there and took it, because she trusted them to get it right in the end. She trusted the system all the way down to the bottom." He shook his head slowly. "I won't. If it comes to that, I won't go quietly. Not the way she had to."

Rex looked at him for a long moment.

"She didn't go quietly," he said. "She went with her head up. There's a difference."

"Not to the people who sent her," Fives said. "Not on paper."

Rex had no answer to that. He had spent three months looking for one.

So he did the only thing he had. He came to attention and saluted, and Fives saluted back, crisp and correct, the way they had both been made to do it ten thousand times on Kamino. Then Fives put his helmet on, and this time he left it there, and he went up the ramp without looking back.

The hatch sealed. The shuttle lifted on a whisper of repulsors, turned its smooth white nose toward the shield, and slid out of the hangar into the dark. Rex watched until the engine glow folded into hyperspace and was gone.

Jesse was standing behind him when he turned around. Rex didn't know how long he'd been there.

"Do we really think it's nothing, sir?"

"No," Rex said, and walked past him.

* * *

The datapad was old—a cadet-issue model, scuffed at the corners, with a crack across one end of its screen. Rex had kept it at the bottom of his footlocker since Kamino for no reason he'd ever been able to explain to himself. It still held a charge. That night, with his door locked, he took the back plate off with the point of his knife, found the little network chip, and prised it out. He held it up to the light for a second. Then he crushed it under the heel of his boot and swept the pieces into the waste chute.

Nothing on it now could be pulled by Fleet, or by anyone else. Nothing could be pushed onto it either.

He sat on his bunk with the pad on his knee and the cursor blinking, and for a long while he didn't write anything. Writing it down made it real. Writing it down meant he was the kind of trooper who kept a file on his own side.

Then he thought of Tup's hand coming away from his temple, smooth as a salute, and he began.

He didn't write what he felt. He wasn't sure he could have. He wrote it the way he'd write an after-action report, numbers and times, because that was the only language he trusted to hold still.

*Day 0. Commander Tano sentenced. Order to strike her from 501st roster received approx. one hour after sentencing. Not confirmed. Still not confirmed.*

*Same night. CT-5385 (Tup) reports headache, right temple. Declines scan. First occurrence on record.*

He stopped. He went back and changed *on record* to *that I know of*, because that was the truth, and the truth was the whole point.

*Day 1. Fox mentions Force-dampening collar to be fitted in flight. New device. Nobody asked why it was ready so fast.*

*Six weeks approx. Dalo Pesk, Kesh Arcade stall 41. Lead found by Fives. Pesk dead before General S. arrived. No marks. Stall cleaned. Seven datapads and cam core missing.*

*Weeks 1–13. Tup reports headaches, right temple, recurring. Skips two scans. I let it go.*

He read that last line twice. He left it.

*Ringo Vinda. Tup kills General Tiplar. No warning. No motive. Repeats "It was the order" until sedated. Does not resist arrest.*

*Same day. Order: no scans, no samples, no neural workup by field medics. Subject to be transferred unexamined to Kamino.*

He wanted the time of that order. He pulled up the *Resolute*'s medbay log on his own terminal, found the directive from Kamino where Kix had shown it to him, and copied the timestamp across by hand. Then, because it was a habit, he pulled up his own incident report to note when it had been filed.

He looked at the two times side by side.

He looked again.

His report—the first report of the incident from anyone on the station, the one he'd dictated with Tiplar's blood still drying on his gauntlets—had cleared the *Resolute*'s comm queue for Fleet at fourteen-twelve.

The directive from Kamino ordering that CT-5385 be kept sealed and unexamined had been logged by the medbay at fourteen-oh-three.

Nine minutes. Kamino had known what to forbid nine minutes before anyone had told them there was anything to forbid.

Rex sat very still. Somewhere in the decks below, the ship's engines changed pitch as the *Resolute* came about. He checked the times a third time. They didn't change.

Slowly, carefully, he typed it in. Then he reached for his comlink and keyed the secure channel he'd assigned to Fives for the check-ins, though it was hours too early, though there was nothing he could tell him yet except *watch your back*.

The channel opened. A voice answered—not Fives. Smooth and recorded and courteous.

*This transport is under medical quarantine by order of the Kamino Cloning Facility. External communications are suspended for the duration of transit. Thank you for your understanding.*

The link closed.

Rex sat on his bunk with the dead comlink in one hand and the cracked datapad in the other, in a locked room on a Republic warship among three thousand of his brothers, and listened to the hull hum around him. For the first time in his life, he could not have said whether the thing he was afraid of was out there, past the hull, or somewhere in here with them.

At the top of a new page, he typed: *CT-5555. Departed 0900. Comms cut.*

Then he sat looking at it, and waited for the check-in he already knew wasn't coming.

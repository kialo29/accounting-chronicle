# Chapter 26: The Dark Between Stars

The mask was lying face-up on a beam just inside the west door, as if someone had set it there to keep it out of the wet.

Anakin knew it before he knew anything else in the room. Bone-white. Slanted slits for eyes. The last time he had seen it, it had been forty meters above him, looking down from a catwalk through dust and the shriek of failing durasteel while he held twenty stories of other people's lives on the end of his arm. It hadn't moved, that night. It hadn't run. It had stood there and watched him choose.

He had followed Padmé to get here. He had lain in the dark at 500 Republica with his eyes closed and heard her moving about the sitting room without lighting a lamp, listened to the door sigh shut behind her at second hour, and he had counted to two hundred before he reached for his boots. Two lift cars behind her down the public shaft. Three transit carriages behind her on the Dovrin Line, the train rattling past the old platform under its cage of scaffolding — he had not looked at it — and then the lower terminal, and the crowds, and her plain grey cloak gone among a thousand others.

It hadn't mattered. Ahsoka was down here somewhere, burning like a lamp left on in a flooded room, and he had only needed to walk toward the light.

Except there had been something next to her. A second presence, quiet and careful and folded in on itself, that he knew from the Resolute's medbay and couldn't make sense of. He had still been trying to make sense of it when red light began to flicker in the high broken windows of the warehouse ahead, and he heard the hum, and he ran.

By the time he reached the iron stair it was quiet. Only rain, and water running somewhere, and Ahsoka's voice below him, low and wrecked: *Not for anyone.*

Now he stood inside the west door with the mask at his elbow, and the room told him the rest.

Black water an inch deep across the floor. A red hilt lying at the foot of the loading ramp, another one half-sunk beside a fallen crane hook — curved hilts, Ventress's; he'd have known them in his sleep. Ventress herself, a few paces in from the south doors, a pale shape gone very still, staring at him. And in the middle of the floor, Ahsoka, the pirate's blue hilt cold in her hand and another that wasn't hers hooked on her belt, a fresh burn shining on her forearm. Standing over someone.

Barriss Offee was on her knees in the water. Her hood had fallen back. The diamond markings on her face were stark in the grey light, and her hands lay open on her thighs, green and patterned and empty.

He didn't think it. It arrived, all of it at once, the way a hull breach arrives — not a sound but a sudden absence of everything that had been holding the air in.

The Soresu turn on the catwalk, tight and economical, the Temple's oldest defensive form. Done by someone who'd learned it from a master who'd learned it from a master. A healer's hands. The healer who had gone to Narkis and come back and stood beside him in the hangar telling him, so gently, how Ahsoka had looked — thinner, but steady, she asks after you. The healer who had gone white at the sight of Rusk's collar case while he stood there thinking *she understands, at least one of them understands.* Who had sat in the gallery every day of the trial. Who had been aboard his ship. Who had been at Florrum, a deck away, while he lied to Obi-Wan about the vents.

Someone Ahsoka loved. He'd had that much in the canyon, and it had nearly killed him not to have the rest.

He had the rest now.

And under all of it, the thing he could not get his mind around, the thing that kept turning over and showing him its other side: Dovrin. The stack tilting. The Gran mother with her child, the Ithorian clutching his ribs, the nosebleed, his fingers locking around nothing. He had held it, and she had stood on the catwalk and watched him hold it, and then she had walked away. And because he had held it, she had been free to walk. And because she had walked, Ahsoka had gone into the ice for four months with a grey collar around her neck.

He had chosen. Every life on that platform, against one person in a mask. He had been proud of it, in some small hidden place — the one thing that whole night he'd done right.

Barriss lifted her head and looked at him.

"Master Skywalker," she said. Her voice was hoarse, scraped thin by whatever she had already said tonight. "You held it up. I stood on the catwalk and I watched you do it. I knew you would. It's the best thing about you." Her mouth moved, not a smile. "I was counting on it."

Ahsoka said, "Barriss, *don't* —"

"I let her pay for it," Barriss said, still to him. "I'm sorry."

He did not decide anything. Later he would try to find the moment and there would be no moment, only his right hand coming up — the mechanical one, inside its dark glove — and closing.

Barriss's hands flew to her throat.

She came up out of the water the way a puppet comes up when someone finally lifts the strings. Knees, then feet, the black water running off the hem of her robe, her boots scraping the floor and then not touching it. He drew her up until her face was level with his own. Her heels kicked once against nothing. Her fingers clawed at the air beneath her jaw where there was no hand to pull away.

It was easy. That was the thing he would remember longest. Holding the stack had nearly split him in half; this was nothing. This was the simplest thing he had ever done. The Force ran through him cold and clean and enormous, and every night of the last five months came down his arm with it — the tribunal rail denting under his fist, the red office, *life*, the transport lifting off from Platform Six without him, the steel cup folding in on itself in a room on Narkis, Windu's face, the beads. Ahsoka's collar light dying in his dream. All of it had been waiting for somewhere to go, and here at last was somewhere.

*Let her know it,* something in him said. *Let her know exactly what it was like for the woman on the bench.*

Barriss stopped clawing. Her hands fell away from her throat and hung at her sides, and she looked at him over the invisible fist, and she let him.

Somewhere above, high in the dark of the crane gantry, he felt Padmé — a bright spike of horror, so sharp and so close that for a heartbeat it nearly reached him. Something moved up there in the dark of the gantry, a shape, a hand, and was gone. He didn't care. He didn't care about anything.

"Anakin."

Ahsoka walked into his line.

She didn't ignite anything. She jammed the pirate's hilt onto her belt, tore Barriss's free of it and let it fall into the water at her feet, and stepped between them with her hands open, so that his raised fist was pointed at her face, and she put her palm flat against his knuckles. He could feel the warmth of it through the glove, through the metal, which wasn't possible.

"Get out of the way, Snips."

"No."

"She did it." His voice came out of him strange, flattened, like a voice on a bad comm. "All of it. The hangar. Letta. Those three troopers — with *your* blades. You were in a cell, you were in the *ice* —"

"I know what she did. I was here when she said it." Her montrals were trembling. She didn't look at Barriss. She looked only at him. "Look at your hand. Look at what it's doing."

"It's doing what someone should have done five months ago."

"This is how it started," Ahsoka said. "Do you understand me? This is exactly how it started. A woman held up off the ground with her hands at her throat, and someone standing where it looked like they weren't touching her at all. That's what she did to Letta. That's the very first thing she did. Everything else came after that." Her voice cracked down the middle and she let it. "You don't get to be the second person in this room who does it."

He felt her then — not her hand, *her* — reaching into him the way she had at dawn on Florrum, except that on Florrum she'd been glad. She wasn't glad now. Her presence pressed against his, bright and familiar and dear, and it was shaking, and what it was shaking with was fear.

Not of Barriss. Of him.

He had seen that once before, he realized, and hadn't let himself see it. Across a steel table on Narkis, with a cup crumpling between them. She had looked at him exactly like this.

"Skyguy," she said, very quietly. "Please."

It went into him like a blade between the plates.

His hand opened.

Barriss dropped. She hit the water on her knees and folded over and dragged in air with a sound like tearing cloth, coughing, one hand braced flat on the flooded floor. Alive. Her shoulders heaved. Nobody went to her.

Anakin looked down at his right hand. The mechanical fingers were perfectly steady inside the glove, the way they always were. It was the rest of him that was shaking — his knees, his breath, something in the back of his skull that felt like it had been struck and was still ringing.

Ahsoka's palm was still resting on his knuckles. She took it away slowly, as if she wasn't sure what would happen when she did.

Over by the south doors Ventress let out a long breath through her teeth.

"Well," she said. "Now I've seen everything. A Jedi with a temper." Her voice was its usual venom, but there was something under it that Anakin, if he'd been capable of noticing anything, might have called recognition. "Careful, Skywalker. For a moment there you looked like family."

He didn't answer her. High in the gantry, nothing moved.

* * *

Obi-Wan came in through the south loading doors with his cloak soaked black and his hand on his hilt, and stopped.

He took in the room the way he took in a battlefield — once, entirely, with no change at all in his face. Ventress. The red hilts. Ahsoka. Anakin. Barriss, on her knees, coughing into the water.

"Ah," he said. "I see we've all had the same idea about where to spend the evening."

Luminara Unduli came in behind him, and did not say anything at all.

She had found the crawlway. Anakin understood it from the grey dust on her sleeves, the cut-metal smell clinging to her robes; somewhere beneath the Temple's eastern foundation there was a hole, and an empty space behind it, and she had stood looking into it and then gone to find Obi-Wan. She had followed her own Padawan's trail down through the rain into the lower levels, knowing at every step what she would find at the end of it. And here it was.

She didn't go to Barriss. She stood just inside the doors with her hands folded in her sleeves, perfectly composed, and only her face gave her away — the stillness of it, like a pool after something heavy has gone under.

Barriss looked up and saw her master, and for the first time that night she bowed her head as though she couldn't hold it up.

Obi-Wan crossed the floor. He stopped a pace from Barriss and looked down at her, and Anakin watched his eyes go to her throat, to the marks there already darkening above the collar of her robe, and stay.

"What happened to your neck?" Obi-Wan said.

Barriss raised her head. Her voice came out in pieces. "I fell."

Obi-Wan looked at her for a long moment. Then he turned his head, not hurrying, and looked at Anakin.

Anakin said nothing. He was very good at saying nothing to Obi-Wan by now. He'd had five months of practice — the Soresu turn, the vents, the canyon. He found that this silence weighed more than all the others put together, and he held it anyway.

Obi-Wan opened his mouth.

Light slammed through the high windows.

Not lightning. Searchlights — three of them, white and hard, crossing and recrossing the flooded floor so that every shadow in the warehouse leapt up and spun. The whine of gunship repulsors filled the air, then the crash of boots on the iron stair outside the west door. An amplified voice rolled over everything, flat and familiar.

"*Ahsoka Tano. This is the Coruscant Guard. By order of the Office of the Supreme Chancellor, you are to lie face down with your hands visible. Any resistance will be met with lethal force.*"

The west door burst inward. Red armor poured through it — a dozen troopers, two dozen, rifles up, fanning out along the walls with the smooth hard precision of men who had done this many times. Fox came last, down the stair and into the light, his helmet turning as he took in the floor.

Anakin had his saber lit before he knew he'd reached for it. He stepped in front of Ahsoka, the blue blade low and humming between her and the rifles, and felt her go still behind him.

"Stand down, Commander."

"General Skywalker." Fox's voice through the helmet speaker didn't change. "Step away from the prisoner, sir."

"She's not resisting."

"My orders are to fire if she does."

"Your orders." Anakin heard his own voice go thin and bright, the way it went on a bridge when a ship was coming apart around him. "Whose orders? Who even told you she was here?"

"The Chancellor's office received an anonymous report an hour ago, General. Fugitive Tano sighted in the old munitions district, armed, possibly with Separatist assistance." Fox's rifle didn't move. "Authorization to fire came down with it."

The Chancellor would never — Anakin felt the thought begin and stop. Of course he wouldn't. Someone in that office had taken a tip at the third hour of the night and pushed it down the chain the way it was written. The Chancellor was asleep. The Chancellor would be horrified.

"She's not armed," he said.

There was a saber on Ahsoka's hip, the pirate's blue one, and every trooper in the room could see it.

What happened next happened in about four seconds. Ahsoka lifted her hands away from her body, open, slow. One of the troopers along the north wall — young, judging by how he held his rifle — saw the movement and flinched, and his finger closed. The bolt came screaming across the floor at the center of Ahsoka's chest, and Anakin's blade was already there; it cracked off the blue and buried itself in a pillar in a burst of sparks. Two more rifles came up. Fox roared, "*Hold fire!* Hold your *fire*!" Somewhere a trooper was shouting about a hostile at the south doors.

Anakin turned his head in time to see Ventress move.

She had both hands out. The hilt at the foot of the ramp leapt off the floor and smacked into her left palm; the one by the crane hook tore itself out of the water in a long silver spray and found her right. She didn't light them. She turned and went for the south doors in three long strides, low and fast, the searchlights sliding off her pale head —

And Obi-Wan was standing in the doorway.

Anakin saw her stop. Saw the two of them look at each other at a distance of perhaps an arm's length, Ventress with her sabers in her fists, Obi-Wan with his hand resting on his own hilt and not drawing it.

Then Obi-Wan stepped one pace to the side, turned his head, and looked thoughtfully up at the roof, as though he'd heard something interesting in the rafters.

Ventress went past him and out into the rain, and was gone.

Anakin stared. In the canyon on Florrum he had stood very much like that and watched Ahsoka go. He knew exactly what that looked like from the inside. He had not known it was a thing Obi-Wan could do.

"South door!" a trooper shouted. "Commander, one went out the south —"

"Let her go." Fox's helmet hadn't turned away from Ahsoka. "Tano's the order. Tano first."

"Commander Fox."

The voice came from behind him, not loud. Fox turned anyway. They all did.

Barriss had gotten to her feet.

She was standing in the middle of the floor where the searchlights crossed, soaked to the waist, one hand at her bruised throat. The light made her face very white around the markings. She took a step toward the rifles, and then another, until she was standing between the Guard and Ahsoka — between the Guard and Anakin's blade — and she lowered her hand.

"You're here for the person who killed CT-2261, CT-4408, and CT-9170," she said. "Outside the detention cells at the Military Operations Center. You're here for the person who bombed the Temple hangar. Who killed Letta Turmond in your own custody." Her voice was raw, but it carried; it carried all the way up into the dark of the gantry. "You've come to the right place. You've just aimed at the wrong person."

Fox didn't move. Behind the T of his visor, Anakin could feel him — a clone's presence, stubborn and plain as a wall — go very still.

"Identify yourself," Fox said.

"Barriss Offee. Padawan to Master Luminara Unduli of the Jedi Order." She didn't look at Luminara. "I supplied Letta Turmond with the nano-droids that her husband carried into the hangar without knowing what he carried. I bought them from a broker named Dalo Pesk in the Kesh Arcade. When Letta asked to speak to Ahsoka Tano, I was in the upper service passage above her cell, and I closed her throat from there so she couldn't say my name. Your three troopers I killed with Ahsoka's own sabers, which I took from the evidence locker. I left her the keycard. I sent her here, to this building, and I was waiting for her when she came. And on the Dovrin Line —" Her voice faltered for the first time. "On the Dovrin Line, the night before the Council voted, I was the one in the mask. I brought the platform down so that General Skywalker would have to choose between me and everyone standing on it. He chose them."

The rain hammered on the roof. Nobody in red armor moved.

"Ahsoka Tano has spent four months in the ice," Barriss said, "for things I did with my own hands. Write it down, Commander. Write that it was a Jedi."

Obi-Wan's voice came quiet and level out of the dark by the south doors. "Was there anyone else, Barriss?"

Anakin saw it — a flicker, there and gone. Her gaze went past Fox to the high windows, where the gunship's searchlights hung white in the rain. Then it came back.

"No one held my hand when I did any of it," she said. "Every choice I made was mine."

"Commander Fox." A different voice now, from above — clear, carrying, trained in a larger room than this one. Padmé was coming down the service stair from the gantry with one hand on the rail, the hood of her plain grey cloak thrown back. "Senator Amidala of Naboo. I'm counsel of record for Ahsoka Tano and I am a witness to everything said in this building tonight. Please tell your men to lower their weapons."

Fox's visor stayed on her through two slow breaths. Then he lifted one hand, and the rifles came down — not all the way, but down.

Anakin's eyes went up to the gantry behind her. No one else was on the stair. The platform above was empty, and at its far end a service-lane door was swinging shut on its closer, slow and soft, the way a door does a moment after someone has gone through it. She had come down alone. He noted it the way he'd note a loose panel on a ship he wasn't flying, and could not have said why it stayed with him.

Padmé reached the floor and walked straight across the water to him, and held out something small and dark between two fingers. A data chip — sealed, Anakin saw, the kind with a tamper strip across the seam. Military format. Her hand was not quite steady.

"A sealed, timestamped recording," she said. "Tamper-evident. Everything she confessed to Ahsoka before you arrived, and everything she has just said to you. I'll be presenting it to the tribunal myself. I'd like it logged in front of Master Kenobi and Master Unduli so there can be no question later of where it's been."

Fox took the chip. Obi-Wan came forward and looked at it and nodded once.

*Everything,* Anakin thought.

He looked up past her at the gantry, where something had moved while his hand was closed. Nothing moved there now; there was nothing up there at all anymore, only the black skeleton of the crane against the black of the roof. Fox was already sending two troopers up the service stair to sweep it.

Padmé turned from Fox, and for one instant her eyes met Anakin's across the flooded floor.

Then she looked away, and he understood that there was a piece of tonight that wasn't on that chip, and that she had decided that it wouldn't be, and that she had decided it for him.

"Gantry's clear, sir," a trooper called down. "Nobody up here."

"I was up there," Padmé said, without looking up. "Alone."

Ahsoka stepped out from behind Anakin's blade.

She walked through the water to where Barriss's saber lay where she'd dropped it, bent and picked it up, and shook it once. Then she carried it across the room to Luminara.

Luminara looked at the hilt as though she had never seen it before. Ahsoka held it out in both hands. After a moment Luminara took it the same way, both hands, like something that might break if she gripped it wrongly, and held it against her chest, and closed her eyes.

Neither of them said anything. Anakin thought there probably wasn't anything to say.

* * *

The rain was coming down sideways on the loading platform outside, driven by the gunships' repulsor wash. Two of them squatted at the platform's edge with their bay doors open and their interior lights glowing a sick red. Troopers stood in a loose ring at the edges with their rifles slung, watching the dark.

They put Barriss in binders first. Fox did it himself. She held her wrists out to him without being asked, and he closed the cuffs on them with a care that surprised Anakin, until he remembered the names she'd said. CT-2261. CT-4408. CT-9170. Fox's men. Maybe Fox had known them. Maybe a man could put binders on the person who'd killed his brothers more gently than anyone else, because he'd already imagined it so many times there was nothing left in the act.

"Military Operations Center," Fox said to the trooper beside him. "Detention level. Secure cell. Nobody in or out without my authorization."

Detention level. Anakin's stomach turned over. The same corridors. The same cells. Somewhere in that building there was a room where Letta Turmond had died against a wall, while Ahsoka stood three paces away and took the blame for it.

Luminara stepped forward. "I'll go with her."

"No, General." Fox didn't say it unkindly. "Not tonight."

Luminara stopped. She still held Barriss's saber in both hands against her robe. Barriss held her master's eyes, and then said something Anakin couldn't hear over the rain, and Luminara's face did not move at all, and Barriss was walked away up the ramp of the farther gunship.

Fox turned to Ahsoka. He had a second set of binders in his hand.

Ahsoka looked at them. Then she turned away from Fox, deliberately, and unclipped the pirate's battered blue saber from her belt and held it out — not to Fox. To Anakin.

He took it. It was heavier than hers had been.

Then she held out her wrists to him.

He stood there in the rain with her surrender held out in front of him and couldn't make his hands work. The fresh burn on her forearm. The thin pale ring at the base of her neck where the collar had been, still raw at the edges. He thought: *the last time someone held out their wrists to me like this, I let her go.* He thought: *I don't know how to do this.*

"Do it properly," Ahsoka said quietly. "They'll want to see it done properly. All of them."

Fox held out the binders. Anakin took them.

He closed them around her wrists as gently as he had ever done anything. Barriss had healed those wrists, he remembered, in a cell before the trial; Ahsoka had told Padmé, and Padmé had told him, and he had thought what a kindness. The cuffs locked with a small, final click.

"Dovrin," he said. It was all he could get out. "Snips. It was her. If I'd gone after her instead —"

"There'd be a Gran woman and her child under twenty stories of hab block," Ahsoka said. "And an Ithorian. And a lot of other people whose names you never learned." She looked at the binders, not at him. "You don't get to carry that one. It isn't yours."

"Then whose is it?"

She didn't answer. The rain ran off her montrals and down the stripes of her lekku. Over her shoulder he could see Obi-Wan standing at the edge of the platform, talking quietly with Luminara, and not quite looking at Anakin, in the particular way Obi-Wan had of not quite looking at a thing he could not stop thinking about. Every so often his gaze dropped — briefly, without seeming to — to Anakin's right hand.

Fox's comlink chimed. He lifted his wrist, listened, and turned to Anakin.

"Message from the Chancellor's office, General." The helmet tilted, as though Fox himself found the words slightly odd. "The Chancellor has been informed. He asks that you escort the prisoner to the Military Operations Center personally. And he asked me to tell you —" A beat. "That he never doubted you, sir. Not for a moment. And that he's grateful it was you who found her."

Something opened in Anakin's chest — warm, enormous, unbearable after the cold of the last hour. He had broken his word to the Chancellor in a canyon on Florrum, and the Chancellor had never known, and here was the Chancellor anyway, reaching down through the rain to tell him he'd done right. That it would be all right. That someone, at least, still saw him clearly.

He felt his throat go tight. "Tell him —" He stopped. "Tell him thank you."

Beside him, Ahsoka had gone completely still. Not tense. Just still, the way she'd stood at the sentencing when the word *death* fell on her, before the word *life* came after it. She was looking at Fox's comlink.

On the ramp of the farther gunship, Barriss had stopped too.

The trooper at her elbow tugged at her arm. She didn't move. She had heard it — Anakin saw that she had heard it — and she was looking back over her shoulder through the rain, not at Anakin, but at Ahsoka. Her face, in the red light of the bay, was the face of someone watching a door close that she had been meaning to walk through.

"Ahsoka," Barriss called. Her ruined voice barely carried. "Come and see me. Before they put me in front of anyone."

Ahsoka's head came up.

"There's one more thing that's true," Barriss said. "I couldn't say it in there."

The trooper pulled her inside. The bay door ground shut on her, and the gunship lifted, and swung away into the rain with its searchlights dimming until there was only the red glow of its engines climbing between the towers, and then not even that.

"What did she mean?" Anakin said.

Ahsoka watched the place where the lights had been. She didn't answer him. And for the first time since a fourteen-year-old with a shoto and no manners had walked down a shuttle ramp on Christophsis and announced herself as his Padawan, Anakin looked at her face and could not read a single thing on it.

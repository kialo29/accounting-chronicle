# Chapter 20: The Dead Man

The dead man wanted to meet at the bottom of the hole he'd died in.

Rex read the second ping three times to be sure. It had come in at 0210 on the same false channel as the first: a Coruscant Guard power-cell fault, a class of fault that hadn't existed for two years, reported from a substation grid that didn't exist either. Run through the old check-in cipher, the grid became a place, and the place became a joke only two men in the galaxy would get. *Outflow chamber, Seventh Refinery. Where the shaft comes out. 0300 tomorrow. Bring a medkit and don't bring anybody.* Signed the same way as before. 5554. Still hurt.

He sat on the edge of his bunk aboard the *Resolute* with the cracked cadet datapad on his knee and the deck humming under his boots, and he did not play the video again. Eleven times was enough. He knew it the way he knew his own rifle: forty seconds, a white room on Kamino, a medical droid's flat voice, Fives strapped to a table and awake, talking through his teeth while something no bigger than a fingernail was lifted out of his skull. *Rex. If they tell you I'm crazy, look at this first.* The rest of what Fives had sent was scans and embryo charts and columns of Kaminoan medical shorthand that might as well have been written in Huttese. Kix could read it. Rex couldn't. And he hadn't decided yet whether he had the right to put that weight on Kix.

The *Resolute* should have been in hyperspace eighteen hours ago.

She was still sitting in her cradle at the fleet yards because Major Teva Rusk's team had arrived at dawn with a crate the Judiciary refused to let anyone else touch, and the Judiciary's paperwork had to be stamped by the Admiralty, and the Admiralty's stamp had to be countersigned by an office that answered to Admiral Tarkin, and Admiral Tarkin, it turned out, was at a dinner. Rex had watched General Skywalker take that news in the hangar with his jaw locked and his mechanical hand closing slowly on nothing. Then the General had turned around and walked away without a word, which was worse than shouting.

Rex wasn't sorry about the delay. He was ashamed of not being sorry, and he decided he could live with both.

He was pulling on the plain brown work jacket he kept at the bottom of his locker — no rank, no colors, the kind of thing a clone wore on a maintenance detail — when the door chimed.

He shoved the datapad under his pillow. "Open."

General Skywalker stood in the doorway in his dark tunic, no cloak, eyes shot through with red. He looked at the jacket. "Going somewhere, Captain?"

"Supply depot on the lower ring, sir. The med stores came up short. Kix wants more fever shots for the boys before Narkis." It wasn't even entirely a lie. Rex had signed for twelve injectors that afternoon. One of them was already in his jacket.

"At two in the morning."

"Depot clerks are friendlier at two in the morning. They want you gone."

That got the corner of the General's mouth, barely. He leaned on the frame. "Tarkin's office says we'll have clearance by noon. Noon. She could be halfway to Serenno by noon."

"If she was going to Serenno, sir."

"I know she's not going to Serenno." It came out too fast. The General looked at the floor and visibly put something away. "Windu thinks she is. The Holonet thinks she is. Rusk has a box with a collar in it, and she's going to walk up to Ahsoka holding it like a gift." The metal fingers flexed. "I keep trying to sleep and I keep seeing that refinery. Fives going over the edge. I was right there, Rex. I was right there and I let Fox—" He stopped. "Don't tell me that's not what happened."

Rex had spent nineteen days not telling him anything, and he found it was a muscle like any other. It got stronger with use. It also ached.

"It happened fast, sir," he said.

"Everything does, lately." The General straightened off the frame. "The Chancellor wants me to bring her to him. Not to Tarkin. Not to the Council. To him. He says that's the only way he can protect her." He said it like a man holding a rope out over a drop, wanting someone to tell him it would hold. "That's something, isn't it?"

Rex thought of the video. A white room. A Chancellor who'd taken Fives's chip and kept it.

"It's something," he said.

The General nodded as if that had been an answer, and looked at him a moment longer than he needed to. "Get your fever shots. Don't let the clerk talk you into the cheap ones."

When the door slid shut, Rex let out a breath he'd been holding since the chime.

He was reaching for the datapad again when his wrist comm gave a single, odd click. Not the Guard channel. Not the fleet net. Something older.

He knew the shape of it before he'd finished decoding it, the way you know a voice in the dark. He had written that code himself on a shipping crate on Christophsis, with a fourteen-year-old Togruta rolling her eyes at him from the other side, and he had made her repeat it until she could do it with her eyes shut. *Officer cut off from unit. Position follows. Condition: able to fight. Not alone.* The position was a relay mark in the Outer Rim, and the relay mark sat in the Florrum system, which was pirate country and nowhere near Serenno.

The signal had come up the old 501st field band, the one nobody had used since they'd changed the codes after Umbara. Nobody watched it anymore. Nobody but him.

Rex sat on the bunk with the jacket half on and looked at the little line of characters until his eyes stung.

Then he copied it to the cracked datapad, scrubbed it from his comm's buffer, and went down to the lower levels to see a dead man.

* * *

The Seventh Refinery had been abandoned for longer than Rex had been alive, which wasn't saying much, and it showed every year of it.

He came in from the south side, away from the street where Fives had run that night, through a gap in a fence somebody had cut a long time ago and nobody had bothered to mend. Rain came down the inside of the great dead stacks in sheets. Everything smelled of rust and old sulfur and the sweet rot of standing water. Two blocks east, past a wall of shuttered hab blocks, stood the warehouse where all of this had started for Commander Tano. He didn't look that way. He didn't have to. He could feel it there the way you feel a wound under a bandage.

The outflow chamber was at the bottom of a long ramp of cracked ferrocrete. The drainage shaft emptied into it from far above through a mouth as wide as a gunship, and the water came out of that mouth in a steady grey curtain and roared into a channel that ran off into the dark. Rex stood at the edge of the light from his hand lamp and listened to it. He thought of how long a man would fall before he hit that water. He thought of whether he'd want to be awake for it.

"You're late," said a voice behind him.

Rex didn't turn around fast. He'd had too many years of not turning around fast. He turned slowly, lamp held low, and there he was, sitting on a rusted valve housing with his back to the wall like a man who had picked that wall very carefully.

Fives.

He was thin. That was the first thing. Not lean the way they'd all been lean, but scraped down, the bones of his face standing out under skin that had gone grey and slick with fever. His hair had grown out into a dark uneven scrub. He wore a laborer's coat two sizes too large, stained along one side, and his left arm was tucked inside it against his chest. The five inked at his temple was still there. So was the grin, sort of. It looked like it hurt him to do it.

"It's two minutes past," Rex said. His voice did something on the last word.

"Two minutes is late. You used to make us run laps for two minutes."

"You used to deserve it."

Fives laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough went on long enough that Rex crossed the space between them without deciding to and caught him by the good shoulder before he slid off the housing. Fives's body under the coat was hot as a cook plate.

"Easy," Rex said. "Easy. Sit."

"I'm sitting."

"Sit harder."

He set the lamp on the ground, pulled open the medkit and took out the first fever injector. Fives watched him with eyes that were too bright. When Rex pressed the injector to his neck, Fives didn't flinch. He just closed his eyes and let his head knock back against the wall.

"I wasn't sure," he said. "When I sent the first ping. I wasn't sure you'd come. Or that you'd come alone."

"I almost didn't."

"Which part?"

Rex didn't answer that. He pulled back the coat. Underneath, Fives had packed the shoulder wound with Coruscant Guard field dressings — stolen, from the look of the stenciled packets — and bound them in place with what had once been a shirt. The dressings were brown and stiff. The smell, when Rex peeled the edge back, made him set his jaw.

"How long since you changed these?"

"Three days. Four. I ran out." Fives's eyes stayed shut. "Lifted them off a Guard patrol kit two nights after. Along with the comlink. Figured they owed me."

"You're lucky you've still got the arm."

"I'm lucky I've still got the head." Fives opened one eye. "You saw me go. Didn't you."

"I saw you go."

"Fox told them I was dead."

"Fox told them your body was unrecoverable." Rex cut away the old binding and started cleaning, which gave his hands something to do. "Which, as far as he knew, was true. Nobody went down after you. I was ordered back from the edge."

"And you went."

"I went."

Fives didn't say anything to that. He didn't need to. The water roared on behind them.

"Tell me how," Rex said.

So Fives told him, in pieces, between the sting of the antiseptic and the slap of the bacta patch going on. He hadn't fallen all the way, not at first. There were rungs inside the shaft, a maintenance ladder half rusted off the wall, and he'd hit one and hung there with his good hand for a while — he didn't know how long — before his grip went. After that he didn't remember. He'd come to on a sludge shelf just above where the water came out, in the dark, half in the current, with his shoulder on fire and his helmet gone. It had taken him most of a day to crawl up to where they were sitting now. He'd lived since on what he could take: water off the stacks, ration bars out of a vending cabinet two levels up that took a kick in the right place, a blanket off a line. He'd moved every night. He'd listened to the Guard channel on the stolen comlink until the battery went low, and then he'd only listened once a day.

"I heard them say my number," he said. "On the Guard channel. Logging it closed. CT-5555, deceased, recovery not attempted." He gave that grin again. It was even worse this time. "That's a strange thing, hearing yourself filed."

Rex pressed the last patch down and held it with the heel of his hand. "Ahsoka knows what that's like."

Fives went very still.

"Tell me," Rex said quietly, "the rest."

Fives told him the rest.

He told it badly, out of order, with the fever going down and his hands starting to shake as it went, and Rex let him. He didn't interrupt. He'd learned in the field that when a man came back from something, the worst thing you could do was put the questions in the order you wanted them.

The chips were in all of them. Fives said it twice, as if he didn't trust it to stay said. Every clone, from the embryo up — grown in, not put in, the way a bone was grown in. AZI-3 had scanned Tup and found it, and scanned Fives and found it, and in the Kaminoan files the droid had pulled there were records showing it on the earliest charts. Lama Su called it a precaution. Nala Se called it nothing at all. The official story on Kamino was a parasite, and then a virus, and Rex said, "A virus from Ringo Vinda. That's what the report said." And Fives laughed until he coughed again.

"It's not a virus," Fives said. "It's not a tumor. It's a switch, Rex. AZI-3 showed me the structure. It's built to be turned on. And when Tup's switched on, he did what it told him. He kept saying it — good soldiers follow orders. You heard him. Over and over, like a recording. He didn't hate her. He didn't hate anyone. Something reached in and moved his hand."

Rex heard Tiplee asking him on the *Resolute* — *Did he hate her?* — and his own silence in answer.

"So there's an order," Rex said.

"There's an order." Fives's good hand closed on his knee. "I don't know what it is. I don't know when. Tup's went off early — something wrong with his, AZI-3 thought. The rest of ours are waiting. Yours. Mine, before they cut it out. Jesse's. Kix's. Every man in the 501st and every man in every legion in the Republic. Sitting in our heads, waiting for somebody to say the right words."

"Who?"

"I don't know." Fives looked at him. "I know who took my chip, though. I know whose desk it went to. I was in the room. I was drugged out of my head and I watched him hold it up to the light like a jewel and put it in his pocket. And then I was the man who'd attacked the Chancellor."

The water roared. A loose sheet of metal somewhere high up the stacks banged in the wind, and banged again.

Rex realized he'd sat down on the wet ferrocrete at some point. He didn't remember doing it.

He waited for the part of himself that had been built to do this — to weigh a report, find the hole in it, send it back up the chain — to tell him Fives was sick. Fever, a fall, a man alone in the dark for nineteen days hearing his own death on the comm. Anyone would come out of that seeing switches in the walls. That was the reasonable thing. That was what the reports would say. That was what Fox would say, and the Judge Advocate, and Kamino, and the Chancellor in his warm red room.

And he'd heard reasonable before. He'd stood in a tribunal gallery and listened to reasonable for two days, a whole chamber of it, footage and forensics and an admiral's level voice, and every piece of it had been reasonable, and at the end of it a girl he'd have died for was led away to a moon made of ice. He had stood there in his armor. He had not said a word. The silence had felt like discipline at the time.

He wasn't going to stand in that gallery twice.

"I believe you," Rex said.

Fives blinked. It was such a small thing, and his whole face changed with it. He looked down quickly.

"Just like that," he said to the floor.

"Not just like that. I watched your video eleven times."

"Eleven."

"I stopped counting after eleven. That's not the same as stopping." Rex rubbed his face. "I can't read the rest of it. The files. The charts. I need Kix for that."

"Then get Kix."

"Not yet."

"Rex—"

"Not yet." He said it in his captain's voice and was sorry the moment it came out, and didn't take it back. "Kix is the best medic in this army, and he's also the man who's going to have to look every one of the boys in the eye afterward knowing what's in their heads. When I put that on him, it's going to be because I've got something for him to do about it. Not just something to carry."

Fives stared at him. Then his gaze went, slowly and deliberately, to the side of Rex's head. Just above the ear.

"You've got one," he said. "Right now. You know that."

"I know that."

"And you're going to walk back up there and stand next to General Skywalker with it."

"Yes."

"Rex, if that order comes while you're—"

"Then you'll have been right, and I'll have been a fool, and you can say so at my funeral." Rex held his eyes. "But if a captain in the 501st asks the medics for a brain scan two weeks after one of his men was declared a traitor and another was declared a lunatic, how long do you think it takes before somebody in an office notices? If I start acting different, they'll look. Right now they're not looking at me. That's the only thing I've got worth anything."

Fives's jaw worked. For a moment Rex saw him at the shuttle hatch on the *Resolute*, a lifetime ago, promising he wouldn't go quietly. "You knocked Fox's arm," he said. "I saw that. Right before I went. You knocked his arm."

"I know what I did."

"Does the General?"

"He saw it. He left it out of his report." Rex paused. "So did Fox."

Fives made a sound that might have been the start of a laugh. "Everybody's lying for everybody."

"Seems that way."

"Does Skywalker know I'm alive?"

"No."

"Are you going to tell him?"

Rex thought of the General in his doorway with his red eyes. *The Chancellor wants me to bring her to him.* He thought of the metal hand closing on nothing.

"No," he said. "Not now. Maybe not for a long time."

Fives studied him a long while. Then he nodded, once, as if Rex had passed a test he hadn't known he was taking.

Rex reached into his jacket and took out the cracked datapad, and turned it so the screen faced Fives.

"There's something else," he said.

Fives read the code twice. Rex watched him do it. The second time, his lips moved.

"That's Christophsis," Fives said. "That's the cut-off code. That's — Rex, that's *her*. She's out?"

"She's out."

So Rex told him that too: the raid on Narkis, the dead guards, Cad Bane, the Holonet bulletin that had the Temple bomber joining Count Dooku, the Council's orders, Windu's authorization of lethal force. Fives listened with his mouth open, then shut, then tight.

"Lethal force," he said.

"In the field. If necessary."

"And Skywalker's on it."

"The General's on it. General Kenobi. Master Unduli and her Padawan. And a Judiciary major who's bringing a new collar along for the trip, in case anybody forgets what she's for." Rex tapped the screen. "That came in two hours ago on the old band. Position's Florrum. She says she can fight, and she's not alone."

"Who's with her?"

"Don't know. Code doesn't have a box for it."

Fives looked at the code a while longer. Then he looked up, and Rex already knew what he was going to say, because it was what Rex had been thinking since the click on his wrist.

"I'm going to her."

"You can barely sit up."

"I'm sitting up fine. I'm sitting up harder, even." Fives leaned forward, and the effort of it showed. "Think about it. Who else in this galaxy is going to listen to me? Kix? You just told me you won't put it on him. The General? He's carrying the Chancellor's messages around like a holy relic. The Council?" His voice went harsh. "The Council heard *her* out and handed her to Tarkin. What do you think they'll do with a dead clone who says the Chancellor has a pocket full of switches?"

"I know."

"She'll believe me. She's the only one who will. She's been where I am." Fives's hand was shaking again, but not from the fever now. "And whoever she's with, she needs somebody at her back who isn't going to sell her the first time a better price comes along. Pirate country, Rex. You know what Florrum's like."

"I know what Florrum's like." Rex was quiet for a breath. "I'd thought of it."

"Then why are you looking at me like that?"

"Because I'd thought of it before you said it," Rex said, "and I don't like that I'm sending you."

Fives sat back against the wall. "You're not sending me. I'm going. You're just paying for it."

"Am I."

"You've got three years of stipend sitting on a chip you've never spent on anything but caf."

That was true, and Rex was annoyed that Fives remembered it.

"And you?" Fives said. "What do you do?"

"Stay." Rex said it plainly. "I stay inside. I stand where they've put me. I keep the General between his worst idea and his next one if I can. I watch Rusk. I watch what comes down the chain about Florrum before it gets there. If anyone gets close to either of you, I want to know before they do." He met Fives's eyes. "She gave me an order before they sent her to Narkis. Look after him. I haven't been relieved of it."

"And what about us? Who's looking after us?"

"I am," Rex said. "From the inside. It's the only side I've got."

Fives was quiet for a long moment. Then he held out his good hand.

Rex took it, and they stayed like that, two men with the same face in a drainage chamber under the city, until the banging metal high above went still.

* * *

Berth Nine in the Undercroft smelled of coolant and fried tubers and was busier at four in the morning than most of the upper city was at noon.

Rex had read General Skywalker's report on the place weeks ago — the Pantoran berth-master, Ventress's freighter, the long nasty message she'd left — and he had memorized the layout the way he memorized anything he might someday have to fight through. He hadn't expected to walk into it in a work jacket with Fives leaning on his shoulder under a pulled-down cap, coughing into his collar, playing a deckhand with a hangover. It was not a hard part for Fives to play.

The Pantoran berth-master looked at Rex's face, and then at Fives's face, and then at Rex's face again. Her expression didn't change. In the Undercroft, Rex suspected, two men who looked alike were not the strangest thing that had walked in before breakfast.

"Outer Rim," Rex said. "Florrum, or close enough to walk."

"Nobody walks on Florrum. They get carried." She tapped her datapad. "Gatch is going out in two hours. Scrap hauler, the *Gravel Saint*. He sells to the pirates on the Florrum markets every few weeks. Two stops on the way, Ord Torvin and a fuel dump off the Lanteeb lane. Two days, give or take what the engine feels like."

"Is he careful?"

"He's quiet. Out here that's better." She looked at Fives, swaying slightly. "He won't take a corpse."

"He's not a corpse," Rex said. "He's just been dead a while."

That earned him the faintest twitch from the Pantoran, which he chose to count as a smile.

Gatch turned out to be an old Weequay with a ruined left ear and hands like knotted rope, who listened to the berth-master, looked at Fives, looked at the credit chip in Rex's hand, and named a price that took most of three years of stipend in a single bite. Rex paid it without arguing. Gatch counted it twice, then jerked his chin at a space between two scrap bins in the *Gravel Saint*'s hold, where someone had laid an old blast blanket over a stack of sheet metal.

"Bunk," Gatch said. That was the whole conversation.

Rex walked Fives up the ramp and got him down onto the blanket. He tucked the medkit under Fives's good arm: six more fever shots, the rest of the bacta, a roll of clean binding, a handful of ration bars. Fives had the stolen Guard comlink in his coat. Rex had spent ten minutes in the Undercroft market buying a battery for it from a Chadra-Fan who sold, as far as Rex could tell, nothing that hadn't been stolen.

"Comlink's got charge now. Don't waste it on the Guard channel." Rex crouched beside him. "I'm sending her a reply from the public relay on the way out, not from the *Resolute*. Old band. It'll say hold position, contact arriving in two days, friendly. When you make planetfall, you raise her on the same band with the 501st ident. Not before."

"And when she asks how she knows I'm friendly?"

Rex thought about it. Then, very nearly, he smiled.

"Tell her Rex says her sense of direction is still terrible."

Fives grinned up at him, and for a second the fever and the bones and the cap were gone and it was just Fives, the way he'd been on Kamino in their first year, before there'd been anything to be afraid of. "She's going to hate that."

"She'll know it's me."

The last thing out of Rex's kit bag was wrapped in a shirt. He'd found it after the fall, wedged against the grating at the lip of the refinery shaft where it had stopped rolling, and he'd carried it since without deciding why. Fives unwrapped it one-handed and turned it over, and his thumb found the dent over the brow before his eyes did. "You never fixed it," Fives said, and Rex said there hadn't been time, which was true and not the reason. "Keep it on you," he said. "You'll want something to talk to out there."

The engines coughed somewhere forward. Gatch shouted something in his own language that didn't sound like a farewell. Rex stood.

"Fives."

"Captain."

"Don't let her do anything stupid."

"Have you met her?"

"That's why I said it."

He went down the ramp and didn't look back, because a man in a work jacket at a smuggler's berth who looked back was a man someone would remember. He heard the ramp come up behind him. He heard the old hauler lift out of its cradle with a grinding complaint, and then the roar of it going up the shaft, and then nothing but the Undercroft again, coolant and tubers and voices.

At a public relay booth three levels up, under a flickering advertisement for Rodian fight-holos, he punched in the old 501st band and keyed the reply by hand. *Hold position. Go to ground. Contact arriving two days. Friendly.* He hesitated over the last sequence, the one that meant *acknowledged, Commander*, and then sent it with it.

Then he wiped the booth's buffer, paid the booth for nothing, and went to find twelve fever shots' worth of an excuse.

* * *

The *Resolute* got her clearance at 1140. By 1200 she was climbing out of the fleet yards through a sky the color of old durasteel, and Rex was standing on the forward observation deck in full armor, helmet under his arm, the cracked datapad buttoned into the pouch at his belt where it had always been.

General Skywalker came up beside him without a sound. He'd slept, maybe, an hour. It didn't look like it had helped.

"Get your fever shots?"

"Twelve, sir. Clerk tried to give me the cheap ones."

"You didn't let him."

"No, sir."

They watched Coruscant fall away under them, the towers turning to a gold scatter, the scatter to a curve. Rex thought of a scrap hauler two hours ahead of them on a very different lane, and a man asleep between two bins in its hold, and held his face still.

"Rex." The General didn't look at him. His voice was odd. Quiet. Not the brittle tone he'd been using for weeks. "Something happened last night."

Rex's heart did one hard, cold thing. "Sir?"

"About two in the morning. After I left you." The General's mechanical hand was resting on the rail, and the fingers were moving slightly, the way they did when he was listening for something. "I was lying there. Not sleeping. And for a second — less than a second — I *felt* her."

Rex said nothing. He was very good at saying nothing now.

"Not like on Narkis. On Narkis there was just — nothing. A wall. That collar. I sat across a table from her and it was like she wasn't in the room." The General finally turned his head. His eyes were bright and wet and fierce. "But last night it was like a door opening a long way off. Somebody turning on a light in a house across a canyon. She's not wearing it anymore, Rex. That collar's off. She's out there and she's *herself* again."

"Do you know where?"

"No. Not yet. It was too far, too fast." His hand closed slowly on the rail, and the durasteel creaked under it. "But I felt her once. I'll feel her again. And the next time I do, I'm not going to need Tarkin, or Rusk, or anybody's clearance."

He pushed off the rail and walked toward the lift, and called back over his shoulder, almost cheerful, the way he'd sounded in the old days before Rex had ever learned the word *tribunal*.

"Set a course for Narkis, Captain. We'll start where they took her. But I don't think we'll be there long."

The lift doors closed on him.

Rex stood alone at the glass, with Ahsoka's position buttoned against his hip and Fives's two-day head start already burning away behind them, and understood that he was no longer racing Tarkin, or Rusk, or the Council.

He was racing the General.

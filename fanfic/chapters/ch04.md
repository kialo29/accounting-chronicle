# Chapter 4: The Tribunal

Someone had placed the evidence table so close to the defendant's chair that Ahsoka could have reached out and touched her own lightsabers. Padmé noticed it the moment she came through the doors, and she knew it was no accident. The two hilts lay in a clear case on grey cloth, each with a small white tag wired to its pommel, #417 and #418. Ahsoka sat a little more than an arm's length away from them with her bound hands in her lap. She was not looking at them, and she was taking great care not to.

The tribunal chamber sat on the Senate side of the complex, a round room built for committee hearings and never meant for this. The seats rose in tiers toward a ceiling of smoked transparisteel, and the morning light came through it the color of weak caf. On the high bench at the far end sat Chancellor Palpatine, with a judiciary clerk on either side of him. Below him, along a curved desk, sat the senators who would decide: Riyo Chuchi, her blue face very still; Mon Mothma in white; Bail Organa, who had nodded to Padmé in the corridor and then looked away as if the nod had cost him something; and six others whose votes Padmé had spent two sleepless nights trying to count.

Above them, the gallery was full.

She saw Jedi robes in a cluster on the left: Master Luminara Unduli and her Padawan, two Knights Padmé didn't know, and Plo Koon standing at the back of the cluster because he would not sit. On the right sat the families of the hangar dead, in the deep blue of Coruscanti mourning. And everywhere else were faces she recognized from committee rooms and receptions, senators and aides and Holonet people, here for the same reason crowds gathered at a speeder wreck.

Padmé set her datapad down at the defense table and took the seat beside Ahsoka.

"Good morning," she said.

"Is it?" Ahsoka said. Then, more quietly: "Sorry. Good morning, Senator."

Two days ago her wrists had been raw from the binders, rubbed open in two places. Padmé looked now and saw clean orange skin under the metal. Somebody had finally sent a medic. It was a small decency, and she was angry at how grateful it made her.

At the prosecution table, Admiral Tarkin squared a stack of datapads with his fingertips and did not look up.

* * *

Tarkin built his case the way an engineer built a bridge: one load-bearing piece at a time, no ornament, nothing that would not hold weight.

He began with the hangar. He did not dwell on it, which was cleverer than dwelling on it would have been. He read the names of the six Jedi who had died, then the number of clones, then the number of civilian workers, and let the blue rows in the gallery do the rest. Then he moved to Letta Turmond.

"The Republic's case does not depend on the defendant's character, or her reputation, or the affection in which she is held by her former colleagues," he said. "It depends on what the record shows. I will now show the record."

The lights dimmed. A holo rose above the floor of the chamber, grey and slightly grainy, the angle high in the corner of a cell. Padmé had watched it forty times. It did not get better.

Letta Turmond sat on a bench. Ahsoka stood in front of her. There was no sound. Letta leaned forward, her mouth moving, her hands opening as if she were offering something. Then her hands went to her throat. Her body lifted against the wall. Her heels kicked twice. On the holo, Ahsoka stepped toward her, one arm out, and from that angle, with no sound, the reaching hand looked like the cause of it.

Letta slid down the wall and was still.

Someone in the gallery made a sound like a breath going the wrong way. Padmé did not turn to see who.

"The prisoner had asked to speak to the defendant alone," Tarkin said, as the lights came back. "She was alone with her. No other person entered that room. The prisoner died by strangulation, with no mark of a hand on her. I am told by the Jedi themselves that only one sort of person can do that."

Padmé stood. "Objection. The Admiral is testifying to Force technique. He is not qualified, and no Jedi has been called to support it."

"Sustained," said Palpatine mildly, from the high bench. "Admiral, I am sure the tribunal is aware of the general capacities of the Jedi. Let us keep to what the record shows, as you so rightly said."

"Of course, Chancellor."

It was a small win and it cost Tarkin nothing. Padmé sat down. Beside her, Ahsoka had gone very still, her lekku drawn in tight against her shoulders.

Next came the three troopers. Tarkin called a forensics technician from the Judiciary laboratory, a narrow Bith named Sabo Kell who spoke softly and kept his long hands folded on the witness rail. Kell described the wounds: cauterized, narrow, two different blade widths. He described the residue profile of a kyber-focused plasma blade. He compared it with test cuts made with items #417 and #418.

"Consistent in every respect," Kell said.

"Thank you. The names, please, for the record."

Kell consulted his datapad. "CT-2261. CT-4408. CT-9170."

Padmé watched Ahsoka's mouth move very slightly with each number. She was not praying. Jedi didn't, exactly. She was counting along.

Commander Fox came after him, helmet under his arm, and gave his account of the escape in the flat voice of a man reading a report he had already filed. The cell door open. The defendant gone. The three bodies in the corridor. The pursuit into the lower levels. The sighting of the defendant in the company of Asajj Ventress, known Separatist assassin. The second capture, in a disused munitions warehouse, where the defendant had been found standing in the middle of crates that held the same nano-droids used in the hangar bombing.

"Standing," Tarkin repeated.

"Standing, sir. Weapon drawn. One of the crates was open."

"Was anyone else present?"

Fox hesitated, which Padmé had not expected. "Not when we arrived, sir."

"Thank you, Commander."

On cross, Padmé asked Fox one question. "What weapon did the defendant have drawn, Commander? Her own lightsabers?"

"No, ma'am. Her own sabers were already in evidence. She had a—" He paused. "A stun baton. One of ours."

"A stun baton. Against a stockpile of explosive droids." She let the room hear it. "Thank you, Commander."

It was something. It was not enough, and she knew it, and so did Tarkin, who was already rising.

"The Republic calls General Anakin Skywalker."

* * *

He came down the aisle from the back of the chamber in his dark robes, and Padmé had to put her hands flat on the table.

He looked like a man who had not slept in four days, which she knew to be true. There was a healing scrape along his jaw. His boots were clean, because he would have cleaned them, but his robes had the faint grey bloom of lower-level damp in the hem. He took the witness stand without looking at the defense table. That was for Ahsoka's sake, Padmé thought, and possibly for hers.

Tarkin was courteous to him. That was the worst part.

"General, on the night before the Jedi Council voted to expel the defendant, you were in the old munitions district of the lower levels. Why?"

"Looking for Ventress."

"You had reason to believe she knew something."

"I had reason to believe she was the only person in the galaxy who could tell us what happened in that warehouse," Anakin said. "So yes."

"And you found her."

"On a gallery over the Dovrin Line platform. She'd been hurt. Someone had beaten her and taken her lightsabers."

"Someone," Tarkin said. "Describe this someone."

Anakin's jaw moved. "Cloaked. A white mask with narrow eyeholes. Fast. Two curved lightsabers, red."

"Two curved red lightsabers," Tarkin said, and turned so that the senators' bench could see his face. "General, are you familiar with the weapons Asajj Ventress is known to carry?"

"Yes."

"Describe them."

"Two curved hilts," Anakin said. "Red blades."

"Thank you."

"She didn't have them," Anakin said. "That's the point. Somebody took them from her. She had a bruise from her temple to here—" he touched his cheekbone with his gloved hand— "and she was scared, Admiral. I've fought Ventress. I've never seen her scared."

"You have testified that the masked figure threw Ventress from a ledge," Tarkin said. "Partners in crime frequently fall out, General. Sometimes literally. You found an assassin who had lost her weapons to someone who knew exactly where to find them, and who then removed her before she could speak. Is that not also the pattern of an accomplice tidying up?"

"Objection," Padmé said. "Speculation."

"I'll withdraw it," said Tarkin, who had already said it. "General. When the figure attacked, did you recognize anything about how it fought? Any style, any training?"

The question was so smooth it took Padmé a moment to understand its danger. If Anakin said yes, if he said it fought like a Jedi, then Ahsoka had a Jedi accomplice, and the case got worse, not better.

Anakin's right hand closed on the rail of the witness stand. The leather of his glove creaked in the quiet. Padmé knew that hand. She had seen it close like that over a dinner table and on a balcony rail and once, a long time ago, around the edge of a sink on Naboo.

"No," Anakin said. "It was dark. It was fast."

Tarkin inclined his head. "Then let us turn to what you did see. The figure used nano-droids on a support pylon. A residential structure began to fall. You chose to hold the structure rather than pursue."

"There were people under it."

"Commendable," said Tarkin, and it sounded like a word he had looked up. "No one died. I am sure the families are grateful. I note only that the one person in the galaxy you say could have cleared the defendant walked away from that platform because you let them."

The room went quiet in a different way.

Padmé was on her feet. "Chancellor, that is not a question."

"No, it isn't," said Palpatine gently. "Admiral, the General's conduct that night is not on trial. I will not have it made to seem so." He turned to Anakin with something close to tenderness. "General Skywalker, you saved a great many lives. The tribunal notes it."

Anakin looked up at the high bench. For an instant his face was naked, a boy's face, grateful and ashamed at once. Then it closed.

"Thank you, Chancellor," he said.

On cross, Padmé had prepared nine questions. She asked three.

"General, where was the defendant on the night you met this masked figure on the Dovrin platform?"

"In a cell," Anakin said. "At the Center for Military Operations. Under guard."

"So whoever wore that mask and carried those weapons was not the defendant."

"No."

"And whoever attacked Ventress that night knew where to find her, and was willing to bring down a twenty-story building to escape. While the defendant sat behind a locked door."

"Yes."

"Thank you, General. That's all."

She sat. It was the strongest point she had: there was someone else. Someone else had been in that warehouse, someone else was still out there, and the girl at the defense table could not possibly have been on that platform.

She watched it land on the senators' bench. She watched Bail Organa write something down. And then she watched Tarkin rise and spend a single sentence to take the point away.

"The Republic has never disputed that the defendant had help," he said. "The only question is whether she directed it."

* * *

The second morning, Ahsoka testified.

She had asked to. Padmé had argued against it, the night before, through the hum of a detention-cell field, and lost. "If I don't speak," Ahsoka had said, "they'll decide what I would have said. I'd rather they hear it wrong from me."

She took the stand in grey detention clothes with her binders still on, because Tarkin had requested it and Palpatine had regretfully allowed it. She sat straight. Without the Padawan beads, the place on her montral where the chain had hung looked bare, and Padmé saw more than one senator's eyes go there and come away.

Padmé took her through it gently, from the beginning. The hangar, and the investigation. Letta Turmond asking for her by name. Fox taking her comlink and her sabers at the door.

"What happened in that room?"

"She told me a Jedi was behind it," Ahsoka said. "She was going to tell me who. And then she couldn't breathe. I tried to get to her. I couldn't stop it. I couldn't see who was doing it, I couldn't feel where it came from, and she died in front of me." Her voice did not shake. Her hands, in the binders, had locked together until the knuckles paled. "I've watched people die in the war. Not like that. Not a prisoner. Not someone who was asking me for help."

She told them about the cell, the keycard she had thought came from a friend, the bodies of the three troopers in the corridor. "I knew what it would look like," she said. "I ran because I knew what it would look like. That's the truth. I'm not proud of it."

"Why did you go to the warehouse?"

"Because someone told me that was where the nano-droids came from. I thought if I could find the source, I could find who'd done this."

Padmé had come to the place she had been dreading. She had told Ahsoka she would ask it. Ahsoka had told her it would do no good. Padmé had decided the tribunal should at least hear her refuse; there was a kind of evidence in that, too, if the right senators were listening.

"Ahsoka," she said. "Who told you where the warehouse was?"

The pause went on long enough that someone in the press seats shifted.

"A friend," Ahsoka said.

"Can you tell the tribunal their name?"

"No."

"This person could corroborate your account. They could tell these senators that you went to that warehouse to find the bomber, not to protect a stockpile."

"I know."

"Then why won't you name them?"

Ahsoka lifted her chin. "Because they helped a fugitive. I was a fugitive. If I say their name, they lose everything I've already lost. The Order would put them out the same way it put me out. I'm not going to do that to someone for being kind to me."

It was said with complete simplicity, and Padmé felt it go into the room like a stone into deep water. She had hoped for sympathy. She saw, instead, three senators exchange glances that said *convenient*.

Ahsoka's eyes moved once, up and to the left, toward the gallery. It was very quick. Padmé followed the look without meaning to and saw only the cluster of Jedi robes: Master Unduli with her hands in her sleeves, the young Mirialan beside her sitting very upright with her own hands pressed flat on her knees, Plo Koon behind them with his head bowed. Then Ahsoka was looking at Padmé again, and her face said *please don't ask me again*.

Padmé didn't.

Tarkin did.

He stood without hurry and approached no closer than he had to. "You have told us a great deal about loyalty, Miss Tano. Loyalty to a friend you will not name. Loyalty to an Order that has expelled you. I wonder if you might extend some to the Republic you swore to serve, and to the three troopers who died in that corridor."

"Don't," Ahsoka said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"Don't use them." Her montrals had darkened at the stripes. "I led those men's brothers on Christophsis and Ryloth and a dozen places that never made the Holonet. I know their numbers. I asked for candles for them. You read them off a datapad."

"Miss Tano—"

"I didn't kill them. I didn't kill Letta. I didn't help bomb the hangar. I've been fighting for this Republic since I was fourteen, Admiral. Where were you?"

The gallery murmured. Palpatine raised one hand, and the murmur died.

"The defendant will confine herself to answering questions," he said. Then, more softly, almost as a kindness: "I understand this is very difficult, my dear."

Tarkin waited until the silence was complete. "The only witness who could support your story is one you refuse to produce," he said. "The only other witness is a Separatist assassin who has vanished. Everything else, Miss Tano, is the record. And the record has your weapons in it."

"No further questions," he said, and sat.

Ahsoka stayed on the stand a moment after she was dismissed, as if she had forgotten how to stand up. Then she rose, and the guards closed in, and she walked back to the defense table without looking at the lightsabers in their case.

Padmé looked at the senators' bench, and she saw it.

She had sat in the Senate long enough to know what a room sounded like when it had already decided. It sounded calm. The senators were writing less. Chuchi was looking at her own hands. Two of the others had their datapads angled toward each other. Mon Mothma was still listening, and Bail was still listening, and the rest had started listening the way people listened to a speech they had already read.

It was not that they believed Ahsoka was a monster. It was that the hangar had burned in the middle of the Temple, on Coruscant, where the war was not supposed to reach. The people in blue in the gallery wanted a name. The people who voted for war funding wanted to know it was safe to keep voting for it. A seventeen-year-old who had been found holding a stun baton in a room full of bombs was a name, and if the name was wrong, no one in this room would ever have to know.

* * *

At the noon recess she found Anakin in a robing room off the east corridor, a narrow space with a coat rack and a water dispenser and a window that looked onto the backside of a ventilation tower. He was standing at the window with his arms folded. He did not turn when she locked the door.

"You were supposed to sleep," she said.

"I did."

"When?"

He thought about it. "Before the vote. Some of it."

She came and stood beside him. Up close he smelled of rain and cheap fuel and the scorched, metallic air of the deep levels. The scrape on his jaw was newer than she'd thought.

"Obi-Wan called me this morning," she said. "He asked if I knew where you go at night."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I'm Ahsoka's counsel, not your keeper. Which is true." She waited. "He says you haven't answered him in days."

"I'll answer him when I have something to say."

"Anakin, if you're caught down there, breaking into gang holds and throwing people through walls—"

"I haven't thrown anyone through a wall."

"—Tarkin will stand up in that room and say the Jedi are hunting down witnesses for the defense. Do you understand? He'll say you're tampering. Anything Ventress says after that is worthless. Anything *you* say is worthless."

"Anything Ventress says is already worthless." He turned at last, and his face was terrible. "You heard him. Separatist assassin. He'll make her a co-defendant the second she walks in. He's not trying to find out what happened, Padmé. He's trying to finish."

"Then help me stop him finishing."

"With what? Another speech?" It came out cutting and he heard it; she watched him hear it. He looked away. "Sorry."

"I'm doing what I can do in that room. You can do something in it too. You can sit there. You can let them see you there, every day, beside her." She heard her own voice tighten. "She looks for you. When she comes in, before she sits down, she looks for you."

His jaw worked. "And when I'm sitting there, I'm not finding the person who did this."

"You've been down there four nights."

"Five tonight."

"Ani."

The name stopped him the way it always did, and she hated using it for leverage, and she used it anyway. He put his mechanical hand flat on the window, gloved fingers spread against the glass, and leaned on it.

"I had them," he said, very low. "On that catwalk. They stood there and watched me hold the stack. Ten meters away. They *watched*, Padmé, like they wanted to see what I'd do. I could have had them. And I let go of the one thing that would have saved her so I could hold up a building."

"You saved two hundred people."

"Tell Snips."

"She knows. She'd have done the same. You trained her to."

He laughed, without any humor at all. "Great. So it's my fault twice."

She put her hand over the back of his on the glass. Leather and cold metal underneath it. She thought of the moment on the stand, his fist closing on the rail, the pause before *No*.

"Is there anything you saw on that platform that you haven't told me?" she said.

He didn't move his hand. He didn't look at her, either. "It was dark," he said. "It was fast."

That was what he had told Tarkin, word for word. She took her hand away.

"All right," she said. "Then I'll go and do my job, and you'll do whatever you're going to do. But be in that chamber for closing. Please. Be where she can see you."

He nodded. He did not promise.

* * *

Tarkin's closing took eleven minutes. Padmé timed it, because she could not stand to do anything else.

He did not raise his voice. He laid out the pieces in the order he had built them: the footage, the weapons, the three troopers, the escape, the assassin, the stockpile. He reminded the senators that the defendant had admitted to running. He reminded them that she had admitted to keeping company with Asajj Ventress. He reminded them that she had refused, under oath and before this tribunal, to identify the one person who might have spoken for her.

"Each of these facts, alone, might have an innocent explanation," he said. "I invite the senators to consider how many innocent explanations they are being asked to accept at once. The Republic is at war. It has been struck in its own capital, in the most protected building on Coruscant, by someone who knew its corridors. The defendant knew those corridors. She has given this tribunal sentiment where it asked for evidence. The Republic seeks the penalty the law provides for the murder of its soldiers in a time of war. Nothing more, and nothing less."

He sat. The penalty the law provided. He had not said the word. He had not needed to.

Padmé rose.

She had written the closing three times and thrown all three away at dawn. She spoke now mostly without the datapad, standing at the end of the defense table where the senators could see both her and the girl behind her.

"The Admiral has asked you to count explanations," she said. "I'm going to ask you to count something else.

"Count the reasons. In two days of evidence, the prosecution has not offered you one reason why Ahsoka Tano would bomb the Temple she was raised in. Not one. No grievance. No payment. No message. No contact with any Separatist before the night she ran for her life. You have heard that she was angry, from the stand, this morning. Of course she was angry. She was accused of murdering soldiers she had served beside for three years.

"Count the years. Since the age of fourteen she has commanded troops of this Republic. She held the line on Christophsis. She fought on Ryloth. She brought younglings back alive from Florrum when she might have saved herself. There is not a single disciplinary entry in her record that involves cruelty, or disloyalty, or a lie. Senators, I have read it. I would invite you to read it. I do not think everyone in this chamber has.

"And count the people in the room. The prosecution needs you to believe there was someone else: an accomplice who struck down three troopers, who attacked Asajj Ventress and stole her weapons, who brought down a pylon on the Dovrin Line. They need there to be someone else, because they know Ahsoka Tano was in a locked cell when that pylon fell. They only need you to believe she was giving the orders. They have not shown you one message, one signal, one meeting. They have shown you a mask that was not her and a weapon that was not hers.

"There is someone else," Padmé said. "There is someone out there, tonight, who knew where Ventress would be, who knew how to fight a Jedi Knight to a standstill, who had access to those cells and to those lightsabers. That person is free. If you convict this girl, they will stay free, and the Republic will be a little less safe and a great deal less just, and you will have given the families in that gallery a name instead of an answer."

She looked up at the blue rows. A woman in the front of them was crying silently with her hands in her lap.

"I am sorry for your loss," Padmé said to her. "I am so sorry. And this will not mend it."

She turned back to the bench. "The defense rests, Chancellor."

She sat down. Her hands were shaking and she put them under the table. Beside her, Ahsoka did not look at her, but under the table, her bound hands moved an inch to the side until the cuff of her binder touched Padmé's wrist and stayed there.

On the high bench, Palpatine leaned forward and folded his hands.

"I thank counsel for both the Republic and the defense," he said. "You have each served this tribunal with diligence, and in the case of the defense, with considerable eloquence. Whatever is decided, let no one say the defendant was poorly represented." He smiled at Padmé, the same warm, slightly tired smile she had seen across a dozen Naboo state dinners when she was fourteen and he was the senator who always remembered her name. "The members of the tribunal will now retire to deliberate. We will reconvene when a verdict has been reached. I would expect that to be tomorrow morning."

The senators rose. They filed out through the door behind the bench in a soft rustle of robes, Chuchi first and fast, as if she couldn't bear the room, Mon Mothma last, pausing once to look back at the defense table. Then the door closed behind them and the chamber came apart into noise.

The guards came for Ahsoka. She stood. She let them turn her by the elbow. At the last moment she twisted to look over her shoulder, not at Padmé but past her, up the aisle, to the seat in the third row behind the defense table where Anakin had sat for every hour of the first day.

Padmé turned to look too.

The seat was empty. It had been empty all through Tarkin's closing, she realized, and all through hers. She had been speaking, and she had not looked, and he had not come.

"He'll be here," Padmé said.

Ahsoka's face did something complicated and then went smooth again. "Sure," she said.

The guards took her out through the side door. The case with her lightsabers went after her on a hover-cart, tagged and sealed, #417 and #418, rattling faintly over the seam in the floor.

Padmé gathered her datapads with hands that would not quite work. When she reached the corridor, her comlink chimed. It was a text-only message, no signature, sent from a relay so deep in the lower levels it barely registered a location at all.

*Someone sold a Dathomirian a med kit two nights ago. Purple bruise, left side of the face. I'm going down. Don't let them read it without me.*

She stood very still in the corridor while the crowd went around her. Then she looked at the timestamp, and at the deliberation chamber doors at the end of the hall, and understood that she had one night to keep a verdict from being read, and Anakin had one night to find a woman the entire Republic wanted to hang beside his Padawan.

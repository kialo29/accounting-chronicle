# Chapter 2: Expelled

The clone on the other side of the energy field was reading her lightsabers their charges.

He did it from a datapad, in the flat voice of a man working through a list, and he did not look up while he did it. "Item four-one-seven. Lightsaber, primary, green crystal, hilt of Togruta-Jedi construction. Recovered at the scene of the deaths of CT-two-two-six-one, CT-four-four-zero-eight, and CT-nine-one-seven-zero, Coruscant Guard. Blade-pattern wounds consistent." He thumbed the screen. "Item four-one-eight. Lightsaber, secondary, yellow-green crystal, shoto length. Recovered at the same scene. Blade-pattern wounds consistent." He looked up then, finally. His helmet was red-striped, and there was a fresh scorch along the edge of one pauldron. "You're required to be informed of what's being held against you. You've been informed."

"I didn't kill them," Ahsoka said.

The guard lowered the datapad. For a moment she thought he might answer. Then he turned on his heel and walked back to his post at the end of the corridor, and she was alone again with the hum of the field and the cold weight of the binders on her wrists.

Consistent. That was the word they kept using. The wounds were consistent with her blades because they had been made with her blades. Somebody had walked into the evidence locker, or wherever Fox had put her sabers after he took them at Letta Turmond's door, and had carried them down this same corridor and cut down three men in their own armor. Then they had left a keycard where a frightened prisoner would find it, and opened the door, and let her walk out into the middle of the bodies.

She had thought it was Anakin, at first. She had picked up that card and believed, for the space of a breath, that her Master had found a way to get her out. She had been wrong about that, and wrong about what was waiting in the warehouse her friend had sent her to, and wrong about how quickly the truth would catch up with her. She was beginning to be very tired of being wrong.

The cell was three paces by four. The bunk was a grey slab jutting out of the wall, the floor was grey, the ceiling was grey with a single light panel that never dimmed. The Republic Center for Military Operations did not believe in windows for people like her. She had not slept. She had sat on the edge of the bunk all night with her back straight and her binders resting on her knees, because she knew there was a holocam behind the light panel, and she knew who would be watching it.

The field at the cell door flickered and went down. Commander Fox came in alone.

He did not take off his helmet. He never did, around her. He stood just inside the threshold with his hands clasped behind him and regarded her through the black T of his visor.

"Thought you'd want to know," he said. "Your partner's friend made another appearance last night. Lower levels. The Dovrin Line platform."

Ahsoka's montrals prickled. "Ventress?"

"Ventress was there. So was a masked accomplice, carrying her red blades. They took a support pylon down with the same nano-droids we found in your warehouse. A twenty-story hab stack nearly went over onto a transit platform full of people."

She was on her feet before she had decided to stand. "How many—"

"None." Fox let that sit, as if he resented having to give it to her. "General Skywalker held the building up. Long enough for everybody to get clear. The accomplice walked away while he did it. So did Ventress, as far as we can tell. We haven't found a body."

Ahsoka sat back down slowly. Her knees did not seem entirely her own.

No one had died. Anakin had held up a building. Of course he had. She could see it as clearly as if she had been there: his feet planted, his jaw set, the whole weight of a tower bearing down on him through the Force while somewhere above him the one person who could have cleared her name slipped away into the dark. And he had let them go. He had looked at a platform full of strangers and at his Padawan's whole future, and he had chosen the strangers.

She was proud of him. It sat in her chest beside something colder, and she could not tell yet which one would win.

"The accomplice," she said. "Masked. Ventress's sabers."

"That's what I said."

"Then they're not her accomplice. That's the person who took those sabers off her in the warehouse. Ventress was unconscious on the floor when I got there. Someone in a mask knocked her out and used her own blades on me. It's the same person, Commander. They're still out there, and they're still—"

"I've logged them as an associate of Asajj Ventress," Fox said, "pending evidence otherwise. You were apprehended in a room full of nano-droid crates. I'll leave the theories to your counsel, when you have some." He turned toward the door. "Be ready in twenty minutes. The Jedi Council has asked to see you."

"Asked," Ahsoka repeated.

Fox paused at the threshold. "The Admiral asked them first," he said, and the field hummed back up behind him.

* * *

They took her to the Temple in a closed transport with no viewports, two Coruscant Guard troopers on either side of her and Fox up front with the pilot. Nobody spoke. When the hatch opened she smelled the Temple before she saw it: stone and old incense and, under that, faintly, the scorched-metal ghost of the hangar that had not yet been fully rebuilt. Her stomach turned over.

The processional hallway was wide and high and lined with columns, and the morning light came through the tall windows in long slanting bars. There were Jedi in the hall. Not many. They stood in twos and threes near the columns, as if they had all happened to be passing, and they went quiet as the guards walked her through. She knew most of their faces. Knights she had sparred with. A Master who had taught her to read star charts when she was nine. A cluster of younglings being hurried away down a side corridor by their crèchemaster, small heads turning to look back over their shoulders.

She kept her chin up. She kept her eyes forward. It was the only thing left she was certain she could do.

Near the great doors to the Council lift, a handful of healers in pale robes stood together beside a column. One of them was Barriss. She had her hood up and her hands folded inside her sleeves, and she looked as though she had not slept either. When Ahsoka passed, Barriss lifted one hand from her sleeve, just slightly, just enough. Her fingers were shaking.

Ahsoka managed something that was almost a smile. Then the lift doors closed between them.

The Council chamber was full. It was always full, she supposed, but she had never stood in it like this, in the center of the ring with her hands bound in front of her and two clones at her back, and the twelve chairs had never seemed so high. Master Yoda sat with both hands folded on the head of his stick and his ears low. Master Windu sat very straight. Master Ki-Adi-Mundi, Master Shaak Ti, Master Saesee Tiin, the holograms of the members who were offworld flickering blue in their seats. Obi-Wan sat with his chin resting against his knuckles, and he was looking at her, at least. That was something.

Master Plo was looking at the floor.

Ahsoka could not see his eyes behind the goggles of his mask. She had never been able to. But she had known him since she was three years old, since he had come to her village on Shili and knelt down on the dirt to be level with her, and she knew the angle of his head the way she knew her own hands. His face was turned toward the tiles at the center of the ring, a little to the left of her feet. It stayed there.

Anakin was standing. There was no chair for him; he was not a Council member. He stood against the curve of the wall behind Obi-Wan's seat with his arms folded and his gloved right hand clenched hard around his left elbow, and when she came in he took one step toward her before Master Windu's glance stopped him.

"Ahsoka Tano," Master Windu said. "You know why you're here."

"I know what I've been accused of," she said. "I didn't do any of it."

"That is not the question before this Council."

"Then what is?"

A ripple went around the ring, small and quiet. Master Ki-Adi-Mundi's mouth tightened.

"Admiral Tarkin has made a formal request on behalf of the Republic military," Master Windu said. "The deaths of Letta Turmond and of three Coruscant Guard troopers occurred inside a military installation. The Senate has demanded a military tribunal, open and accountable to the Republic, and not an internal Jedi proceeding. The Admiral's position is that the Order cannot be trusted to judge one of its own." He paused. "In light of the bombing, and of how many in the Senate already believe the Jedi answer to no one, this Council has concluded that refusing would do the Order more damage than we can afford."

"So you're giving me to them."

"We are removing you from the Order," said Master Windu, "so that the Republic may try you under its own law."

There it was. She had known it was coming since Fox's voice at the cell door. She had known it all night, sitting on the edge of the bunk. Knowing it did not seem to matter at all.

"That's the same thing," she said, "with longer words."

"Master Windu." Anakin had come off the wall. "You can't do this. Not now. Not after last night."

"General Skywalker—"

"There's a second suspect. I saw them. Masked, carrying Ventress's sabers, using the same nano-droids from the warehouse, while Ahsoka was locked in a cell on the other side of the city. Whoever bombed this Temple was on that platform last night. It wasn't her." His voice was rising. He did not seem to notice. "Give me time. A week. Give me three days."

"The masked individual has been logged by Commander Fox as an associate of Ventress," Master Ki-Adi-Mundi said. "Ahsoka herself was apprehended with Ventress. A second conspirator does not clear the first."

"She wasn't with Ventress, she was—"

"The decision has been made, Anakin." Master Windu's voice did not rise to meet his. It simply didn't move. "The vote was taken this morning."

Anakin turned to look at Obi-Wan. It was not a long look, but Ahsoka saw it, and she saw Obi-Wan meet it, and she saw the very small shake of his head. Not *no*. Something more like *I tried*.

"Then vote again," Anakin said.

Nobody answered him.

Master Yoda stirred at last. He looked at Ahsoka for a long moment, his great old eyes heavy-lidded, and she thought he was going to say something to her, something that would make the shape of this make sense. But all he said was, "Sorrow, there is, in this. For all of us." And then he lowered his eyes as well.

Ahsoka found she had nothing to say to that. Sorrow did not get anyone out of a cell.

"There is one more thing," Master Windu said. "Your Padawan beads."

Her hands moved before she could stop them, the binders clicking, her fingers rising halfway toward the beads that hung from the base of her left montral. She had worn them since the day Anakin had taken her as his student. She had added to them on Christophsis and on Ryloth, on Mon Cala, on Felucia. Each bead was a thing she had survived. She could not take them off with her wrists bound, and she found, abruptly, that she would not have taken them off if her hands had been free. If they wanted them, they could come and get them.

Nobody moved.

Master Plo's hands had tightened on the arms of his chair. Obi-Wan had closed his eyes. Anakin stood in the center of the chamber, three steps away from her, and his face had gone white around the mouth.

"Don't," he said, to the whole room. "Don't you dare ask me."

"I would not," Master Windu said quietly. He rose from his chair. "It should be done by someone who voted for it."

He crossed the chamber. His boots made no sound on the tiles. He stopped in front of her, and he was much taller than she was, and for a moment she thought he would hesitate. He did not. His fingers found the clasp beneath the beads and undid it, and the beaded chain came away from her montral with a small dry whisper, a sensation she had felt every night for years when she took it off to sleep and had never once thought about.

The weight was gone. It had not been much of a weight. She had not known how much of her balance it held.

Master Windu closed his hand around the beads. "I'm sorry," he said, low, for her alone, and it sounded like he meant it, and that was somehow the worst part.

He went back to his chair. Fox's hand settled on her shoulder from behind.

Ahsoka turned around without being told. She did not look at Master Plo again. She did not look at Anakin, because if she looked at Anakin she was going to come apart in the middle of the Council chamber, in front of all of them, and she would rather die.

That was a thought she would remember later, and not fondly.

* * *

The senator came in the afternoon.

Ahsoka heard her before she saw her, a voice in the corridor outside, crisp and polite and absolutely unwilling to be turned around. "—and if the Commander would like to file an objection, he's welcome to, but he'll find the Judiciary codes are very clear about a defendant's right to counsel, and I'm afraid I'm in a hurry." Then the field dropped, and Padmé Amidala stepped into the cell with a datapad under one arm and a guard hovering uncertainly behind her.

"Thank you," she told him. "That will be all."

The guard looked at Ahsoka. Then at the senator. Then he retreated, and the field came back up.

Padmé was wearing something dark blue and plain, for her, with her hair pinned back tight. She looked around the cell once, briefly, taking it in—the slab, the light, the binders—and something went across her face and was put away. Then she sat down at the far end of the bunk as if it were a couch in her own apartment and set the datapad on her knee.

"I'm told the Council finished with you this morning," she said.

"They finished with me a while ago," Ahsoka said. "This morning was just the paperwork."

Padmé's mouth tightened at one corner, not quite a smile. "I'm sorry. I wanted to be there. They wouldn't admit anyone from the Senate." She glanced at the side of Ahsoka's head, at the bare place below her montral, and then very deliberately did not look at it again. "I'm here to offer to represent you before the tribunal. If you'll have me."

Ahsoka stared at her. "You're a senator."

"I'm aware."

"You'll be defending someone they think bombed the Temple. Someone they think killed a prisoner and three clones. Half the Senate will—"

"Half the Senate thinks most things I do are a mistake," Padmé said. "The other half just hasn't caught up yet." She tilted her head. "A tribunal is a military court, but the defendant may choose any counsel with standing before the Republic Judiciary. Senators have standing. It's an old provision. Nobody's used it in forty years. I looked." She tapped the datapad. "I'd rather it was me than whoever the military assigns you. They'll give you someone very decent and very junior who has been told exactly how hard to try."

Ahsoka looked down at her binders. Her throat had gone tight, and she was furious with it.

"Did Anakin ask you to do this?"

"Yes," Padmé said. "Last night. Before he went down to the lower levels." A pause. "I'd already decided."

There was something in the way she said his name. Ahsoka had heard it before, on the edges of things—at receptions, on the bridge of a cruiser, once in a hallway in 500 Republica when she had come around a corner too quickly. She had never let herself think about what it was. She did not let herself think about it now.

"All right," she said. "Yes. Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." Padmé activated the datapad. "Tell me everything. From the bombing to the warehouse. Don't leave anything out because it makes you look bad, or because you think it doesn't matter. I'll decide what doesn't matter."

So Ahsoka told her. She told her about Jackar Bowmani and the nano-droids and Letta Turmond asking for her by name. About Fox taking her sabers at the door. About Letta saying a Jedi had been behind it, and then grabbing at her throat, and dying on the floor of her cell before she could say who. About the keycard and the bodies and running. She told her about the lower levels and Ventress—Padmé's eyebrows went up and stayed there—and about the comm call to a friend in the Temple—she left the name out, and Padmé, watching her, let it go for now—and the warehouse, and the figure in the mask.

"The figure in the mask," Padmé said, when she had finished. "Tell me about that again. Slowly."

Ahsoka closed her eyes and made herself see it. "Hooded. The mask was white, like bone. The eyes were slanted. They didn't say a word. Not once. They came at Ventress from behind while she was turned toward me, knocked her flat, and took her sabers off her belt before she hit the floor. Then they came at me."

"Could you tell anything about them? Size, species?"

"Smaller than Ventress. My height, maybe a little more. Not a droid, not a clone." Ahsoka hesitated. "They were good. Really good."

Padmé's stylus paused over the screen. "Good how?"

Ahsoka opened her mouth, and closed it again.

"I'll tell Anakin," she said.

Padmé held her eyes a while. Then she nodded and wrote something down, and did not push, and Ahsoka liked her more for it than she would have been able to explain.

At the door, waiting for the guard, Padmé turned back.

"I won't lie to you," she said. "This is going to be very hard. The evidence is bad, and the people who want you convicted want it for reasons that have nothing to do with you. But they have to prove it. You don't have to prove anything. Remember that, when they start talking."

"And if they prove it anyway?"

Padmé did not answer right away. "Then we make it cost them," she said.

* * *

Anakin came after the light panel had dimmed one notch, which was the only way the Center for Military Operations admitted it was night.

She knew it was him before the field went down. She would have known him through a durasteel wall. His presence in the Force was a storm on the horizon on the best of days, and tonight it was something worse, something knotted and close and turning in on itself. When he stepped into the cell he stopped just inside the threshold the way Fox had, and for a moment he just looked at her.

He looked terrible. There was a dark crust of dried blood just inside one nostril that he had missed when he washed. His robes were the same ones from the Council chamber, and they had been the same ones the night before, too, she would bet; the hem was grey with lower-levels grime. His right hand hung at his side, and as she watched, he flexed the mechanical fingers once, twice, as if they did not quite want to close.

"Hey, Snips," he said.

She had promised herself she was not going to cry. She managed it, barely.

"I heard you held up a building," she said.

He let out a breath that was almost a laugh. "It was a small building."

"Fox said twenty stories."

"Fox exaggerates."

"Fox has never exaggerated anything in his life." She moved over on the bunk, and after a second he came and sat down beside her, heavily, the way he sat in the cockpit of the *Twilight* after a long run. He smelled of smoke and engine coolant and stone dust. "You had them," she said. "Didn't you. The one in the mask."

He was silent for so long that she thought he was not going to answer.

"They stopped," he said at last. "On a catwalk, while I was holding it. They stopped and they watched me. I could have—" His mechanical hand closed into a fist on his knee and stayed that way. "There were kids on that platform, Ahsoka. A Gran woman with a baby. An Ithorian who couldn't run fast enough. If I'd let go—"

"Then they'd be dead," she said. "And you'd be you, and you wouldn't be able to live with it. I wouldn't want you to." She bumped his shoulder with hers, very lightly, the binders clinking. "You did the right thing, Master."

"I'm not your Master," he said. "They made sure of that this morning."

That landed somewhere under her ribs. She let it.

"Then listen to me as somebody who isn't your Padawan," she said. "Because I need you to hear this, and you're the only one I can tell. Ventress is the only witness. She's the only other person who saw the one in the mask up close. She was in the warehouse. She was on the platform. If anybody knows who they are, or where they came from, or how they knew where we'd be, it's her."

"Ventress was knocked off a ledge into a four-hundred-level drop."

"Ventress," Ahsoka said, "is too spiteful to die from falling."

That time he did laugh, short and surprised, and it seemed to hurt him.

"There's something else." She lowered her voice, though there was nothing to lower it against but the hum of the field and the holocam behind the light. "In the warehouse. When the masked one came at me. I've fought a lot of people, Anakin. Droids, bounty hunters, Ventress, Grievous. I know what it looks like when somebody's learned to fight from somewhere else." She swallowed. "They didn't. They fought like they'd learned it here. In the salles. The way they closed off my high line. The way they covered when I pressed. It was Temple training. I know it was. I'd stake my life on it."

She felt it in him before she saw it on his face. A stillness, sudden and complete, like a hand closing over a candle flame.

"You saw it too," she said.

He did not look at her. He was looking at the opposite wall, at nothing. "On the platform. They used a Soresu turn on me. Clean as anything Obi-Wan ever drilled into me." His jaw worked. "I almost told Fox."

"Almost?"

"What was I going to say? That whoever bombed the Temple fought like a Jedi?" He turned his head and finally met her eyes, and the look on his face was one she had never seen there before. "You know what Tarkin does with that, Snips? He doesn't hear 'somebody else did it.' He hears 'Jedi conspiracy.' He hears 'the Padawan had help from inside.' He'd thank me for it. He'd put it in the record as a character witness."

She wanted to argue. She found she couldn't.

"So who?" she said instead. "Who in the Temple would—"

"I don't know." His voice had gone rough. "I don't know, and I don't know who to ask, because I don't know who it is." He looked down at his clenched hand and seemed to notice it for the first time, and made it open, finger by finger. "But Ventress might. And I'm going to find her. I swear to you, Ahsoka. I'll take the lower levels apart rivet by rivet if I have to. I'll find her, and I'll make her talk, and I'll bring it in front of that tribunal and make every one of them look at it."

She believed him. That was the trouble with Anakin. She always had.

"Did you leave the keycard?" she asked. "In my cell. Before. The night I got out."

He frowned. "What keycard?"

So that was that. She nodded, slowly, and filed it away with everything else she had been wrong about.

The guard came to the edge of the field and said, "Time, General," in a voice that meant it was well past time and he had been generous. Anakin stood. He looked down at her, and she saw his eyes go to the side of her head, to the empty place below her montral, and stay there.

"They shouldn't have done that," he said. "Not in front of everybody. Not like that."

"Master Windu said it should be someone who voted for it."

"Master Windu," Anakin said, very evenly, "says a lot of things."

He went to the door. The field dropped for him. He was halfway through it when she heard herself say it.

"Skyguy."

He stopped. He did not turn around, not all the way. She saw his shoulders go tight under the dirty robe.

"Find her," she said. "Please."

He nodded once, without looking back, and then he was gone, and the field came up behind him with its low steady hum, and Ahsoka sat on the edge of the bunk and pressed the heels of her bound hands hard against her eyes until she saw colors.

* * *

She tried to meditate.

It should have been easy. It was the first thing they taught you, before you could hold a training saber, before you could read: sit, breathe, reach out. Let the Force move through you the way water moves through stone. She had done it on battlefields with artillery falling. She had done it in the belly of a crashing gunship. She folded her legs on the bunk and rested her bound hands in her lap and closed her eyes, and she breathed.

The Force was there. It was always there. But tonight it felt like a room she had been asked to leave, the furniture still in its place, the lights still on, and her standing at the doorway with no right to go back in.

*Jedi are not supposed to feel like that*, she thought. And then: *But I'm not a Jedi.*

She opened her eyes.

Not a Jedi. She turned the words over carefully, the way she would have turned a strange piece of tech, looking for the seam. She had been a Jedi since before she could remember. Master Plo had found her when she was three and brought her to the Temple, and she had grown up inside it, in the crèche and the salles and the archives, under the great vaulted ceilings with the light coming down. She had never been anything else. She had never wanted to be. Every bead on that chain had been a promise, kept on both sides. She would give the Order everything, and the Order would be what it said it was.

And this morning they had sat in their high chairs and taken it back, and the worst of it—the very worst of it, worse than the beads, worse than Master Windu's hand at her montral—was how quietly it had gone. Nobody had shouted except Anakin. Nobody had stood up except Anakin. Obi-Wan had shaken his head. Master Yoda had spoken about sorrow. Master Plo, who had knelt in the dirt on Shili to be level with a frightened little girl, had looked at the floor.

They had not fought for her. Not one of them. They had weighed her against the Senate and the Admiral and the Holonet, against the Order's name and the war and the bombing and the dead, and they had looked at the scales, and they had let her go.

She had always thought that if the worst happened, the Order would be the wall at her back. That was what it meant to belong somewhere. That no matter how far out you went, there was a place that would come and get you.

There was no wall. There had never been a wall. There had only been a door, and it had always opened in both directions, and she had simply never been on the wrong side of it before.

Ahsoka sat very still on the slab and let that settle into her, all the way down, until she was sure it was true. She did not cry. She was past that, for now. Instead she felt something quiet and hard come into the space where the grief had been, something she did not have a name for yet. It was not anger, not quite. It was not peace. It felt like standing up after a fall and finding out which bones still held.

The field went down.

She looked up. Commander Fox stood in the doorway, and behind him the corridor was dim, the guards' lights reflecting off the polished floor. It was very late. He had no business being here at this hour unless someone had sent him.

He held out a datapad.

"The charging document," he said. "Filed with the Judiciary an hour ago. You're entitled to a copy. Your counsel's been sent one."

Ahsoka took it in her bound hands. The text was dense and formal, column after column of it—the bombing, the conspiracy, Letta Turmond, the three troopers, item four-one-seven and item four-one-eight. She read it without taking any of it in. Her eyes slid down to the bottom of the screen, where the block of official seals was, and the names.

*Presiding: Supreme Chancellor Palpatine.*

*Prosecuting officer: Admiral Wilhuff Tarkin, Republic Navy.*

And beneath that, in the same plain official type as everything else, as if it were one more line in an inventory:

*Penalty sought by the prosecution: death.*

She read it twice. Then she looked up at Fox.

He stood in the doorway with his hands behind his back, his visor giving back nothing at all. But he had not left. He was still standing there, and after a moment Ahsoka understood that he was waiting to see what she would do, so that he could put it in his report.

She handed him back the datapad. She sat up straighter on the slab.

"Thank you, Commander," she said. "Tell the Admiral I'll see him in court."

# Chapter 23: The Order

Six hours out of Florrum, the medbay console chimed with a follow-up request concerning a Chancellery aide's left wrist, and Barriss Offee, who had healed that wrist herself months ago and knew there was nothing left in it to follow up, felt her hands go perfectly still.

The duty droid rolled toward the console. "A routine query, Healer. I can answer it from the file."

"No," Barriss said. "I'll take it. Isolation two."

She did not hurry. That was the first discipline she had learned in the clinic as a youngling, carrying trays: the one who runs spills. She walked past the three troopers asleep under scald dressings from the geyser field, past the cot where Master Kenobi had sat an hour earlier, grey with ore dust from the crown of his head to his boots, declining everything but a cold pack for his shoulder. *I'm told I was buried,* he had said, mildly, while she scanned him. *Which seems an exaggeration, since here I am.* He had looked at her a moment longer than the scan required. Everyone was looking at everyone a moment too long on this ship.

Isolation two was a white box with one cot and a smell of antiseptic so strong it had a taste. She sealed the door, sat on the edge of the cot, and keyed the query open.

It was not Dace Orrin.

The Chancellor resolved out of the blue static a hand's height tall, seated in one of his deep chairs with his fingers folded on his knee, as if she had dropped by for tea and he were glad of it. Even in miniature, even in monochrome, she could feel the warmth of that red room on her face.

"Barriss. Forgive the subterfuge. I find a wrist is the most discreet thing in the galaxy. No one ever asks about a wrist." He tilted his head. "You look tired, my dear."

"Chancellor."

"I won't keep you. I only wanted you to hear something from me before you heard it from the Admiral, who has a way of making news sound like a sentence." A small, rueful smile. "Major Rusk tells us the vessel that left Florrum jumped Core-ward. She believes it will make for Coruscant. With one engine running as hot as the Major describes, she estimates three days."

Barriss said nothing. She had known since the hangar deck, since she'd watched General Skywalker come up the ramp with white crust caked on his boots and a face like a shut door. She'd known since before that, honestly — since the dawn she'd felt Ahsoka in the Force for the first time in five months, bright and whole and free of the collar, and had sat in this medbay with her hands pressed flat on her knees, making herself small.

"She isn't running," the Chancellor said. "I think we must both admit that now. A girl who runs goes outward, toward the Rim. A girl who comes home has something to say." He let a breath pass. "And I think, from what Anakin did not tell me in his report, that she knows to whom she wishes to say it."

"Anakin told you —"

"Anakin told me she lost him in the vents. Anakin is a very poor liar to the people who love him." The hologram's hands opened, closed again. "He believes she knows who did it. He believes it's someone she cares for. He said that much without meaning to."

The antiseptic taste had reached the back of her throat. She swallowed it.

"Let me be plain with you," he said, "because you have always been plain with me, and I value it. If young Tano proves her innocence, she does not merely prove that she did not do a thing. She proves that someone else did. A verdict cannot be set aside into empty air. The Senate will want the chair filled. There will be a second trial."

"Yes."

"I sealed what I had, Barriss. My little camera. The gallery. Those later recordings from the detention level I told you I had no need to watch. I could keep them sealed because there was a verdict standing in front of them, solid as a wall. Nobody asks for footage of a closed case." His voice was very gentle. "In a second trial, everything is asked for. Admirals file requests. Senators subpoena. Clerks find things in drawers. I could protect you from a question nobody was asking. I can't protect you from one the whole Republic is asking at once."

She heard herself say, "Then there's nothing you can do."

"Not then. No." He looked down at his folded hands, as though they disappointed him. "The only shelter I can offer you is a world in which there is no second trial. I'm afraid that is the only world in which my help is worth anything at all."

The cot creaked as she shifted. In the next room a trooper muttered in his sleep and was quiet.

"And how," Barriss said carefully, "would there be no second trial?"

The Chancellor smiled at her with real fondness, as one smiles at a clever student who has asked the question one hoped for.

"I'm a politician, my dear. I only know the ways that involve committees." He sighed. "But you were a healer long before you were anything else. A healer knows better than anyone that not every patient can be saved. That a wound left open will poison everything around it. That sometimes the hardest mercy is the one done quickly, by a steady hand, before the suffering spreads."

She did not answer. She could not have.

"And there is Anakin to consider," he went on, softer still. "You've seen what he is when she's in danger. You saw the cup at Narkis — oh yes, the Commandant wrote it up; a steel cup crushed like paper. If Ahsoka sets a name before him, I do not believe there is anyone in this galaxy who could stand between Anakin Skywalker and the owner of that name. Not his Council. Certainly not an old man in a red office." He shook his head. "I would grieve that. For him as much as anyone."

"What do you want me to do?"

"Want?" He seemed faintly surprised, then sorry for her. "Nothing, Barriss. Nothing you don't already want for yourself. I only wished you to understand where things stand, so that whatever comes, you'll have chosen it with your eyes open. That is all friendship ever really is, I think. Clear sight, shared." The hologram flickered as the relay shifted. "Rest, if you can. You'll be home before she is."

The blue light folded down to a point and went out.

Barriss sat on the edge of the cot in the white room for a long time.

He had not said it. She went back over every word, the way she went back over a scan she didn't trust, and he had not said it once. He had talked about committees and wounds and mercy and friendship. He had talked about Anakin. He had told her what he could not do.

She understood him perfectly.

She looked down at her hands, waiting for them to shake. They did not. They lay on her knees, green and patterned and steady as a surgeon's, and that frightened her more than anything he had said.

* * *

The Temple clinic at night was the only place on Coruscant that still felt like the Order she had joined.

Barriss had been home two days. She'd taken the late rotation both nights, because the clinic at the late rotation asked nothing of anyone. A Padawan with a sprained knee from the training salle. An old Master whose lungs rattled when it rained above the Temple, which was always. Racks of bacta sleeves to count and date and turn so the oldest came forward. The lights dimmed to amber. The air faintly sweet with kolto. Work that had an end to it.

She was turning the bacta racks when Luminara came in.

Her master did not announce herself. She never had. She came across the clinic floor in her dark robes with her hands folded into her sleeves, sat down on the end of an empty cot, and watched Barriss work, the way she had watched her a thousand times in the gardens and the archives and the salles, with that stillness that had once felt like shelter.

"You should be asleep, Master," Barriss said.

"So should you." Luminara's eyes went to the rack. "You've dated that sleeve twice."

Barriss looked. She had. She peeled the second label off with her thumbnail and set the sleeve back in its slot.

"Master Kenobi gave his report to the Council this morning," Luminara said. "Ventress fought him with a knife. A single knife, and the Force, and a great deal of falling ore." She paused. "Not her own blades. It seems she never recovered them. Master Kenobi found that curious. So did Master Windu, though he didn't say so."

"She's a bounty hunter," Barriss said. "She could have lost them anywhere."

"Yes. She could." Luminara let the words rest. "You stayed aboard at Florrum."

"The medbay needed a healer. There were burns from the vents."

"There were. Three, and minor, and Commander Rex's medic could have managed them blindfolded." Her voice held no accusation. That was the worst of it. It was only careful, the way a hand is careful on a bandage it suspects is hiding something worse. "I asked you once about a bruise on your arm. Do you remember? The day after the Dovrin platform."

Barriss's left forearm, under her sleeve, remembered. The mark had faded months ago. The shape of it hadn't.

"I remember."

"You told me it was a crate. Then you told me it was sparring. Then you wouldn't let me heal it." Luminara looked down at her own folded hands, then up again. "I told myself a great many things after that. That you were grieving. That the war was in you the way it is in all of us now. That a Padawan is allowed a door or two her master doesn't open." She drew a slow breath. "I am not asking about the bruise tonight."

The clinic was very quiet. Somewhere at the far end a monitor kept its patient time.

"Barriss," her master said, gently and directly, "is there something you need to tell me?"

There it was. Five months of it, laid on the cot between them like a tray of instruments, every one of them clean and ready. All she had to do was pick one up.

She thought: *The warehouse. The mask. Letta's throat under my hand from across a corridor. The three troopers outside the cells — I lit candles for them because she asked me to. She asked me to.* She thought of Ahsoka at Narkis, holding up her healed shin and smiling at her like a sister. She thought of a red room and a gentle voice saying *a steady hand.* And she thought of Luminara on a witness stand in a second trial, being asked under oath what she had suspected and when, and having to answer.

"No, Master," Barriss said. "There's nothing."

She heard how it sounded. Calm. Courteous. A good Padawan's answer, given in a good Padawan's voice. She had become very good at that voice.

Luminara held her gaze, and Barriss watched her master know.

It was not dramatic. Nothing in Luminara's face moved that a stranger would have seen. But something behind it settled, the way a stone settles to the bottom of still water and the surface closes over it, and Barriss felt it in the Force as plainly as a hand withdrawn.

She waited for the rest. For Luminara to rise, take her wrist, say *No. Sit down. We are not leaving this room.* For her to call Master Windu. For her to do anything at all that would take the choice out of Barriss's hands and put it somewhere else.

Luminara stood. She smoothed her robes.

"Then go and sleep, Barriss," she said. "Leave the racks. They'll still be here at dawn." She crossed to the door, and stopped with her hand on the frame, not turning around. "I'll be in my quarters. If that changes."

And she went.

Barriss stood among the bacta sleeves with the amber light on her hands and understood that she had just been given the most terrible thing her master had to give: her own freedom. No one was going to stop her. No one was going to drag the truth out of her for her own good. Luminara had looked straight into the closed door and decided, as a Jedi Master decides, that she would not break it down — that if it opened, it had to open from inside.

Barriss had wanted, more than she had wanted anything in all those months, for someone to break it down.

She left the racks. She did not go to sleep.

* * *

Crawlway C-31 had not changed. That was the thing about the Temple's underside: nothing down there ever changed. The upper Temple was rebuilt, repainted, re-dedicated, wreathed in candles for the hangar dead. Three levels below the eastern foundation, it was still the same old dark, the same weeping durasteel, the same smell of cold stone and stale lubricant that had been there when the first Jedi laid the first block.

She went down on the third night, when the clinic rotation ended and the corridors had emptied.

The route was in her body. Through the service door behind the old water-reclamation pumps. Down the ladder with the third rung bent. Along the conduit tray on her hands and knees, the glow rod in her teeth, the stone close enough on either side to brush her shoulders. Past the junction where the air went suddenly warm from some forgotten heat exchanger and then cold again. She counted the panels as she had counted them five months ago, with her own saber in one hand and Ventress's in her satchel and her heart going so hard she had thought the whole Temple would hear it.

Tonight her heart was quiet. She noted that, as a healer notes a symptom.

The panel was where she had left it, its edge fused to the frame in a ragged seam where she had run her blade along it. She knelt, lit her saber, and the blue light filled the crawlway like water filling a cup. She cut along the old seam slowly, not wanting to touch what was inside, and the panel tipped toward her with a scrape of hot metal and leaned against her knees.

The bundle sat in the cavity behind it, exactly where she had put it.

The surgical drape had gone grey with dust and stiff with damp. She drew it out with both hands, and even through the cloth she felt them — the two curved hilts lying side by side, heavy and wrong, their bled crystals humming just beneath her senses like a held breath that had never been let go. Five months in the dark had not quieted them. If anything they felt more awake, the way a wound left untended grows hot.

She laid the bundle across her knees and unfolded the drape.

The mask was on top. Bone-white, slanted eye-slits, the curve of the brow catching the blue of her saber. She had forgotten how light it was. She had worn it for barely an hour in all, and in her memory it weighed as much as the hab stack at Dovrin had weighed on Anakin Skywalker's outstretched hand.

Underneath lay the sabers. She did not touch them yet.

She had come down here once before to destroy them. She remembered kneeling in this exact spot with the drape open and her hand over the hilts, reaching for the crystals to crush them, and finding she could not. She had told herself then that it was weakness — that some coward in her wanted to keep a proof, a way back, a door. She had sealed them in the wall and walked away and lit four candles every dawn after, and never once let herself ask what the door was for.

Now she knew what they were for, or what someone wished them to be for. Ventress's blades, found beside a body, would tell a story the whole Republic already wanted to believe: the fugitive and her Separatist accomplice, falling out in the lower levels. A sad end. No second trial. A steady hand.

He had not said it. He had not needed to. He had only reminded her where she'd hidden the knife.

Barriss closed her hand around the first hilt. The crystal's hunger came up her arm like cold water. She lifted it, and the second, and laid them in her satchel side by side, and wrapped the drape over them so the curves didn't show. She held the mask a moment longer. Then she put it in too.

She sealed nothing behind her. She left the panel leaning against the wall with its seam still glowing faintly orange, cooling in the dark, and went back along the conduit tray on her hands and knees with the satchel dragging at her shoulder and the glow rod in her teeth.

* * *

The comlink chimed as she came up the ladder.

Not her Temple comlink. That one sat in the clinic charging cradle where she'd left it, logged and traceable. This was the other one — the old, scuffed, civilian unit she had carried in her inner pocket since the warehouse without ever switching it off, for the same reason she had never managed to crush two kyber crystals. It had one channel set on it. A private frequency she had given Ahsoka long ago, years before any of this, laughing: *In case you ever need a healer and don't want the whole Temple knowing what you've done to yourself.* Ahsoka had used it exactly once. From the lower levels, on the run, with Ventress beside her, asking her friend in the Temple where the nano-droids had come from.

Barriss had answered that call. She had given her an address.

She hung on the ladder with one hand, three levels below the eastern foundation, and stared at the little amber light blinking against the dark of her robe.

It was a text message. No voice. No image. No signature. She brought it up with her thumb.

*I know. Meet me where you left me.*

That was all.

She read it four times. The words did not change. She found herself checking the signal path the way she would have checked a stranger's pulse — and there was no relay tag, no Holonet routing. A short-range bounce off the city grid. Whoever had sent it was on Coruscant. Was here. Already.

Rusk had said three days. It had been three days.

Barriss climbed the rest of the ladder because her body knew how. She stepped out through the service door behind the reclamation pumps and stood in the dim corridor with the satchel pulling at her shoulder and the comlink still lit in her palm.

*Where you left me.*

An old munitions warehouse in the lower levels, its roof half gone, its floor stacked with nano-droid crates that Barriss herself had seen arranged. A girl on her back among them, unconscious, with a stolen Guard baton lying out of reach where it had been knocked from her hand. The Coruscant Guard's lights already sweeping the windows. Barriss had stood over her for perhaps three heartbeats before she ran — long enough to see that Ahsoka was breathing, not long enough to do anything about it.

*I know.* Not *I think.* Not *Tell me it isn't you.* Ahsoka had never in her life been able to say a hard thing without softening it with a joke, and she had not softened this.

There was no time in the message. There didn't need to be. Ahsoka would be there now, or soon, and she would wait. She had waited in ice for four months. She could wait in the dark for a night.

Barriss walked.

She went back up through the Temple by the servants' ways, the satchel against her hip. At this hour the halls belonged to the night-shift droids and a few insomniac Knights pacing the colonnades. Nobody stopped her. Why would they? A healer with a healer's bag, coming from the lower levels at night; there was always someone down there who needed something.

At the junction below her quarters she stopped, without quite meaning to, and looked down the long corridor toward Luminara's door.

There was a line of light under it. Her master was awake.

*I'll be in my quarters. If that changes.*

She stood there long enough for the light to become the only thing she could see. Then she turned the other way.

In her own room she did not turn on the lamp. She took off her outer robe and put on the dark hooded cloak she had worn to the Chancellor's office that first afternoon, the one that hid the face. She clipped her own saber to her belt, where it had always been, where its blue crystal sat calm and familiar and faintly reproachful against her side. She checked the satchel once — the drape, the two curved hilts beneath it, the white mask on top — and buckled it closed. She put the old comlink back in her inner pocket, still on.

On the shelf by the window stood the four candles she lit every dawn, burned down to stubs. She looked at them for a long time. She had never named them to anyone. Jackar. CT-2261. CT-4408. CT-9170. A man she had killed through his wife's hands, and three troopers she had killed with Ahsoka's blades, and then lit candles for at Ahsoka's request.

There was no candle for Letta. Ahsoka had guessed wrong about that, kindly. There had never been room on the shelf.

Barriss pulled up her hood.

She took the old maintenance lift down from the eastern wing, the one the clinic used for deliveries, and stepped out into the rain at the base of the Temple's long shadow. Below her the city fell away level after level into neon and steam, toward the old munitions district and a building with half a roof, and a girl who had once pulled her out of her own mind on Geonosis and asked nothing for it.

She had three blades with her. One was her own. Two had been made to kill. She had a white mask that could make her anyone, and a face underneath it that the Chancellor had saved in a single frame.

He had given her a world with no second trial. Luminara had given her a door she would have to open herself. And Ahsoka, from somewhere in that rain, had given her five words and an address she already knew.

Barriss went down into the lower levels, and she did not know — truly did not know, for the first time since the hangar burned — which of the three blades she meant to light.

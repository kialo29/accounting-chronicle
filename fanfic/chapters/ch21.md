# Chapter 21: Green Hands

Rex's message had said two days, and the second day was nearly gone.

Ahsoka sat cross-legged on the rusted roof of the ore crusher with the borrowed saber across her knees and the Force running through her like meltwater through a cracked dam, and she was not thinking about the warehouse. She was carefully, deliberately not thinking about it. It took more concentration than any kata she had ever learned.

Below her the outpost baked in the sulfur light. Hondo called it Number Nine — "because, my friend, the first eight exploded" — and had charged her for the directions. It was a dead place: a winch tower leaning over a shaft somebody had capped with plate durasteel, a line of ore hoppers rusting on a buckled rail, a bunkhouse whose roof had given up on its eastern half. The canyon walls rose red on both sides, close enough that the sky was only a yellow seam overhead. Every hour or so a geyser somewhere up the canyon coughed, a smell like old eggs came drifting down the rail line, and the hoppers ticked as they cooled or warmed.

She could feel all of it. That was the trouble. For four months the collar had held her in a silence so total she'd forgotten what the galaxy sounded like, and now it was all back at once — the stone's slow weight, the tiny lives in the cracks, the heat-pulse of the geysers, the hard bright knot of Ventress on the bunkhouse step — and every bit of it pulled at her attention except the one thing she would not look at.

Each time she sank toward stillness, the thought rose to meet her. Each time, she stood up inside herself and walked away from it.

A pebble struck the roof beside her knee.

"You're doing it again," Ventress called up.

Ahsoka opened her eyes. Ventress had the long rifle Hondo had lent them — he'd itemized that, too — propped across her thighs, and she was cleaning her one knife on her sleeve with the absent care of someone who had nothing else to clean.

"Doing what?"

"Flinching. You go quiet, and quieter, and then you jerk back like you've put your hand on a hot pipe." Ventress tilted her head. "It's very loud."

"Then stop listening."

"I'd love to. It's a small canyon."

Ahsoka unfolded her legs and climbed down the crusher's side ladder, the rungs gritty with yellow dust. Her neck still felt strange without the collar's weight, the raw ring of skin there tender under the scarf Hondo had thrown at her on their way out. She caught herself reaching for it and dropped her hand.

"Your clone is late," Ventress said.

"He said two days. It's still the second day."

"For another hour." Ventress sighted down the canyon along the rail line, more out of habit than interest. "If it's the Republic who answers, they'll come in loud. If it's Bane, they'll come in quiet. I would like to know which kind of night I'm having."

"It's Rex."

"You sound sure."

"I am sure."

"You were sure about the smuggler, too," Ventress said.

That landed, and Ventress knew it had landed; she didn't bother to look pleased. Ahsoka sat on an overturned ore bucket across from her and studied her own hands. Tess had looked her in the eye over a blaster wound and kept the credit chit. There had been no apology in it and no lie, either, and Ahsoka found she couldn't hate her, which bothered her more than hating would have.

"Tess was afraid," she said. "People do things when they're afraid."

"People do things when they want to. Fear is just the excuse they bring along." Ventress slid the knife into her boot. "You keep doing that, by the way. Deciding why people hurt you. Always the kindest reason on the shelf."

"Is this the part of the deal where you lecture me?"

"The deal," Ventress said, "is that I get into the Temple, and you get a witness. A witness to what, exactly? I've told you what I saw. A hooded buyer with green hands. A dead Rodian. A fighter who knew where to wait for us." Her voice didn't rise. It got very precise instead, and colder. "You have spent two days on that roof pretending you don't know what that adds up to."

"I don't."

"Your friend in the Temple—"

"Is not part of the deal."

The words came out harder than she meant. Somewhere up the canyon the geyser coughed. Ventress watched her for a long moment with that pale, unreadable face — the bruise from the platform had faded months ago, but Ahsoka could still picture it there — and then she shrugged, as if the whole subject bored her.

"As you like," she said. "It's your neck."

It wasn't fair, and it wasn't finished, and they both knew it. Ahsoka looked away down the rail line toward the canyon mouth, where the walls opened onto the flats, and because she was looking — because the Force was open and she was straining away from the thing in her own head — she felt him before she heard him.

A presence, small and far off and moving fast. Hot with fever. Stubborn as a rivet. Achingly, impossibly familiar, the way the 501st had felt from the command deck on a good day: a hundred bright, separate lights that all burned the same color.

She was on her feet before she knew it.

"Speeder," Ventress said a moment later, and the rifle came up.

* * *

It was one of Hondo's skiff-bikes, with one of Hondo's younger boys driving it far too fast, and it stopped in a spray of yellow grit thirty meters short of the bunkhouse, as if the boy didn't care to get any closer to two women with weapons. The passenger climbed off the back. He did it badly, favoring his whole left side, and stood swaying in the dust while the bike wheeled and screamed away down the canyon.

He wore a spacer's coat too big for him, and a hood, and his left arm was bound across his chest inside the coat. A medkit hung on his right hip, a Guard-issue comlink clipped beside it.

Ventress's rifle tracked his chest. "That's close enough."

The man lifted his good hand, slow, and pushed back the hood.

His hair, which she had only ever seen regulation-short, had grown out ragged and dark. There were hollows under his cheekbones that had not been there the last time she'd seen him, and his skin had a sheen that wasn't sweat from the heat. At his right temple, small and blunt, someone had inked a single numeral: a five.

He looked past the rifle at Ahsoka, and the corner of his mouth twitched.

"Rex says her sense of direction is still terrible," he said.

Something in her chest came loose. It was so stupid. It was such a Rex thing to say, and such a Fives thing to repeat, standing half-dead in the sulfur dust with a gun on him, that she laughed — a short, cracked sound that surprised her as much as anyone.

"Fives."

"Commander." He tried to straighten into something like attention and listed sharply to the left instead. "Sorry. Long trip. Captain of that hauler flies like he's got a personal grudge against the stars."

She was across the yard before Ventress could object, and she caught him under his right arm as his knees went. He was too light. He'd always been all shoulders and opinions; now she could feel his ribs through the coat.

"You know this one?" Ventress hadn't lowered the rifle.

"501st," Ahsoka said. "He's ours."

"He's on fire."

He was. Even through the coat the heat came off him, and in the Force his presence had a ragged, smoldering edge, like a bright light seen through smoke. "Inside," she said. "Help me."

Ventress looked at Fives for one more long second, then slung the rifle and took his other side, careful of the bound arm — more careful than Ahsoka would have expected.

They got him onto the bunk in the half of the bunkhouse that still had a roof. Ahsoka peeled the coat back. The binding beneath was stiff and discolored, and when she cut it away the shoulder underneath was angry: a blaster wound, high on the left side, that had closed wrong and swollen hot and dark around the edges.

"Fox," Fives said, watching her face. "Coruscant Guard. Good shot. I'd have told him so, but I was busy falling."

"Fox shot you?"

"Officially, Commander, I'm dead. It's a whole story."

"Then you'd better tell it." She had Rex's medkit open on the floor: bacta, binding, a row of six fever injectors with one already gone. Inside the lid, on a strip of tape, Rex's blocky handwriting said ONE EVERY TWELVE HOURS. DON'T BE A HERO. "And hold still."

She wasn't a healer. She'd never had the patience for it. Healing in the Force was a slow listening, a coaxing, and she had always been better at pushing things out of the way. But she laid her palm a finger's width above the wound and closed her eyes, and she listened.

The heat was a knot of wrongness under the skin, a dull, stubborn fire the body was losing to. She couldn't burn it out. She could hold the pain away — fold it back like a blanket so his breath eased — and she could steady the body's own fight, lend it a little of her strength, the way you'd brace a door someone else was trying to hold shut. When she opened her eyes, the swelling looked no different, but his jaw had unclenched.

"Huh," Fives said. "That's better than the shots."

"Talk," she said, and reached for the bacta.

He talked.

He told it plainly, the way clones told things in debrief, and it was worse for that. Ringo Vinda: a rotating hub, a sun-glare, and Tup — sweet, earnest Tup, who'd once asked her in all seriousness whether Togruta montrals could hear through walls — putting his blaster to General Tiplar and pulling the trigger. Tup saying good soldiers followed orders, over and over, like a droid stuck on a loop. Kamino forbidding scans. A medical droid that scanned him anyway. A growth in Tup's brain that wasn't a growth. Tup dying on the table when they took it out.

"So I had them check me," Fives said. "Same thing. Same place. AZI pulled it out while I was awake." He tapped his temple, just under the inked five. "Every clone, Commander. From the tank. Before we've even got eyes. They grow it into us."

Ahsoka's hands had stopped on the binding. She made them start again.

"What does it do?"

"Nothing. Until somebody tells it to." He watched the broken roof. "Tup didn't hate her. He didn't hate anybody. Something in his head got told to kill a Jedi, so he did, and then he couldn't tell you why. Just kept saying it was an order."

"An order from whom?"

"That's the part." Fives's voice dropped. "The Kaminoans were going to bury it. Call it a virus. General Shaak Ti got me a hearing with the Chancellor instead. I thought — he's the top. If anybody can stop it, he can." He breathed for a while. "Somebody drugged me on the way in. Next thing I know there's a guard saying I attacked him. The Chancellor. And he's holding my chip in a little vial, and he doesn't give it back."

From the doorway, where she had been leaning with her arms folded, Ventress made a soft, unpleasant sound.

"How wonderful," she said. "A secret worm in every soldier's skull and a politician holding the jar. Was there thunder? Did he laugh into the dark?"

"Ventress."

"I'm only asking for the details. It's a very good story."

"It's not a story." Fives turned his head on the bunk to look at her, and something in his flat, fevered voice made Ventress's mouth close. "I ran. Down through the lower levels, past 79's, to an old refinery Rex and I used to know. Rex found me. General Skywalker found me. I told them. Fox fired anyway, and Rex knocked his arm or I'd be dead, and I went down a drainage shaft and the Republic wrote me off." He shut his eyes. "Rex believes me. He didn't, at first. Then he thought about your trial, Commander. How he stood there and let it happen. He said he wasn't doing that twice."

Ahsoka couldn't speak. She tied off the binding because her hands knew how.

Rex. Rex at her cell door, his salute stalling halfway and then completing. Rex teaching her the Christophsis codes in a mess hall at three in the morning, patient, deadpan, making her repeat them until she could tap them out half asleep. There was something in his head. There had always been something in his head. It had been there while he taught her.

And Jesse, and Kix. And the small clone detachment at Narkis, who had stood in the Yard tunnel and watched her haul ice. Every one of them.

And the Chancellor, who had said *death* in that warm, regretful voice and then, with everyone watching, said *life* — and she had told herself it was a mercy, or tried to.

"Does Anakin know?" she said.

"No." Fives's eyes opened. "Rex wants to tell him. Can't figure how. The General's —" He hesitated. "Close to the Chancellor, these days. Rex says he talks about him a lot."

The doorframe creaked. Ventress was gone. Through the gap in the wall Ahsoka could see her out on the rail, her back to the bunkhouse, her pale head bowed a little. In the Force she had gone very still and very tight, like a wire pulled to its limit.

Ahsoka thought she understood that, a little. Ventress knew what it was to be made into a blade by someone who praised you while he did it.

She gave Fives the second injector from Rex's kit. He grimaced, and then the fever's grip loosened enough for him to look almost like himself.

"Whoever did it to you," he said drowsily. "The hangar. The troopers outside your cell. Rex and I walked that night over and over. Never fit. Whoever killed those three walked right into the Guard's own building and walked right back out. Rex always said it had to be someone they'd wave through without blinking." His eyes drifted shut again. "Someone they'd never think to look at twice."

"Sleep, Fives."

"Yes, Commander."

He was asleep before she'd finished packing the kit.

She sat on the floor beside the bunk for a long time afterward, her back against the cold wall, her palms resting on her knees. They still felt warm from the healing. The bacta had left them faintly sticky, and the smell of it — green and sharp and clean — was the smell of every medcenter she'd ever been patched up in.

It was the smell of Narkis, too. Of the infirmary on Ring Two, and Barriss's hands on her split shin, warm, steady, so careful. Barriss bent over her the way she had just bent over Fives, listening for the wrongness under the skin.

And afterward — Ahsoka remembered it exactly — Barriss's hands had shaken.

She'd thought it was the cold.

* * *

Night on Florrum was never fully dark. The geysers lit the low cloud from beneath, and the canyon walls held a dull orange glow like the inside of a forge gone to embers. Ahsoka went out past the hoppers and sat at the edge of the capped shaft, where the plate durasteel still held a little of the day's heat, and she let herself look.

It was the first time in two days. It was the first time in four months. She had been walking away from this since the Well — since Merrick Tal's voice through the vent, telling her that whoever had really done it knew exactly where she was. She'd heard him, and asked the question — *who knew?* — and every time the answer had begun to take shape, she'd stood up inside herself and left the room.

She didn't stand up now. She made herself sit.

Who knew where she would go?

She had been running. The lower levels, her own face on every public screen, Anakin and Rex and Fox all somewhere above her, and Ventress — of all people — at her side. She had needed to know where the nano-droids came from, and she hadn't trusted anyone. Except one. She had opened her comlink in a stinking service corridor and called the one person in the Temple she was sure of, and a voice she loved had told her: an old munitions warehouse, down in the lower levels. Go and see.

And when they went and saw, someone was waiting.

Ventress had said it in Hondo's storeroom. Not followed. *Waited.*

There are a hundred ways, Ahsoka told herself. The channel could have been monitored. Barriss could have told someone — Luminara, a Council member, anyone she thought would help.

But she'd begged Barriss to tell no one. And Ahsoka, in her turn, had promised never to name the friend who sent her there — not to Tarkin, not to Padmé, not to anyone — and she had kept that promise in a tribunal chamber with her life on the table, because she had believed her friend would be in danger if she didn't.

She'd protected Barriss. From the Republic. With her own neck.

Next.

Who had visited her cell?

Anakin. Padmé. Fox and his Guards. And Barriss, before the trial, to heal her wrists — no. Before that. The first time had been before the escape. Barriss had come down to the detention level with her hood up and sat outside the energy field and been so gentle, so sorry, and had said in that soft way of hers that the Council was already deciding what to do with Ahsoka, that no one was coming. And then she'd gone, and some time later a keycard had appeared where no keycard should be.

Ahsoka had thought it was Anakin. She'd been so sure it was Anakin, breaking the rules for her the way he always did, that she hadn't asked him until the day she was expelled, and when she finally did, he had looked at her with honest confusion and said no.

She had let it go. A sympathetic Guard, she'd decided. Someone in the building.

*Someone they'd wave through without blinking.*

Three troopers dead outside her cell, cut down with her own sabers — the sabers Fox had taken from her at the door of Tarkin's base and that had never been hers again. Someone had walked in, gotten hold of them, killed three men with them, and left her a keycard and an open door. Someone had made that corridor look exactly like what a desperate Padawan would do. And she, desperate, had done it. She had walked out among the bodies and run, because staying felt like dying and running felt like a chance.

It had never been a chance. It had been the second half of the trap. The visit had been the first.

Her lekku felt tight; she realized she was hunched like something freezing. She made herself straighten.

Who could fight her like that?

The warehouse. The masked figure with Ventress's red blades. She'd fought it among the crates and lost, and she'd told herself at the time that it was the dark, and the shock, and an unfamiliar style. But it hadn't been unfamiliar. That was what had bothered her in the Well, on the nights she let herself remember the fight at all. It had defended like water. Patient. Circling. Never overextending, never giving her a single thing to hit, just waiting for the opening she always gave in the end.

Anakin had seen it on the Dovrin platform. He'd come to her cell and told her, low so the Guards wouldn't hear: a Soresu turn. Temple training.

*You always give me one,* Barriss had said once, laughing, in a practice salle the year after Geonosis, when Ahsoka had come in too fast for the third time and ended up flat on her back. *An opening. Every single time. I only have to wait for it.*

Ahsoka put her hands over her face.

Not yet, she thought. That's three things. Three things isn't proof. Half the Order learned Soresu. Luminara taught it — Barriss had learned it from her, yes, but so had others. The Temple was full of patient duelists.

And green hands. A hooded buyer at Dalo Pesk's stall, paying for nano-droids. Green, Ventress had said, and patterned. As if it were a detail, a scrap of description to hand over and forget.

There are Mirialans all over the Core, Ahsoka thought. There are — Luminara is — there are other species with—

She heard how it sounded. She stopped.

That was the trouble with the thought she'd spent four months not thinking. She was a good investigator. Anakin had made her one, and Obi-Wan, and Rex; they had taught her to look at a scene and see what was missing, to ask who had access, who knew, who gained. If anyone else had brought her these things — a comlink call, a keycard, a defensive form, a pair of hands — she'd have seen it in a heartbeat. She'd have been proud of herself.

She'd have been sure.

And there was still the last thing. The thing that wasn't evidence at all.

Narkis, day ninety-two. Barriss in her healer's robes, come all the way out to the edge of Wild Space on a prison circuit nobody wanted, sitting by her cot. They had talked about Geonosis — the tunnels, the worm, Barriss's hands at her throat with something else looking out through her eyes, and Ahsoka dragging her back from it. They had almost laughed about it, the way you could about a thing you'd both survived. And Barriss had said, so quietly, that she lit candles every morning. Four. The three troopers Ahsoka had asked her to remember, and one more.

Ahsoka had thought — had let herself think, warm with it, grateful — that the fourth was for Letta Turmond. For the woman who'd died in front of her, accused and terrified, insisting that a Jedi was behind it all. A candle for someone no one in the Temple would ever mourn. She'd loved Barriss for it.

But the Temple had its own candles for the six Jedi lost in the hangar. Everyone lit for them. Barriss's four were her own — unmarked, private. Three she had been asked for.

The fourth, no one had asked for.

Either it was for Letta, whose throat had closed before she could say which Jedi, or it was for the hangar worker who'd carried a bomb he never knew he had — the one person in this whole story more innocent than Ahsoka herself. Either way, it was a candle for someone Barriss had no reason to mourn.

Unless she'd killed them.

People didn't light candles every morning for strangers. They lit them for the ones they couldn't stop seeing.

Ahsoka sat on the lip of the capped shaft with the warm durasteel under her and the orange cloud overhead, and the thing she had been holding the door against for four months walked in and sat down beside her, and it had Barriss's face.

She didn't make a sound. She had imagined, once or twice in the Well, how it would feel to learn who'd done it. She'd imagined rage, the clean hot kind, something she could stand on. There wasn't any. There was only a hollowing, as if someone had reached in and taken out everything behind her ribs and left the shape.

Barriss had sat outside her cell and been kind.

Barriss had sent her to the warehouse.

Barriss had fought her among the crates with Ventress's red blades, and won, and left her there for the Guard to find, and gone home, and lit a candle.

Barriss had come to the tribunal. Ahsoka had looked up at the Jedi gallery once during her own testimony, searching for one friendly face, and found her there.

Barriss had come to Narkis. Barriss had held her shin in both hands and closed the wound and eased the burning under the collar and asked how she was sleeping. Barriss had listened to her talk about trying not to hate the Council, and nodded. And when Ahsoka had warned her — had actually warned her, frightened for her, *be careful, someone is cleaning up behind this* — Barriss had gone grey, and thanked her, and left with her hands shaking.

Not the cold. Never the cold.

A geyser went up somewhere with a long hiss, and for a moment the canyon walls flared bright. Ahsoka pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until colors bloomed behind them, and her shoulders shook, once, twice, and then she held still. She'd learned stillness in the Well. She'd learned you could carry a weight that ought to break you, if you stopped trying to set it down and let your bones take it.

She had saved Barriss's life on Geonosis. She'd always thought that was the end of that story: the worm, the tunnels, her own hand pulling her friend out of the dark. And for one sick, hopeful moment, sitting there, she reached for it — maybe something had been in Barriss's head again. A worm. A chip. Something that made you do what you'd never do, the way Fives said it had been with Tup. Maybe—

A worm didn't light candles. A chip didn't come back to visit the cell, or fly to the edge of Wild Space to heal the person it had buried there. Whatever Barriss had done, she had chosen it. And every morning since, kneeling in front of four small flames, she had chosen it again.

"You've finally looked," Ventress said behind her.

Ahsoka didn't turn. She hadn't heard her come; she hadn't been listening for anything. Ventress came around the edge of the shaft cover and lowered herself onto the warm plate an arm's length away, rifle across her knees, and looked out at the glowing canyon instead of at her. It was almost tactful.

"I felt it from the bunkhouse," Ventress said. "Like a building coming down. Very quietly."

"Don't."

"I'm not mocking you." A pause. "For once."

They sat. The geyser subsided. Far up the rail line something small scuttled over the stones and went still.

"Say it," Ventress said.

Ahsoka's throat closed. She had kept the promise in front of the Republic. She had kept it in front of Anakin while a steel cup crumpled on the table between them. She'd kept it alone in the Well for ten days. It turned out that letting it go was harder than any of that.

"Barriss," she said. "Barriss Offee. Master Luminara's Padawan." Her voice came out level and strange, like someone else's. "She's a healer. She's one of the best duelists in the Temple. She's my friend."

Ventress turned the name over. "The one who sent you to the warehouse."

"Yes."

"The one with the green hands."

"Mirialan," Ahsoka said. "The backs of her hands are patterned. Little diamonds. I used to—" She stopped. She had used to think they were beautiful. "Yes."

Ventress let out a long breath through her nose. "Well," she said. "There's our ghost."

"I can't prove it."

"You know it."

"I know it," Ahsoka said. "And what I have is your word about a hooded buyer — the word of a bounty hunter and former Separatist assassin, which any tribunal in the Republic will weigh against the word of a Jedi healer from the Temple clinic. And a dead Rodian with no marks on him and no recording." Her mouth twisted. "That's all. It's less than Tarkin had on me."

"Tarkin had footage."

"Tarkin had footage of my back, and my hand moving, and a woman dying." She laughed, a short dry sound with nothing in it. "And it was enough."

Ventress was quiet a moment. "So what do you want? Proof?"

"I want—" Ahsoka shut her eyes. What did she want? She wanted Barriss to have said it. She wanted to go back to that cell on the detention level and hear her say it there, while there was still time for it to matter. She wanted back the years when Barriss had only been Barriss. "Something that can't be argued with. Something she can't explain away and the Council can't look past."

"Like my sabers," Ventress said.

Ahsoka opened her eyes.

"She took them," Ventress said. "She used them on you. Then what? Melt them down? Drop them in a reactor?"

"No." The answer came from somewhere below thought; she knew it the way she knew Barriss's guard. "No. She'd never destroy them. Kyber's alive. She'd feel the crystals every second she held them — she couldn't crush them any more than she could crush a bird in her hand. She'd hide them. Somewhere no one would ever look, and she'd tell herself she'd deal with it tomorrow." Her voice cracked on that, and she pushed through. "Somewhere close. Somewhere she could reach. Under the Temple, probably. Down in the Temple's depths — the old foundations, the crawlways nobody's walked in—"

Ventress went rigid.

Ahsoka felt it before she saw it: the hard bright knot of Ventress's presence drawing suddenly tight, the way a hunting animal's whole body goes still at a sound in the grass. Ventress's head had come up. Her pale hands had closed on the rifle stock hard enough to make it creak.

"Say that again," Ventress said.

"The Temple's depths. The foundations. Under—"

"There." Barely a breath. Ventress's eyes were unfocused, looking at nothing; the Force around her had narrowed to a needle. "Oh, *there* you are."

"Ventress?"

"For months," Ventress said, very low, "they've been a whisper. A pull. Somewhere on Coruscant, that much and no more — like trying to hear your own name called across a city." She drew in a slow breath and let it out through her teeth. "And you say *depths*, and they turn over. Like a sleeper hearing its name. Down. Under a great deal of stone." Her lips drew back from her teeth in something that was not quite a smile. "Under your precious Temple."

The hunger in her was so naked it was almost hard to look at. Ahsoka looked anyway.

"That's not proof either," she said.

"It's my sabers in the cellar of the Jedi Temple," Ventress said. "Where a bounty hunter couldn't have put them and a Jedi could. If that isn't proof, little Jedi, it will certainly start a conversation."

Ahsoka said nothing. She thought of the Temple at dawn, the long light through the high windows, Barriss kneeling in front of four small flames. She thought of Barriss going down in the night to check on the things she'd hidden, the way you'd keep touching a bruise.

She had been gone long enough that she'd stopped calling the Temple home. She hadn't understood until now that some part of her had still been waiting to be called back to it. That part was quiet now. It had stopped waiting.

"Go and sit with your clone," Ventress said, and stood. "I'll take the watch. You're no use to me like this."

It was, Ahsoka understood after she'd gone, the kindest thing Ventress knew how to say.

* * *

She didn't sleep. She sat by Fives's bunk through the last hours of the night, listening to him breathe — rough, but steady — and gave him the third injector when the fever climbed again toward dawn. Twice he muttered something with Rex's name in it. Once he said Tup's.

When the cloud over the canyon began to turn from orange to the dirty gold of a Florrum morning, she went out to the crusher roof again and sat cross-legged, and laid the borrowed saber across her knees, and opened herself to the Force because she didn't know what else to do with her hands.

It was a mistake.

He was there.

Not near, not yet. But there — at the far rim of everything, where a few nights ago there had been only a faint pressure, like weather on the other side of a mountain. Now it had a shape. A storm, coiled and burning and grieving, so familiar it hurt to touch. Yesterday it had been somewhere out past the stars. This morning it was closer. Much closer.

And it was turning toward her, the way a compass needle swings and settles.

She felt the instant he felt her. A jolt across the bond, raw and astonished, like someone grabbing for a hand in the dark — and then, terribly, joy.

She slammed herself shut. Too late. She knew it was too late.

Ahsoka was on her feet on the roof with the hilt in her fist and her heart slamming against her ribs. Below, Ventress had already stepped out of the bunkhouse doorway with the rifle in her hands, her face tipped up toward the yellow seam of sky.

"What is it?" Ventress said. "What did you just do?"

Ahsoka didn't answer. She was looking up too, past the canyon rim, into the gold, where nothing showed yet.

"Skyguy," she whispered, and it hurt exactly as much as she'd known it would.

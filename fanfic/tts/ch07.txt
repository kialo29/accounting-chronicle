Chapter 7. The Deep.

The collar came out of its crate in two curved halves, nested in grey foam like something precious, and the technician lifted them into the cabin light the way a jeweler lifts a setting to look for flaws.

"Chin down, please," he said. "It's cold for a moment. After that you won't notice it at all."

Ah-soka didn't move. She sat on the steel bench bolted along the transport's bulkhead, her binders locked to a ring between her boots, and looked at the thing in his hands. Grey metal, dull, no wider than two fingers. A seam where the halves would meet. A small glass eye set at the front, dark for now, waiting.

They had been in hyperspace for nine hours. The Judiciary transport had no viewports in the hold, only a long strip of white lights that buzzed when the hull flexed, and twenty-four men of the Core-uh-sant Guard sitting in rows along the opposite wall with their rifles upright between their knees. She had felt them since Platform Six. Not their faces, which were helmets, but the steady pressure of them in the Force: a cold, contained hostility, like standing beside a sealed reactor. Three of their brothers had died outside her cell with her lightsabers in the wounds. Nothing said in the tribunal had changed what they felt when they looked at her.

She didn't blame them. That was the part she couldn't get past. If she'd been one of them, she'd have hated her too.

"Prisoner." The sergeant at the hatch had red markings on his pauldron and a voice like a closed door. "Chin down."

She put her chin down.

The metal was colder than she'd expected. It touched the back of her neck below her mon-trahls, then the sides of her throat, and the technician's gloved fingers fitted the halves together with a fussy precision that made her want to bite him. There was a click. A second click. A faint hiss as the seam sealed itself, and then the collar sat against her skin as if it had been grown there.

"Registering," the technician murmured, tapping his datapad. "Keyed to the facility master. The warden will hold the control. And, one moment, activation."

She had a moment. She knew it, the way you know the last step before a drop.

So she used it. She closed her eyes and opened herself, all the way, the way she hadn't dared to since the verdict, because she had been afraid of what she'd feel. She let it come. The hyperdrive's deep thrum, not as noise but as something almost alive, a note held under everything. The Guard troopers, each one distinct now that she was looking: the sergeant's tight, banked anger; the trooper nearest the bench, who was very young and, under the anger, afraid of her; the one at the end of the row who was thinking about something else entirely, someone else, far away. The technician's eager pride, bright as a struck match. Beyond the hull, hyperspace itself, a current she'd ridden a hundred times and never once really listened to. And somewhere behind all of it, thin as a thread and getting thinner, a pull back toward Core-uh-sant that she didn't let herself name.

She had been three years old when Plo Koon found her. The Force had been there before that. It had been there before her name.

The light came on. Blue.

It wasn't like a door closing. A door closing made a sound. This was the opposite of a sound, the sudden absence of one she hadn't known she'd been hearing her whole life. Like a hum in a room that stops, and only when it stops do you realize it was there, and that the silence underneath it is enormous.

The sergeant's anger was gone. Not gone, he was still sitting there, rifle across his chest, still angry, she was sure of it. She just couldn't feel it. The young trooper was only armor. The technician was only a thin man with ink on his cuffs. The hyperdrive was noise. The ship was a box. She was inside a box, and the box was inside nothing.

She reached. She didn't decide to; it was reflex, the way a hand goes out in the dark for a wall. She reached, and reached, and there was no wall. There was no dark. There was nothing to reach into.

Her breath went short. Her lekoo drew tight against her back and her hands closed so hard on the binder chain that the links bit her palms. She could still feel the shape of the hold around her, the ceiling close above her mon-trahls, the bulkhead at her shoulder, that much was her own body, and she clung to it the way someone overboard clings to wreckage. But under it, where the Force had always been, there was a silence so complete it felt like a wound.

"There," the technician said. "Readings are clean. Suppression at full." He looked up at her, interested. "How does it feel?"

She opened her mouth. It took her two tries.

"Quiet," she said.

He smiled, delighted, and made a note. "That's exactly what it ought to feel like."

The young trooper by the bench turned his helmet toward the far wall and kept it there.

They made two more jumps. Thirty hours, maybe more; there was no chrono in the hold and she lost count after the second meal. She tried to meditate and found that meditation without the Force was sitting very still in a cold room while your heart pounded. She stopped trying. On the third jump she tried again, out of something like hope, and the silence was worse for having been tested. After that she counted rivets. There were four hundred and twelve on the bulkhead opposite. She counted them nine times and got four hundred and twelve every time, and it was the only thing in the galaxy that seemed to be holding still.

When the transport finally dropped out of hyperspace, someone opened the cockpit hatch and a pale blue light fell across the hold. The young trooper leaned to look forward through it, and she heard him say, under his breath, "Stars. That's bleak."

She leaned too. Through the hatch and the cockpit canopy beyond it hung a moon the color of old ice, white streaked with blue, hard-edged against nothing. A dim orange sun sat on its horizon like something that had given up halfway through rising. No city lights. No lights at all, except one dark pock in the glacier, ringed in faint amber, getting larger.

Narkis. The edge of Wild Space. The place they'd chosen instead of killing her.

The light at her throat blinked, slow and patient, as if it were counting.

Scene break.

The cold reached her before the ramp had finished lowering.

It came up the incline like water, through the grey coat, through the thermals beneath it, straight to the bone. Her first breath of it burned. Her second made her lekoo ache all the way to the tips. Toe-groota came from a warm world; she had fought on Hoth-cold planets before, but always with the Force banking a little heat in her, the way you'd shield a candle with your hand. There was no hand now.

The hangar had been cut into the glacier itself. Its walls were ice, blue-white, veined with darker bands where centuries of snowfall had compressed into layers, and the roof was a durasteel dome set into the ice like a lid. Amber floodlights hung from it on chains. Everything was frosted, the deck, the cargo cranes, the rails along the gantries, and everything breathed: guards, mechanics, the six clone troopers in insulated grey-white armor standing at the hangar's edge, all of them trailing white plumes like small engines.

Judiciary guards met the ramp. They wore dark grey greatcoats and padded helmets with amber visors, and they did not look at her the way the Guard troopers had. They looked at her the way you'd look at cargo that had arrived with a warning label.

The Guard sergeant handed a datapad down to one of them. She saw him hesitate on the last line before he signed it, and then he signed it and walked back up the ramp without a word, and the transport's hatch began to close behind him.

"Twenty-two fourteen."

The woman at the foot of the ramp was the only person in the hangar not wearing a greatcoat. She wore a uniform of olive-grey, every seam pressed, the collar fastened to the throat, as if the cold had been informed it was not to touch her and was complying. Fifties, Ah-soka guessed. Human. Iron-grey hair drawn back into a knot so tight it seemed to hold her face in place. She had a datapad in one hand and nothing at all in her expression.

Ah-soka looked down at her own coat. Stenciled across the left breast in black: NK9-2214.

"That's me," she said.

"That's you. Your name is in the file and it will stay there. Here you are a number. That isn't an insult. It's the same for every person in this facility, including people who were once considerably more important than you." The woman did not offer her hand. "I am Commandant Ilsa Vorne. I am the warden of Narkis-Nine. The inmates call it the Deep. I prefer the designation, but you'll hear both. Walk with me."

One of the guards took Ah-soka's elbow and pushed, hard enough to make her stumble on the frosted ramp.

"Sergeant Holt." Vorne didn't raise her voice. "She's walking. You needn't help her."

The hand let go.

They went through a pressure door into a corridor where the cold was very slightly less cold, and Vorne walked a half-step ahead with her datapad and spoke the way someone reads a manifest.

"Three counts a day. You will be at your cell grate for each. Labor shifts are ten hours. You'll be assigned to the ice cut in two days, once medical clears you. Meals on Ring Two at the posted times. If you miss a meal, you've missed it. Holo-net access one hour a week, monitored. Correspondence by permission, also monitored." She glanced back. "You were a soldier. You understand schedules."

"I was a Jedi," Ah-soka said.

"You were a commander in the Grand Army of the Republic, which is the only part of your former life the Republic recognizes, and the part it has now concluded." Vorne stopped at a second door and turned. "Hold still."

She stepped close and touched the collar's light with one gloved fingertip, as if checking for dust. Then she unclipped a small black cylinder from her belt, no longer than a finger, a single recessed stud at one end, and pressed it once. The blue light flickered and steadied.

"This is keyed to my office and to my person," Vorne said. "There is no other control on this moon. If the collar is damaged or tampered with, my office knows within a second, and you will spend thirty days in the Well." She clipped the cylinder back onto her belt. "The Well is the bottom of the shaft. It is isolation. I don't recommend it."

"Do you put a lot of Jedi in collars?"

"You are the first Force-user this facility has held. You may be one of the first anywhere to wear that device for any length of time." Vorne's voice did not change. "I requested it because I don't intend for your presence here to become a problem. Admiral Tarkin recommended this facility to the tribunal. He recommended me. I served under him for six years on the Outer Rim, and I don't intend to disappoint him." She paused, very slightly. "Neither do I intend to be cruel to you. I want you to understand that those two things are not in conflict."

Ah-soka looked at her. At the pressed seams and the tight knot of hair and the eyes that held no interest in her at all, beyond whether she would be a problem.

"The other prisoners," she said. "Do they know who I am?"

"They have the Holo-net. Your trial was extensively covered."

"Then you know what they'll want."

"I know exactly what they'll want. What they want, and what they're permitted to do about it, is the business of my guards." Vorne turned back to the door. "It isn't yours. You are not a commander here, Twenty-two fourteen. You don't get to worry about anyone but yourself."

Ah-soka thought of Rex in the cell doorway, his salute stalling halfway up. Of the order she'd given him.

"I was never very good at that," she said.

"You'll learn." The door opened onto a gust of colder air and a sound like wind in a long pipe. "Everyone here does."

Medical was a Twy-lek droid-tech who scanned her twice, frowned at the collar readings, and said nothing. Supply issued her one thermal blanket, rated for conditions she suspected it had never met; one cup; one bowl; one spoon, short-handled, soft metal. A holo was taken. Her fingers were printed. Somebody read her the facility regulations aloud, all forty-one of them, while she stood on a painted square.

Then the guards walked her out onto the shaft.

It was the size of a city block across, a vast round hole bored straight down into the glacier, and the prison was built around its walls. Cell galleries ringed it in tiers, grated walkways bolted into the ice, each ring a little narrower than the one above, so that looking down was like looking into a funnel. Orange heat strips ran along the walls at waist height and did almost nothing. Frost furred every rail. Far overhead, a disc of blue twilight showed through the dome; far below, the shaft narrowed into darkness, and at the very bottom there was a single small square of white light, like a coin at the bottom of a well.

"Ring One is administration," Holt said, in the bored voice of someone who'd given this tour a hundred times. "Ring Two, mess and medical, and the tunnel to the Yard. Three through Five, cells. Below Five is the Well." He jabbed a gloved thumb downward. "You don't want the Well."

"So I keep hearing."

He gave her a look through the amber visor and didn't answer.

Her cell was on Ring Four. A number on the frosted lintel: 4-17. Three walls of ice sealed under a clear coating that sweated in the warmth of the heat strip and froze again when it faded. A shelf cut into the ice for a bunk. A grate for a door, so that everyone on the gallery could see in, and she could see out, across the shaft, down the funnel, up to the dome.

The grate slid shut behind her. The lock engaged with a sound she felt in her teeth.

Scene break.

The mess on Ring Two went quiet when she walked in.

Not all at once. It went quiet the way ice cracks, a line of silence running out from the door across the long durasteel tables, one conversation stopping, then the next, until the only sound in the hall was the scrape of the serving droid's ladle and somebody's spoon set down very deliberately on a tray.

There were maybe two hundred of them. Every species she'd ever fought beside or against: humans and Rodians, a pair of Aqualish, a Wee-kway with half his face frostbitten grey, Sullustans, a Gran, a broad-shouldered Zabrak with tattoos faded to the color of a bruise. All in the same grey thermals. All looking at her.

She took a tray. She held it out. The droid filled her bowl with something beige that steamed, and she turned to find a seat, and somebody said, loud enough to carry, "There she is. The Temple's little bomber."

She kept walking.

"Killed her own troopers." Another voice. "Cut 'em down in the corridor and walked out over the bodies."

"Heard she cried at sentencing."

"I'd cry too. Jedi don't do time. Jedi do the sentencing."

The Zabrak stood up as she passed his table. He didn't step into her way. He just stood, so she had to pass close, and said quietly, "My brother served under one of your generals. Three hundred men in his company. Your general walked off that field with a scratch on his arm." He looked at the collar. "Hope it chokes you."

She didn't stop. She didn't answer. She was aware, distantly, the way you're aware of weather, that her hands were steady on the tray, and that this was the only thing she had any control over at all.

There was an empty table at the far end, near the heat vent that didn't work. She sat with her back to the ice wall and ate, because the regulations said if you missed a meal you'd missed it, and because the beige stuff was hot, and because Rex had once told her that the first rule of any bad posting was eat what they give you while it's warm.

She had three spoonfuls before the bench across from her creaked under a great deal of weight.

The Trandoshan who sat down was big even for his kind, his scales a dun, mottled green, a notch cut out of the ridge above his left eye where something had once bitten or burned it. He still wore a labor harness over his thermals, its buckles rimed with frost. He didn't have a tray. He folded his clawed hands on the table and looked at her for a long moment with his slit-pupiled eyes, and then he leaned forward and breathed in, slowly, through his nostril slits.

"Wasskah," he said.

Every muscle in her back went tight.

"You remember." He sounded pleased. "I can smell that you remember. The hunting moon. The lodge in the trees." His tongue flicked once. "Garnac was my father's brother. I am Krasso. I was to go to Wasskah the next season, to hunt beside him. I was to earn my first Jedi pelt for his wall." He tilted his head. "Instead there was a message that he was dead. And a name."

"Your uncle hunted children," Ah-soka said. "For sport. He dropped them in a forest and ran them down."

"He hunted Jedi." Krasso's voice didn't rise. "You were never children."

She was reaching before she knew it, reaching to read him, to feel which way the threat would move, the way she'd done in every fight since she was old enough to hold a training saber. She reached, and hit the silence, and in the half-second it cost her, his hand shot across the table and closed on the collar.

He hauled. She came half over the table, tray clattering, the edge of the collar biting up under her jaw so hard that light burst behind her eyes. The status light flickered against his knuckles. Someone was laughing. Someone was cheering.

Her body didn't need the Force for the next part. It had been trained by Anakin Skywalker, who did not believe in fighting fair. She drove the heel of her hand up into the soft joint of Krasso's wrist, twisted against the thumb, and turned her whole weight out from under the grip. He let go, she didn't know if she'd made him or if he'd chosen to, and she landed on her own side of the table on her knees, gasping, with her hand at her throat.

Krasso hadn't moved from his seat. He was looking at his wrist, flexing it, and his lipless mouth had pulled back from his teeth.

"Good," he said. "The Scorekeeper prefers prey that fights."

On the gallery above the mess, two Judiciary guards stood at the rail with their rifles slung, watching. Neither of them moved.

"Krasso." A new voice, dry and rasping, from behind Ah-soka's shoulder. "You're sitting in my light."

The woman who'd come up to the table was human, and old, sixties at least, with white hair cropped close to a skull that looked like it had been in a few fights of its own. Her face was weathered into a permanent squint, the kind spacers got from too many years staring through bad viewports. Two fingers of her left hand were stiff and slightly darker than the rest, old frostbite, and she was holding her tray in the other hand as if she'd never had a problem in her life.

Krasso looked up at her. Something passed between them that Ah-soka couldn't read, and couldn't feel, and hated not feeling.

"Varro," he said.

"That's right. And you've got a shift at the cut in an hour, and Holt's watching, and Holt's in a mood because his heat strip's out. So." The woman tilted her head toward the door. "Go on."

Krasso stood. He looked down at Ah-soka on the floor for a long moment.

"The cold is long, little hunter," he said. "I am patient."

He went. The mess went back to its noise, a little louder than before, as if to make up for the show ending early. The old woman set her tray down where Krasso had been sitting, lowered herself onto the bench with a grunt, and started eating.

Ah-soka got up off the floor. She picked her bowl up. Half of it was still in it. She sat down.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't." The woman didn't look up. "Krasso kills you, there's a lockdown. Lockdown, nobody moves. Nobody moves, no trade. No trade, I don't get my heat packs. I hate being cold." She chewed. "Tess Varro. Life. A Hutt's enforcer, on Nar Shaddaa, before you ask. He had it coming, and he had it coming at me."

"I wasn't going to ask."

"Everybody asks eventually. Gets it out of the way." Tess looked at her for the first time. It was a long look, thorough, the look of someone pricing a cargo. "You're the Jedi."

"Not anymore."

"Good. Keep practicing that. It almost sounds true." Tess went back to her bowl. "Here's what's free, and then after this, nothing's free. One. Nobody in here is innocent. Stop looking like you are. It makes people want to fix that."

"I am innocent."

"Sweetheart, so's every third person in this room, and the other two-thirds are lying about it. I'm not. I killed him. Makes a nice change, doesn't it?" She pointed her spoon. "Two. Eat all of it, every time, even when it tastes like that. Especially when it tastes like that. Three. The cold takes the ones who sit still. You'll want to curl up in your bunk and not come out. Don't." The spoon pointed at the collar. "Four. Down here, a Jedi isn't a hero. A Jedi's the face on the recruiting poster that got your son killed. Every one of these people lost somebody to this war, and you're the closest thing to the people who ran it they're ever going to get their hands on."

Ah-soka looked down at her bowl. Her throat ached where the collar had bitten. "And five?"

"Five's not free." Tess scraped her bowl clean. Then, a little less dry: "That thing. What's it like?"

Ah-soka was quiet for a while.

"Quiet," she said finally.

Tess looked at her, and for just a moment the squint went somewhere softer, and then it came back.

"Yeah," Tess said. "I figured." She stood, tray in her good hand. "You want to feel better about your evening, look down the shaft tonight after lights. Bottom of the Well. There's one fellow in this place they hate worse than you. Separatist officer, Raxus man. Republic caught one of the high-collar types alive, which I hear doesn't happen much, and nobody knows what to do with him, so they keep him down there by himself with the lights on and wait to see if he breaks." She shrugged. "Eleven months, so far. He hasn't."

She walked off. Ah-soka sat alone at the end of the table with half a bowl of beige food going cold in front of her, and ate all of it.

Scene break.

The lights went down on the shaft at what the regulations called night. It made no difference to the sky. The disc of blue twilight at the top of the dome stayed exactly the same color, the way it had stayed the same color all day, and would, she understood, stay the same color for as long as she was here.

The cold came into the cell the moment the heat strip dimmed to its night setting. It came through the blanket, through the coat she wore under the blanket, through the thermals under that. It found her lekoo first and settled into them like a hand closing. She curled on the ice shelf with her knees to her chest and her hands tucked into her armpits and shook, and tried to remember the last time she had been truly cold without being able to do anything about it.

She couldn't. That was its own kind of answer.

She didn't mean to sleep. She slept.

In the dream she was in the Temple hangar, before. She had never been there before, she'd come afterward, with Anakin, to the smoke and the scorched deck and the sheets laid over shapes, but in the dream she was there in time, standing on the deck while the mechanics worked and the gunships idled and Jedi walked past her talking about nothing. She knew what was coming. She opened her mouth to warn them and nothing came out. She reached for the Force to throw them clear and there was nothing to reach for, and then the light came, white, rolling, and the sound came after it, and she was standing in fire that did not burn her while it burned everyone else.

Then it was the corridor outside her cell, and the three troopers were on the floor, and her lightsabers were in her hands, and she didn't remember picking them up. Then it was Letta Turmond's throat, closing on nothing. And on a catwalk above all of it, as there was always someone above all of it, the bone-white mask, tilted slightly to one side, watching her with interest, the way the technician had.

She woke with her hand already at her own throat.

The cell was dark except for one thing. The light on the collar threw a faint blue glow across the ice in front of her face, and her breath went up through it in short, shredded plumes. Her heart was going so hard she could feel it in her mon-trahls. She reached, of course she reached; she would reach every time, she understood that now, a thousand times a day for the rest of her life, and there was nothing there, and the nothing went on and on.

Her fingers found the seam of the collar at the back of her neck. They dug in. For one long, bright second she didn't care about Vorne or the Well or thirty days or anything at all except getting it off, getting it off, getting back the thing that had been there before her name, She stopped.

She made herself stop. She unhooked her fingers one at a time and put her hand flat on the ice beside her face, and the cold of it burned, and she held it there.

Then she cried, because there was nothing else left to do. She tried to do it quietly. She didn't manage it very well.

After a while, through the ice wall to her left, a dry voice said, "Everybody does it the first night."

Ah-soka went still.

"Do it in through the nose," Tess's voice went on, muffled, unbothered. "Out through the mouth, slow. Warms the air before it hits your chest. And for stars' sake, do it quieter. Some of us have a shift in the morning."

Silence. Then the creak of a bunk on the other side of the wall as someone rolled over.

Ah-soka lay on her back and looked at the dark.

In through the nose.

She knew that. She'd known it since she was four years old, sitting cross-legged on a mat in the crèche with a dozen other younglings, before any of them had ever lifted so much as a pebble. Sit. Breathe. Count. The crèche master's voice had been patient and very old. The Force will come when you are still. But first, be still. She had always thought the breathing was a door. That it was the thing you did to get to the Force, and once you were through, you didn't need it anymore.

In through the nose. Count four. Out through the mouth. Count six.

It wasn't a door. It was just breathing. There was nothing on the other side of it.

But her heart slowed anyway.

She did it again, and again, and the plumes of her breath grew longer and steadier in the blue light, and the shaking in her hands went from a rattle to a tremor to something she could almost ignore. It turned out that being still was something her body could do on its own. It turned out that it had been doing it for her all along, and she'd been giving the credit to something else.

She lay there and thought, very clearly, the way she used to think on the bridge of a cruiser before an engagement.

Tarkin wanted her finished. The Council had wanted her gone. The Senate had needed someone to be guilty, and she had been standing closest, holding a stun baton in a room full of nano-droids. Somewhere on Core-uh-sant, the person who had actually done it, who had fought like a Jedi, Anakin had said, a Temple turn in the dark, was walking around free. Waking up every morning. Doing whatever they did.

And everyone, everyone in the galaxy who mattered, had agreed that the best thing Ah-soka Tano could do now was disappear quietly into the ice and let it all be finished.

She thought about that for a long time.

Then she sat up on the shelf, pulled the blanket around her shoulders, and decided, with the cold in her bones and the silence where the Force had been, that she was not going to do that.

She was going to live. Not because she had a plan, and not because she believed anyone was coming. She would live because a great many powerful people had decided she shouldn't matter anymore, and she had never once in her life been good at doing what she was told.

She got up. Her legs were stiff. She went to the grate and put her hands on the frozen bars and looked out.

The shaft was dark, ring under ring of black galleries spiraling down, the heat strips glowing faint orange along the walls like embers in a dead fire. Nothing moved. Here and there a cough echoed off the ice. And at the very bottom, where the funnel narrowed to its tightest point, there was the white square she'd seen when she arrived. The Well. The only cell in the Deep with its lights still on.

She could see into it from here, just barely, foreshortened. A bunk. A bare floor. A man sitting on the edge of the bunk, upright, his back very straight. He wore the remains of a uniform, a dark tunic with a high collar, its insignia gone, pale patches on the shoulders where rank had once been stitched. His hair was neat. His hands rested on his knees. He wasn't sleeping. He looked as if he had not slept in a long time, and had decided, with great discipline, that this was not going to be anyone's business but his own.

Eleven months, Tess had said. With the lights on.

As she watched, he lifted his head.

He looked straight up the shaft, past Ring Five, past the dark galleries, up through the cold, and she realized, a beat too late, what he must be seeing. In all that blackness, there was exactly one other light. A small blue one, at the throat of a figure on Ring Four, standing at her grate.

They looked at each other across the whole height of the Deep.

Then the man in the Well stood. He did it the way officers on parade grounds stand, all at once and without waste. He squared his shoulders in the ruined tunic, and he raised his hand, and he saluted her.

It wasn't a Republic salute. She didn't know the form. But she knew what it was. She'd been saluted by clone troopers since she was fourteen years old. Two days ago Rex's salute had stalled halfway before he made himself finish it.

This one didn't stall at all.

Ah-soka stood at the grate of a Republic cell, in a Republic collar, on a moon the Republic had chosen for her, and looked down at the only person in the Deep who had offered her a courtesy.

He was the enemy.

She returned the salute.

End of chapter.

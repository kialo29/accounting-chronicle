Chapter 19. Old Wounds.

The blue blade snapped on between them with a sound like a held breath let go, and Ventress, who had walked unarmed into a pirate nest on purpose, found that her first feeling was not fear but offense. Somebody had handed Tano a lightsaber that sang like a lullaby. It hummed in her fist, placid and blue and completely wrong for her, and she held it in that backward grip as though the hilt had insulted her personally.

"Put that away before you hurt yourself," Ventress said. "It doesn't even like you."

"It doesn't have to like me." Tano didn't lower it. There was a raw ring around her throat, angry pink against the orange, and a small burn under the left side of her jaw. Collar scars. Fresh ones. "What do you want?"

"At the moment? A chair. Something to drink that won't strip the paint off my teeth." Ventress let her gaze drift up to the balcony. "And for your host to stop pointing two pistols at me. One is a threat. Two is just showing off."

"Two," Hondo called down, "is called being prepared! You are the one who walked into my house with no weapons at all. Either you are very stupid or you are very dangerous, and I have heard about you, so I know which." He beamed. "Also, I am told you owe me money."

"I owe you nothing."

"Not yet. But the night is young."

The pirates laughed, a few of them, nervously. None of them lowered their rifles. Ventress could feel each of them in the Force like coals in a grate, little heats of greed and fear and drink. The old woman on the crate was another kind of heat, steadier, banked low. And Tano, Tano was a bonfire somebody had just finished putting out and relighting. The Force poured off her in uneven pulses, too much and then too little, the way blood comes back into a numb hand. Ventress had felt that once in herself, after Dooku had thrown her away and she'd lain half-dead on a ship deck finding out she was still alive. She did not care for the memory, or for recognizing it in someone else.

"You didn't answer me," Tano said.

"I want," Ventress said, "the same thing you want, little girl. I want the creature who wore my face in that warehouse." She touched the empty clips on her belt with two fingers, lightly, the way one touches a bruise to see if it still hurts. It did. "And I want my sabers back. You can keep the Jedi lullaby."

The blade wavered. Only a little. But Ventress had spent her life watching for the moment an opponent's guard thinned, and she saw it.

"Hondo," Tano said, without looking away. "Is there somewhere we can talk?"

"There is everywhere you can talk. It is my base. Every room in it is available, at very reasonable rates." He holstered one pistol, considered, and kept the other. "Juko! Take our new guest's cloak. Check it for knives. Then check it again, because she will have hidden them better the first time."

Scene break.

There had been four knives. Juko found three. Ventress let him keep them as a professional courtesy.

Hondo's idea of a private room was a storage bay off the hangar stacked with crates of stolen ration bars and a single flickering lamp. Someone had dragged in two chairs and a table that stood on three legs and an old fuel canister. Tano sat with the borrowed saber on the table in front of her, unlit, one hand never more than a few centimeters from it. The old woman, Tess, had followed them in uninvited and settled on a crate by the door with her bandaged hand in her lap, and Tano hadn't told her to go. Ventress noted that, and filed it.

"Start with Core-uh-sant," Tano said.

So Ventress did, though she gave it in pieces and kept the worst of it for herself.

She had not gone to Ord Mantell. The flight plan she'd filed with the Marrow Lark's captain had been a gift to Skywalker, who would waste three days on it, and she hoped he had enjoyed every one. Grell had dropped her at a fuel stop in the Bith systems with a hold full of illegal spice and a firm request never to see her again. From there she had worked. Collected bounties. Slept badly. Bought information with the credits and spent the information on more questions.

Because she had spent her whole life being the thing in the dark, and in that warehouse something had come out of a darker dark than hers and put her on the floor. It had moved like a Jedi. Not like Kenobi, no wit in it, no flourish, but with that same infuriating serenity, the whole body turning around a still center. Only the center hadn't been still. That was what had stayed with her through the months, waking her at odd hours in odd ports: the feel of it in the Force, the half second before the blow landed. Calm, yes. Cool water. And beneath the water something gone soft and wrong, like a pool in a garden with a dead thing at the bottom.

"A Jedi," Tano said quietly.

"A Jedi." Ventress leaned back until the chair creaked. "Or something raised by them. You can wash the training out of your hands, but you can't wash it out of your spine. I should know."

Tano looked at the table. Her lekoo had gone very still.

"Before I left Core-uh-sant," Ventress went on, "I found the man who sold the nano-droids. It took me two nights and one broken arm, his bodyguard's, not mine. Rodian. Dalo Pesk. He kept a stall in the Kesh Arcade. Forty-one, with a shutter that screamed."

"Pesk," Tano said. The name meant nothing to her; Ventress watched it mean nothing.

"He remembered the buyer, because she paid in clean credits and Pesk had never seen a clean credit before in his life. Hooded. Spoke very little. Came twice. The first time in gloves. The second time she'd forgotten them, or didn't think a Rodian salvage dealer was worth hiding from." Ventress turned her own hand over on the table, white and long-fingered in the bad light. "Green hands, he said. Green skin, with markings across the backs of them. A pattern. Neat, he said, like somebody had done it with a ruler."

"Lots of species have, "

"Do you know, that's exactly what I told him. He said all you soft-skinned types look the same to him anyway." Ventress's mouth twisted. "He had a cam over the stall. A recording. He wanted two thousand for it, and I didn't have two thousand, and before I could go and take two thousand from somebody who didn't need it, your Skywalker kicked the door in on half the Undercroft looking for me. I had a choice between a datacard and my neck." She shrugged. "When I could risk Core-uh-sant again, weeks later, the stall was shut and Pesk was dead. No mark on him, they said in the arcade. Hands folded in his lap like a good boy. Cam gone. Datapads gone."

"Somebody cleaned up," Tess said from the door.

"Somebody very tidy." Ventress didn't look at her. She was looking at Tano.

Because something had happened in Tano's face, or not in her face, which was holding perfectly level, but beneath it, where Ventress could feel it. It was like watching someone step onto ice and feel it shift. A small, sharp, involuntary recoil. Not from Ventress. From a thought. Tano's eyes had gone, for the space of a heartbeat, somewhere far away, to some memory with green hands in it, and then she flinched from it so hard Ventress felt the snap in the Force, the way one feels a door slammed two rooms over.

Tano picked up the borrowed saber and put it down again a centimeter to the left.

"What about the mask?" she said. Her voice was steady. Too steady. "The mask was yours. Your face. How did they get it?"

Ventress let the silence go on long enough to make clear she had noticed.

"Anyone with eyes and a Holo-net feed knows my face," she said at last. "I'm famous. It's a burden." She tilted her head. "Which brings me to something you told me. That night, in the lower levels, when you were so desperate and so very trusting. You said you had contacted a friend. A friend in the Temple. And your friend sent us to that warehouse, and at that warehouse someone was waiting who knew exactly where to stand."

"No," Tano said.

"I haven't asked anything."

"You're going to. The answer's no." She met Ventress's gaze, and there was the Jedi in her, all the way down, that stubborn, brilliant, idiotic loyalty that made them so easy to kill. "My friend's in danger because of me. Someone is killing everyone who knew anything about those nano-droids. Pesk. Letta. If my friend's alive it's because they're inside the Temple where nobody can reach them. I'm not putting their name in your mouth."

"How noble."

"It's not noble. It's just what I'm doing." Tano's hand closed on the saber hilt. "So. You want your sabers. You want the person who took them. What do you need from me?"

There it was. Ventress almost smiled.

"The sabers are on Core-uh-sant," she said. "I can feel them. Not well, it's like the ache of a pulled tooth, the place where something used to be, but I can feel the direction. Core-uh-sant. And not in some gutter. Somewhere quiet. Somewhere old." She spread her hands. "I need a way into the Jedi Temple that doesn't involve fighting my way through every robed hypocrite in it. You grew up there. You know where the walls are thin."

Tano was quiet a long moment.

"And what I need," she said, "is a witness."

Ventress laughed out loud. It surprised them both.

"A witness. Me. In front of a Republic court." She wiped one eye with a knuckle. "Oh, you've grown a sense of humor in that ice box. Very good."

"I'm serious. You're the only person alive who saw the masked figure take your sabers. The tribunal said it was my accomplice. You know it wasn't. You know it knocked you out." Tano leaned forward. "I'm not asking you to like it. I'm asking you to say what happened."

"To Tarkin."

"To anyone who'll listen. If it comes to that."

"And if it comes to that, they'll arrest me on the spot."

"Probably." Tano held out her hand across the three-legged table. "Better have the real thief standing next to you when they do."

Ventress looked at the hand. Orange, ringed in old calluses, a fresh ice-burn across the palm. Not a Jedi's hand anymore, quite. Something rougher.

She thought of the half second in the warehouse. The dead thing at the bottom of the pool.

She took it.

"If you get sentimental at any point," she said, "I'm leaving."

"Same," said Tano.

Scene break.

Ventress did not sleep. She rarely did in places where she'd been searched, and Hondo's base had a hundred noises she didn't know yet, the boom of the geysers out in the canyons, the drip of condensation from the hangar ribs, pirates singing a song about a Hutt that seemed to have no last verse. She found a ledge on the outer wall above the landing pad, out of the floodlights, and sat there with her knees drawn up and watched the sulfur sky go from yellow to rust-brown to a sickly, starless black.

Below her, Tano had fallen asleep sitting up against a crate with the borrowed saber across her knees. Hondo, on his way past with a bottle, stopped, considered her with his head on one side, and laid a blanket over her shoulders with a delicacy that would have ruined his reputation if Ventress had told anyone. Which she might. Later. When it was useful.

It was nearly the third hour when she saw the old woman.

Tess came out of the barracks door in her borrowed pirate's jacket and crossed the yard without hurrying, the way people do when they very much want to hurry and know it would look wrong. She went to the base of the north tower, where the big comm dish sat hunched against the dark like a beggar's bowl, and began to climb the service ladder. She climbed one-handed. The bandaged hand stayed in her jacket pocket the whole way up.

Ventress watched her the way she might watch a lizard on a wall. Idly. Without much intention.

At the top Tess crouched beside the dish's housing for a long time. When she came down, her pocket hung differently.

None of my business, Ventress told herself. The old woman was Tano's pet, not hers. Perhaps she was sending a message to some grandchild on some miserable little world. Perhaps she was stealing copper from the dish. Pirates' bases were full of people stealing from each other; it was practically the local currency.

But she noticed the small light at the base of the dish afterward, a pinprick, amber, that hadn't been there before. It blinked. Then it waited. Then it blinked again.

She watched it blink forty times before she climbed down to wake Tano. By then the sky over the eastern canyons had begun, very faintly, to scream.

Scene break.

The Justifier came in low and fast out of the sunrise, its engines a raw shriek off the canyon walls, and the first thing it did was shoot the north tower.

The dish went over in a cascade of sparks and wire. The amber light went with it. Ventress, already halfway across the yard with Tano stumbling awake behind her, thought: of course. Never leave the doorbell for the next visitor.

"Hondo!" Tano shouted.

"I see it, I see it!" From somewhere in the hangar came the voice of a man very offended to have been woken. "Who invited Bane? I did not invite Bane! Somebody has very bad manners and it is not me!"

Pirates spilled out of every door, half dressed, rifles in hand. The ship swung around over the pad and its ramp dropped while it was still a meter off the ground. Two figures came down first, the Aqualish, Vosh, with a heavy repeater, and a lean grey-skinned crewman with a shock-net launcher. Behind them, a third figure stepped off the ramp into the air and didn't fall. Rocket boots flared blue. A wide-brimmed hat. A long coat flapping like a carrion bird's wing.

"Mornin'," said Cad Bane, drifting down onto the landing pad. "Didn't mean to wake the household. I've come for my property, and then I'll be on my way."

"I am not your property," Tano said. The blue saber came to life in her hand.

"Wasn't talkin' to you, Commander. Not first, anyhow." Bane's red eyes moved over the pirates, the ruined tower, the yard, and stopped, for half a heartbeat, on Tess standing in the barracks doorway. He touched the brim of his hat to her.

Tess didn't move. But Ventress felt her in the Force, and all that banked, steady heat in her went suddenly cold and small.

Ah, Ventress thought. There you are.

Then Vosh opened up with the repeater, and there was no more time to think about anything.

Ventress had no sabers. She had three fewer knives than she'd come with. What she had was a pirate base full of loose objects and a lifetime's practice at making them hurt. A rifle came out of a dead-drunk Wee-kway's hands and into hers; she didn't bother firing it, only swung it by the barrel into Krinn's jaw as he raised the net launcher. The net went off into the air and came down over two of Hondo's men, who began cursing each other. She reached into the Force and found the repeater in Vosh's hands, heavy, hot, its power pack humming, and wrenched it up and sideways, so that his next burst stitched a line across the Justifier's own hull. He bellowed. She threw a crate of ration bars at his head. It was undignified. It worked.

Across the pad, Tano was moving toward Bane.

She wasn't good with the borrowed saber yet. Ventress could see it, the blade was a fraction too heavy for her, its balance wrong for the reverse grip, and the first pistol bolt she deflected went wild into a fuel tank instead of back at its sender. But she was fast. And she had the Force back, and she was using it like someone who had been starving for months and found a table laid. She leapt. Bane's rocket boots kicked him up and away; she followed him into the air off a stack of cargo, higher than any of the pirates could believe, and her blade sheared the tip off his hat.

"Now that," Bane drawled, landing on the hangar roof, "was a good hat."

He fired twice. She blocked both. He fired at the pirates below instead, and she had to throw herself down in front of them, deflecting, and he laughed.

"That's the trouble with you lot," he called. "Always somebody to save."

He reached into his coat. Ventress, who had been killing people for a long time, knew the gesture of a man reaching for something that wasn't a weapon and mattered more. A small black cylinder came out in his long fingers. He pointed it at Tano, quite casually, and pressed it.

Nothing happened.

Tano straightened slowly. She touched the raw ring around her neck with two fingers, the way Ventress had touched her empty clips the night before.

Bane looked at the cylinder. He looked at her bare throat. And for the first time, Ventress saw him do arithmetic he didn't like.

"Huh," he said, and put the control back in his inner pocket. "Well. Worth a try."

"Hondo," Ventress said, not loudly. "That gun on the hangar roof. The big ugly one. Does it work?"

"Does it work?" Hondo appeared at her elbow out of nowhere, dragging a pirate's bandolier over one shoulder, outraged. "It is the finest gun I have ever stolen! It works magnificently, when it is pointed at something!"

"Point it at his ship."

Hondo looked at her. Then he grinned with every tooth he had, and ran.

Bane saw it too late. He'd been watching Tano, and Tano had seen Ventress's thought arrive and understood it, Ventress felt that too, the uncomfortable brush of another mind meeting hers in the Force, quick and clear. Tano went at him again, harder, driving him off the roof and back toward the pad, and Bane, for all his drawl, was retreating. His boots flared. He hung above the yard, a pistol in each hand, firing down at the blue blade that kept finding every bolt.

Ventress felt for the ground.

Florrum was a world built over its own fury. Under the landing pad, under the hangar foundations, the geysers ran through the rock in hot pressured veins, and Hondo's people had capped the nearest one years ago to feed the base's boilers. She could feel the cap. She could feel the furious old water beneath it, pushing, pushing, the way she had once felt rage beneath her own ribs.

She opened it.

The pad's edge burst upward in a column of boiling, sulfur-yellow steam, straight up into the air where Bane hovered. He screamed, a short, ugly sound, the first honest thing she'd heard out of him, and his boots stuttered and his coat smoked and he dropped out of the cloud trailing vapor, one arm wrapped across his face. He hit the deck of his own ramp hard. On the hangar roof, Hondo's great ugly gun spoke once, twice, and the Justifier's port engine housing came apart in a bright orange flower.

"Vosh!" Bane rasped. "Get us up!"

The Aqualish was already dragging Krinn up the ramp. The ship lurched skyward, listing, black smoke pouring from its side. Bane got to his knees on the closing ramp. His face was blistered down one side and his breathing tubes were hissing. He found Tano in the yard with his eyes and held her there.

Then he looked past her, to the barracks doorway, and fired.

The bolt took Tess low in the side. She folded down onto the threshold without a sound.

"Never pay twice for the same goods," Bane called down, his voice thick and raw. "Tell your friend, Jedi. Next time I come, I'll bring the collar to fit the key."

The ramp sealed. The Justifier shrieked away over the canyons, trailing smoke, until it was a dark speck against the yellow, and then nothing.

Pirates were cheering. Hondo, on the roof, was hugging his gun.

Ventress was watching Tano, who had already dropped the saber and was running toward Tess in the doorway.

Scene break.

It was not a killing wound. Juko said so, with his loupe pushed up on his forehead and his black-stained fingers packing the burn with something that smelled like engine grease and probably was. It had cooked through flesh and missed everything important. She would live, if she lay still and the fever didn't take her. Bane, Ventress suspected, had missed on purpose. Dead traitors made no point. Live ones did.

Tess lay on a cot in the infirmary, a corner of the barracks with more bottles than bandages, with her white hair plastered to her forehead and her bad hand on her chest. Tano sat beside her on an upturned crate. Ventress leaned in the doorway and did not offer to help.

"You don't have to say it," Tess said. Her voice was very thin. "I know you know."

"Say it anyway," said Tano.

Tess closed her eyes.

"The power cell," she said. "From the crates on his ship, at Narkis. It wasn't a power cell. It was one of his homing tags, he puts them on cargo so he can find it again if somebody steals it. I knew what it was when I saw it. I've been in the trade forty years." A breath, shallow and careful. "Its range was rubbish. Needed a big dish. So I asked you about the dish. And you told me."

Tano didn't move.

"He'd left a channel open on it. In case any of his goods wanted to sell themselves back. Funny man." Tess's mouth twitched, though it wasn't a smile. "I called him last night. Told him where you were. He wired the first half before he even got here." Her good hand fumbled at her jacket. A credit chit came out, scuffed and ordinary. "There's enough on here to buy a ship. A small one. And a name that isn't on any Judiciary list. That's what he promised. A name."

"You were going to let him take me," Tano said.

"I was going to let him take you," Tess agreed. "Yes."

The room was very quiet. Out in the yard, the pirates were still celebrating; somebody had found the Hutt song again.

"Sixty-one," Tess said at last. "I'm sixty-one, kid. I was going to die in that ice. I'd made my peace with it, near enough. And then you came, and you held up a mountain with your mind, and I saw what you were worth and I thought, " She stopped. "I'm not going to say I'm sorry. I'd be lying, and you'd know. I'm sorry I got shot. That's about as far as I go."

Ventress waited for the Jedi to come out, the forgiveness, the soft speech about second chances. She had heard it from Kenobi often enough to recite it.

It didn't come.

Tano looked at the credit chit in Tess's hand for a long time. Then she reached out and folded Tess's fingers, the broken one, the two frostbitten ones, all of them, closed around it.

"Keep it," she said. "You earned it. That's what you'd tell me, isn't it? Never let anyone pay you less than you're worth."

Tess's eyes opened. They were wet.

"Hondo will look after you until you can stand," Tano said. "He'll charge you for it. You can afford it now." She stood. "Then buy your ship. Buy your name. Go somewhere warm."

"Kid, "

"Goodbye, Tess."

She walked past Ventress in the doorway without stopping. Her face was perfectly composed. Her lekoo were not; they had drawn in tight against her shoulders like something bracing against cold, and in the Force she felt like a bruise somebody was still pressing.

Ventress followed her out. She told herself it was because she wanted to see what the girl did next. That was nearly true.

Hondo's comm room sat beneath the wreck of the north tower, half its panels dark from the blast. Hondo met them at the hatch, rubbing his hands.

"A great victory," he said. "Bane runs away crying, my gun is a hero, and only some of my men are injured. I will be generous and not charge you for the dish." He paused. "I will charge you for the geyser cap."

"Does the short-range array still reach the relay at the edge of the system?" Tano asked.

"Does it, of course it does. It is a very fine array. Why?"

"I need to send something. Very small."

Hondo looked at her face and, for once, said nothing clever. He stood aside.

Tano sat down at the console. Ventress watched her fingers move, not typing a message, nothing so foolish, but building a string of numbers, short bursts and pauses, a pattern that meant nothing at all to Ventress's eye. It had the shape of something drilled in. Something from a different life.

"What is that?" Ventress said.

"Christophsis. My first week." Tano didn't look up. "The 501st had a code for an officer cut off from her unit. Position, condition, whether she could still fight. Rex made me memorize it in case I ever got myself lost." A breath that wasn't a laugh. "He said with my sense of direction it was only a matter of time."

"Your clone captain. The one who's hunting you."

"The one they've ordered to hunt me." She hesitated over the last sequence. "If anyone in the army still knows that code, it's him. And if he's the one who answers, it'll be because he wants to."

"And if it's not him who answers?"

"Then I'll have told the Republic exactly where I am." Tano's finger hovered over the transmit key. "And we'll leave tonight anyway."

She sent it.

The array clicked and hummed and fell quiet. Somewhere out past the yellow sky, a burst of numbers went out into the dark toward a man who still called her Commander.

Tano sat looking at the dead console with her hands in her lap. Ventress watched her. She was thinking, she couldn't help it, about the storage bay the night before. The lamp. The table on its three legs. The moment she'd said green hands, and that brilliant, stubborn, loyal mind had reached for something and slammed the door on it so hard the whole Force rang.

A friend in the Temple, Ventress thought. Someone who knew where we would be.

Someone with green hands.

She didn't know the name. Tano did, and wouldn't let herself.

Ventress settled her shoulders against the hatch frame and said nothing. There was no hurry. She had been hunting a ghost with green hands for months. It had never occurred to her until now that the girl might have trusted the ghost with her life, and thanked it for the trouble.

End of chapter.

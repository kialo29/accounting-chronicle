Chapter 9. Garnac's Blood.

On her third day in the ice cut, Ah-soka watched a man lose the tip of his finger to a durasteel railing. He didn't scream. He looked at the railing, where a small grey crescent of skin stayed stuck to the frost, and then he looked at the guard on the gantry above, and then he pulled his glove back on and went on working. Nobody stopped. Nobody said anything at all. By the end of the shift the crescent had frozen white and she could no longer tell which part of the railing it had been.

"Rule one," Tess Varro said that night, through the wall between their cells. "Bare skin never touches bare metal. Not once. Not for a heartbeat. The Deep doesn't give you a second try."

"I noticed," Ah-soka said.

"You noticed. Good. Half of them don't notice until they're counting on nine."

Ah-soka lay on her bunk in 4-17 with her coat pulled over the grey thermals and her hands tucked into her armpits. The heat strip in the ceiling buzzed and gave off about as much warmth as a memory. Through the narrow slot in the cell door the corridor light was the same blue it always was. There was no night on Narkis. There was only the shift, and the time between shifts, and the dark the ice made when you shut your eyes.

"Rule two," Tess said. "You don't run. Not in the Yard, not in the tunnel, not in the cut. A guard sees somebody running, he doesn't ask why."

"And rule three?"

A pause, and then the creak of Tess turning over on her bunk.

"Rule three is you don't make me teach you all of them in one night. I'm sixty-one. I need my sleep."

In the morning, Ah-soka saw the cut for herself in proper daylight, or what passed for it.

The ice cut was a long blue wound in the glacier, a hundred meters deep and terraced like a stairway for something much larger than people. Every shift, the prisoners of Rings Three and Four filed down the Yard tunnel from Ring Two and out onto the terraces, where the cutting rigs waited on their treads under a skin of frost. The rigs cut blocks. The blocks went up on cables to the refinery, where something in the old ice, gas trapped ages ago, Tess said, before anyone had a name for this moon, got cooked out of it and sold to people who would never know that Narkis existed.

The work was simple. That was the worst thing about it. Ah-soka had expected to hate the cold and the weight, and she did, but what she hadn't expected was how much room the simple work left in her head.

You guided the plasma saw down the scored line. You kept your weight back so the kickback didn't take your arm. You set the hooks. You signaled the cable crew. You stood clear while the block went up, swinging gently, shedding a glitter of frost on everyone below. You moved to the next line. And all the while, in the space behind your eyes, there was a silence where the Force used to be.

She'd thought of it, on the transport, as going deaf. It wasn't quite that. Deafness was a lack. This was more like being shut in a room with no windows, knowing there was weather outside, a whole sky of it, and being able to feel nothing through the walls. Twice a shift she would reach for it without meaning to, when a block shifted on its hooks, when someone moved fast at the edge of her vision, and there would be nothing there, and her stomach would drop like a missed stair.

The collar was always cold. That was the other thing nobody had told her. It drank heat from her neck all day, and the little blue light on its side burned steady as a pilot lamp.

Tess worked the line beside her. She moved without wasted motion, short and wiry in her grey coat, white hair cropped close to her skull, two fingers on her left glove stuffed with rag where the fingertips should have been.

"Quota's forty blocks a crew," Tess said on the fourth day, not looking up. "You make forty, you eat. You make forty-five, Holt marks it, and next week quota's forty-five. So you make forty."

"Even if we could do more?"

"Especially." Tess tapped the saw's guard with her rag-stuffed finger. "Rule five. Never show them what you can do. They'll just take it."

Ah-soka almost laughed. It came out as a puff of steam.

"Something funny, Jedi?"

"Not a Jedi." It came out easier than it used to. That frightened her a little. "Someone told me almost the same thing once. About droids, actually. He said if you let them see your best, they'll plan for it."

"Smart someone."

"He was showing off."

Tess grunted, and for a while they cut without talking. Down the terrace, past two rigs and a stacked pallet of hooks, Krasso worked with the Ring Three crew. He was easy to find. Even among Trandoshans he was big, with dun-green scales and a notch in his left brow ridge like someone had once tried to split his skull with an axe and been disappointed. He never looked at her when she looked at him. But every time she turned her back, she felt the place between her shoulder blades go tight, the way it used to before blaster fire, and she knew, without the Force, with only the old, stupid animal part of her, that he was watching.

"He's patient," Tess said, following her glance. "Trandoshans think hunting is prayer. He's not going to do it in the mess, where it's quick. He's going to do it where it means something."

"Where's that?"

Tess put the saw down and looked at her, really looked, for the first time all shift. Her eyes were pale and very tired.

"Rule six," she said. "Cut Three, terrace eleven, at shift change. The gantry cam on that side's been frozen for two years. Guards walk the upper rail at change, and from the upper rail there's a ledge that hides the whole bottom of the terrace. Everybody knows. Guards know. They like that it's there. Saves them paperwork."

"Why are you telling me?"

"Because you're getting moved to Cut Three tomorrow," Tess said. "I saw the roster. Somebody traded somebody something to put you there."

Scene break.

She could have asked to stay in Cut Two. She thought about it most of the night, lying with her hands under her arms, listening to the heat strip and to Tess's breathing through the wall. She could have gone to Holt. She could have made a complaint. She could have pretended to be sick.

And then what? Krasso would wait. He had told her so, the first night, with his claws locked in her collar: the cold is long. Tomorrow, or in a week, or in a year, somewhere with a frozen cam and a ledge in the way, he would come. The only choice she had was whether she walked into it with her eyes open.

So she went to Cut Three.

The shift was long and quiet. Krasso's crew worked the terrace above hers, and she did not look up at them, and she counted her blocks with the rest of the crew, and she kept her weight back on the saw. Around her the other prisoners were a little too careful not to stand near her. A Zabrak woman she'd never spoken to brushed past her at the hook pallet and said, very low, "Don't be near the edge," and was gone before Ah-soka could turn.

The horn for shift change was a long, flat bray that echoed off the ice for a full ten seconds. The crews began to file toward the ramps. The guards on the upper rail turned and walked away along it, unhurried, the way people walk when they've decided not to see something.

Ah-soka stayed by the rig.

He came down the terrace wall instead of the ramp. She heard his claws bite the ice before she saw him, and she was already moving when he landed, which was the only reason the ice pick, a cutter's pick, half a meter of hooked steel with a cable-grip handle, took a chunk out of the rig's housing and not out of her skull.

The sound rang across the cut.

"Garnac," Krasso said. His voice was a rasp in the cold. "Say his name."

She backed along the rig. Her heart was going hard. Without the Force, the world was so slow to tell her things. She couldn't feel where he'd go next. She could only see him, his weight, his shoulders, the angle of the pick, and guess.

"I didn't kill your uncle for sport," she said. "He was hunting children."

"He was hunting prey." The pick came round in a flat arc. She dropped under it. It whistled over her mon-trahls close enough that she felt the air move. "You were prey. You were supposed to be prey. And you put him in the dirt."

He was strong. Much stronger than she was, now or ever, and he wasn't stupid. He kept the pick high, kept his feet wide, and herded her, step by step, toward the open lip of the terrace, where there was nothing beyond the edge but blue air and a ten-meter drop to the next shelf of ice.

Don't be near the edge.

Her back foot found it. Frost crumbled under her heel and fell away.

On Wasskah she had been hunted without her sabers. She'd been hungry, and frightened, and so angry she shook. But she'd had the Force then, faint and stubborn beneath everything, telling her when to jump. Now there was no one telling her anything. There was only what her body remembered: years of drills in the Temple's training rooms, Master Plo's patience, Anakin's endless, maddening, again, Snips, a thousand falls onto the practice mats until falling stopped being a thing that happened to her and became a thing she did.

Krasso lunged.

She went down instead of back. Flat on the frost, sliding, under the pick and between his planted feet, the way she'd once gone under a battle droid's legs on Christophsis. Her shoulder slammed the ice. She hooked his ankle with her boot as she passed and pulled.

It wasn't enough to drop a Trandoshan. It was enough to make him stagger. And on terrace ice, with his weight thrown forward into a swing that met nothing, stagger was enough. His claws scrabbled. The pick flew out of his hand and spun away into the blue. He went down on one knee at the very edge, and the frost gave under him, and for a long, terrible moment he hung there half over the drop, scrabbling at a lip of ice that was crumbling as fast as he could grip it.

Ah-soka was on her feet. She didn't remember getting there.

The second cutter's pick was lying on the hook pallet. She took it. The grip was so cold it bit through her glove.

She put the hooked point under his jaw, just where the scales went soft.

Krasso went very still. He was holding himself up on his elbows and one foot. Below him the ice fell away in a sheer blue face. His eyes, yellow with black slits, fixed on hers.

"Do it," he said.

She could. That was the thing that would stay with her afterward, through everything. Not that she wanted to, though some part of her, the part that had stood in a tribunal while Tarkin said murderer in that clean, considerate voice, wanted to very much. Only that she could. A push. Not even that. She could take the pick away and step back and let the ice do it, and on the upper rail no one would see, and in the report it would say accident at shift change and Commandant Vorne would initial it.

She thought of three troopers she had never met, whose numbers she'd had to hear read aloud in a courtroom. She thought of a Gran child on a falling platform, alive because someone had chosen to hold up the world instead of chasing what he wanted.

She took the pick away from his throat.

Then she reversed it, set the hook into the ice beside his hand, and leaned back on the handle with all her weight.

"Grab it."

Krasso stared at her.

"Grab it, or don't," she said through her teeth. "My arms are cold."

He grabbed it. For a moment his weight nearly pulled her over the lip with him, and she braced both boots against the rig's tread and hauled, and he got a knee onto the terrace, and then the other, and then he was lying on the ice on his belly with his chest heaving and his claws dug in to the root.

She let go of the pick and stepped back out of his reach, breathing hard. Her shoulder was screaming.

"Your uncle hunted people because it made him feel big," she said. "You can be better than him. Or you can come at me again, and I'll put you on the ice again. Your choice. But I'm not going to be your prayer."

Krasso lifted his head. There was something in his face she couldn't read.

Then the gantry lights came on above them, white and blinding, and a voice on the loudhailer shouted for every prisoner on terrace eleven to get on their knees with their hands on their heads, and she understood that the guards on the upper rail had not walked as far away as all that.

An hour later, still in binders, she stood before the person who decided what that meant.

"Fighting," Commandant Ilsa Vorne said. "Possession of a tool outside a work line. Endangering facility equipment."

Her office on Ring One was warm. That was the first thing Ah-soka noticed, and she hated herself a little for how much she noticed it. Warm, and quiet, and spare: a desk with nothing on it but a datapad and a stylus laid parallel to its edge, a viewport onto the eternal twilight of the ice, a single holo of a Republic cruiser on the wall. Vorne sat very straight behind the desk, iron-grey hair pinned without a strand out of place, collar control cylinder at her belt.

Sergeant Holt stood behind Ah-soka's chair. Her hands were bound in front of her. Her shoulder had swollen so that she could not lift her right arm above her chest.

"He attacked me," Ah-soka said.

"I'm aware." Vorne didn't look at the datapad. "Prisoner Krasso has already given his statement. He states that he attacked you, that you disarmed him, and that you pulled him back from the edge of terrace eleven. He was quite insistent on that last point. I don't believe I've ever heard a Trandoshan insist on a debt before. It was almost touching."

Ah-soka said nothing.

"Do you know why I am punishing you anyway, Twenty-two fourteen?"

"Because you can."

"Because the rule is the rule." Vorne folded her hands. "A prisoner who fights goes to the Well. Not a prisoner who starts the fight. Not a prisoner who loses. A prisoner who fights. If I begin making exceptions for prisoners with good reasons, I'll find that every prisoner in this facility has an excellent reason, and then I will have no rules at all, only reasons. And this is a very cold place, and very far away, and it does not survive on reasons." She paused. "Prisoner Krasso will go to the Well too, when medical is done with his hands. Fourteen days for him. Ten for you."

"That's generous."

"It's accurate. You also showed a degree of judgment. I note judgment." Vorne picked up the stylus, made a short mark, set it down parallel to the edge again. "I'll note something else, since we are here. The cam on that terrace is going to be repaired. Sergeant Holt will see to it."

Behind her, Holt shifted his weight. He didn't say anything.

"Take her down," Vorne said.

Scene break.

The Well was not dark. That was its cruelty.

Ah-soka had imagined black, a hole, a nothing, somewhere she could at least shut her eyes and pretend. Instead the cell was a cube of ice faced in durasteel, three paces by three, with a light panel set flush in the ceiling behind a grille, and the light never went off. It was the same cold blue as everywhere on Narkis, but brighter, so bright that the ice in the walls glowed with it and she could see the faint pale ghosts of bubbles frozen a hundred thousand years deep. There was a bench, a drain, a thin thermal blanket, a slot for meal trays. There was no window. High in one corner of the wall was a vent grille, the size of two hands, through which came a breath of slightly less frozen air.

The first day, she slept, because her shoulder hurt and the medic had given her something for it. The second day she paced. Three steps, turn. Three steps, turn. She counted the bubbles in one block of the wall and lost count and started again. She tried closing her eyes and found the light came through her eyelids pink and red and would not leave her alone.

On the third day, someone spoke through the vent.

"You're the one who saluted."

She was lying on the bench with her arm across her eyes. She took it away. The voice had been a man's, dry, thin from disuse, with an accent she couldn't place at first. Precise consonants. Every word set down carefully, as if they cost something.

"Who's there?"

"Your neighbor. I'm afraid we haven't been introduced." A pause, and a sound that might have been a man settling himself against a wall. "Merrick Tal. Formerly Commander Tal of the Confederacy of Independent Systems, Third Raxus Picket. Currently nothing very much. And you are Ah-soka Tano, formerly of the Jedi Order, convicted of murder and treason by the Galactic Republic. They talk about you on the guard channel. I hear it through the floor."

She sat up. Her shoulder twinged.

"You saluted me first," she said.

"I did. The night they brought you in. I was looking up the shaft, I can see a slice of it, through my door slot, if I stand on the bench, and I saw a Toe-groota girl on Ring Five with a collar on, looking down at me." He let a breath out. "You looked like someone who'd just been told the rules of a game she didn't know she was playing. I remembered that look. I wore it for a while."

"How long have you been down here?"

"Eleven months and twenty-two days. The light doesn't change, so I count the meal trays. I'm told I'm being kept for exchange." Something dry in his voice. "I'm told that every quarter. I've begun to think the Republic has forgotten what I'm being exchanged for."

Ah-soka pulled her knees up and wrapped the blanket around her shoulders. Up close to the vent the air was a fraction less cold, and she could hear him more clearly. He breathed slowly, like someone who'd learned to make his breath last.

"I've fought your people," she said. "A lot of them."

"I'd imagine. Droids, mostly. We don't put many living soldiers in front of Jedi. It seems a poor use of them." A pause. "Where?"

"Christophsis. Ryloth. Geonosis. Other places."

"Ryloth." He was quiet a moment. "That was a bad business. I won't defend it. Wat Tambor was a disgrace to the uniform he didn't wear. There are disgraces on every side of a war, Miss Tano. I've only noticed lately that your side gets to put its disgraces in charge of prisons."

"You don't get to call it my side."

"No?" He sounded genuinely interested. "What side are you on, then?"

She opened her mouth and found she didn't have an answer.

They talked through the vent over the following days. There was nothing else to do. He told her about Raxus, wide grey seas, a capital built of white stone, a Senate where, he said, the delegates still read their own speeches. He had been a schoolteacher's son. He had joined the Confederacy's defense forces at twenty because the Republic Senate had voted, three years running, to tax his world's shipping to pay for a war that wasn't his world's war, and his world had decided to leave, and the Republic had decided they were not allowed to.

"We asked," he said. "That's what nobody on Core-uh-sant will tell you. We asked to go. We filed the petitions, we sent the delegations. And your Senate laughed at us and kept the taxes, and when we left anyway, they called it treason and sent your army. Your clones. A million men grown in jars to kill people who wanted to be left alone."

"Your side built droid armies before we had clones," Ah-soka said. "You bombed cities. I've seen what your Separatist leaders do to people who get in their way. Count Dooku tried to have Senator Amidala killed. More than once. Don't tell me you only wanted to be left alone."

"I didn't say we were good. I said we asked." His voice didn't rise. It never did. "There's a difference between a cause and the people who run it. I'm sure you understand that better than most. You were loyal to an Order. How did your Order repay you?"

She didn't answer. On the other side of the ice she heard him shift, and then sigh.

"I'm sorry. That was cheap."

"It was," she said.

"But it was also true. I've read the transcripts, they let me have a datapad once a week, old Holo-net bulletins, heavily trimmed, but they don't trim everything. Your tribunal was a Republic military court, presided over by the Chancellor, prosecuted by an admiral, judged by senators who had already decided. The footage they convicted you on had no sound. The weapon they say you used was taken from you at the door. And when your teacher couldn't produce a witness, they decided you'd directed an accomplice, which is the sort of thing you say when the facts won't fit the verdict and the verdict won't move." He paused. "And then the Chancellor commuted your sentence, and the Holo-net called it mercy. Mercy. To lock a seventeen-year-old in a glacier forever for something she didn't do."

"You don't know I didn't do it."

"I know you saluted a stranger down a shaft." His voice was quiet. "I know you pulled a Trandoshan off the edge of a cliff who'd come to kill you. I heard about that on the guard channel too. And I know that the Republic that put you here isn't interested in whether you did it. It needed someone. You were convenient." A long pause. "That's what the Republic does, Miss Tano. It needs, and someone pays. Worlds. Clones. Children. You were raised inside the machine, so you couldn't see it. Now you're outside. Look at it."

She lay awake for a long time that night under the light that didn't go off.

He was wrong. She knew he was wrong. She'd stood on worlds where Separatist droids had burned the fields and herded the villagers into camps. She had seen Grievous's work up close. Whatever the Senate had done to Raxus, it did not make what she'd seen right.

But the words stuck. It needs, and someone pays. She thought about Tarkin's clean voice. She thought about the Council, and Master Windu's hand closing on her beads. She thought about the way Vorne had said the rule is the rule, and how nobody in that whole chain of people had ever needed to be cruel, only correct.

Scene break.

On the sixth day, she stopped fighting the light.

It happened almost by accident. She had been sitting on the bench, cross-legged, from long habit, the way she had sat for meditation every morning of her life since she was three years old, and she had closed her eyes and, as she always did, reached.

Nothing. The windowless room. The collar's cold kiss under her jaw.

The old panic came up, the missed stair. And she was so tired of it, so tired of falling every time, that instead of reaching again she simply stopped. She let the reaching go. She sat in the nothing and did not ask it for anything.

And she found she could still hear.

Not the Force. Just the world, the plain one. The tick of the ice settling, somewhere deep in the walls. The hum of the light panel, which she'd thought was steady and was not, it rose and fell, faintly, with some pulse in the power grid far above. The drip in the drain, every eleven or twelve heartbeats. Merrick Tal's slow breathing through the vent. Her own heart. Her own breath, going in cold and coming out warm, the way it had on that first night on Ring Four when she'd been about to claw the collar off her throat and had found her breathing instead.

When she was six or seven, a crèche master had made the younglings sit by the Temple's fountains for an entire afternoon and listen, and Ah-soka had spent the whole time fidgeting and reaching outward, sure the lesson was somewhere out there in the Force if she could only grab it. She had thought, then, that without the Force there would be nothing to listen to at all.

But here was the ice, and the water, and the hum, and here was she. Still.

It wasn't peace. She didn't want to call it that, didn't trust it enough. It was quieter than peace, and heavier, and it asked nothing of her. It was the kind of stillness the ice had, the kind that came from being exactly what it was and not reaching for anything else.

She sat in it until the meal tray came, and when she opened her eyes the light was the same and the walls were the same, and her hands, she noticed, were not shaking.

"You've gone quiet," Merrick said through the vent, later. "For a while I thought you'd died. People do, down here."

"I was meditating."

"With that collar on? I thought the whole point, "

"So did I," Ah-soka said.

He seemed to consider that. "And?"

"And it turns out I'm still here without it." She leaned her head back against the ice. "I'm not sure what that means yet."

"It means they can't take everything," Merrick said. "It's the first thing you learn in a place like this, and the last thing they want you to know."

On the tenth day, the door hissed open, and Sergeant Holt stood in it with his breath steaming.

"Up," he said. "Time's done."

She stood. Her legs were stiff and her shoulder had settled to a deep, grudging ache. She had expected to feel relief, and there was some. There was also, to her surprise, a small reluctance, as if she were leaving a conversation halfway through.

"Miss Tano," Merrick said through the vent. "One more thing, before they take you up."

Holt made an impatient noise. Ah-soka held up her bound hands, one moment, and to her astonishment, he let her have it.

"I've been thinking about your case," Merrick said. "I don't have a great deal else to think about. You say you didn't kill those three troopers. I believe you. So someone else did. Someone who could walk into a Republic military prison, take a Jedi's lightsabers out of an evidence locker, kill three armed men, open your cell door, and walk out again without being seen. Someone who knew exactly where you'd go and when." His voice was very quiet now, and very clear. "That person is still free, Miss Tano. They watched you be convicted. They know you're alive. And they know exactly where you are."

The cold seemed to come through her coat all at once.

"Up," Holt said again, and this time he took her elbow.

She went. Up the shaft, past Ring Five, toward the dim blue glow of her cell and Tess's dry voice and the shift horn and forty blocks a day for the rest of her life. And all the way up, the stillness she'd found held, she was surprised how well it held, but in the middle of it, like a stone dropped into ice water, sat a question she had never once let herself ask.

Who knew where I'd go?

End of chapter.

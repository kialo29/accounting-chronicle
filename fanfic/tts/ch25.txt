Chapter 25. Where It Began.

The comlink on Ah-soka's wrist clicked three times, and somewhere above her in the dark a Senator of the Republic and a man the Republic had declared dead settled in to watch her best friend confess to murder.

If her friend came.

Ah-soka let her hand fall back to her knee. The old munitions warehouse had not improved in five months. Rain still came through the roof in long silver threads, through holes punched by something heavy a long time ago, and it still pooled on the floor in the same low places, and the same smell still hung over everything, wet rust, dead machine oil, the faint chemical sourness that never quite leaves a building where explosives were once stored. The crates were gone. The Guard had taken every one of them as evidence, and where they had stood the grime of the floor gave way to pale, clean rectangles, a row of them, like the outlines of graves. A strip of evidence tape had snapped and lay soaking in a puddle near her knee. On the support pillar to her left, at about the height of her throat, there was a long black scorch where a red blade had missed her the first time.

She had been found right about here. On her side, between two of those crates, with Fox's lamp in her eyes and the rain on her face and no idea yet how completely she had been finished.

She breathed, and let the memory go by.

High in the north end of the building a crane gantry hung from its rusted rail, a cab of grey durasteel and broken windows forty meters above the floor. She did not look at it. She did not need to. Fives was up there with his left arm strapped to his chest and his helmet propped on the rail in front of him, the dent over its brow pointed down at her like an eyebrow raised in doubt. They had put a scrap of black tape over the little recording light so it wouldn't show. Padmay was beside him. Ah-soka could feel them both in the Force as two small warm points, one stubborn and feverish, one steady and very frightened and refusing to be. She drew a thin veil over them, the way she'd once learned to hide younglings in a cave, and left it there.

Herself she did not hide. Barris would need to feel her here, alone and waiting, before she came through any door. Whoever else in the city might reach down tonight and feel it too, Ah-soka had decided, would have to be a problem for afterward.

By the south loading doors, Ventress was a cold knot of impatience in the shadows.

"Your friend," Ventress had said, an hour ago, "is late."

"She'll come."

"You keep saying that as if it were a kindness."

Ah-soka had not answered, because it wasn't.

She closed her eyes now and went down into the stillness she'd found in the Well, the one that hadn't needed the Force at all, just the breath going in and the breath going out and the knowledge that the next one would come. The burn under her left jaw itched. The raw ring around her neck where the collar had sat for four months ached in the wet. Hondo's saber lay across her thighs, heavy and plain, the worn black ridges of its grip cool under her fingers.

She waited.

Scene break.

She felt Barris before she heard her.

It came the way Ventress had described it on Florrum, in a storeroom that smelled of ore dust: calm water, very clear, very deep, and at the bottom something that had stopped moving. Ah-soka had wanted to argue with that description when she heard it. She didn't now. It came toward her through the lower levels like a cold current through warm sea, unhurried, and stopped outside the narrow personnel door in the west wall, the same door, Ah-soka realized, that she had come through herself five months ago, with Ventress at her back and a friend's directions in her head.

The door scraped open. Rain blew in.

Barris stepped through with her hood up and her healer's satchel over one shoulder. Her own lightsaber hung at her belt where it always had. She shut the door behind her with care, the way she did everything, and stood with her back to it, dripping.

Neither of them spoke.

Then Barris opened the satchel. Ah-soka watched her lift away a fold of pale cloth, a surgical drape, Ah-soka saw, still creased from long storage, and take out something white. She turned it once in her hands. Then she raised it and fitted it over her face.

The bone-white mask. The slanted eye-slits. The hood falling forward around it.

And there it was again, the figure from among the crates, the shape that had come out of the dark with a red blade in each hand. Ah-soka's body knew it before her mind did. Her shoulders rose; her grip shifted on the saber hilt; her heart kicked hard against her ribs, the way it had in this room when she had still believed she was fighting a stranger.

"Is that who I'm talking to?" Ah-soka said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, distantly.

The mask regarded her.

"I thought it would be easier," Barris said. The voice behind it was flattened and strange, but it was hers. "If you were speaking to this. I thought it might be easier for both of us."

"It isn't."

"No." A long breath, audible even through the mask. "No, I can see that."

She reached up and took it off.

Her face was thinner than it had been on Narkis. The diamond markings across her cheeks stood out darker than Ah-soka remembered, the way paint stands out on a wall that has lost its color. There were shadows under her eyes, but her eyes were very steady. Barris set the mask down on the corner of a fallen beam beside the door, face up, so that it stared at the roof with its empty slits and filled slowly with rain.

"Ventress is at the south doors," Barris said. "I can feel her from here."

"She wanted to come."

"Of course she did." Something almost like humor touched her mouth and died there. "She'll want them back."

"She'll want a lot of things." Ah-soka stood up. Her knees were stiff from kneeling. She did not ignite the saber; she held it loosely, emitter down, along her forearm. "Tell me."

"You already know. Your message said so."

"I want to hear you say it."

Barris studied her for a while. Rain fell between them in a curtain from one of the roof holes, catching what little light there was, and Ah-soka thought: She doesn't know about the gantry. She thinks it's just us, and Ventress, and nobody would ever believe Ventress. She kept her eyes on Barris and did not let her thoughts rise.

"Where would you like me to begin?" Barris said.

"The hangar."

"The hangar." Barris nodded slowly, as if accepting a diagnosis. "I bought the nano-droids from a Rodian in the Kesh Arcade. He didn't ask what I wanted them for. People down here don't. I gave them to Letta Turmond, and Letta gave them to her husband in his lunch tin, and Jackar Bowmani carried them into the Temple hangar the way he carried in everything else, every morning, for eleven years." She paused. "He never knew. You should know that. He thought she'd packed him a meal."

Ah-soka's hand tightened on the hilt.

"Six Jedi," she said. "Tutso Mara. Clones. Workers. People who fixed lifts."

"I know the number."

"Why?"

And Barris, who had always been the quiet one, who had sat at the back of every class and asked the question nobody else had thought of, lifted her chin.

"Because nobody was looking," she said. "Ah-soka, when did you last see a Jedi heal someone who wasn't a soldier? When did you last see one of us sit with a farmer, or a child, or a dying man who wasn't wearing armor we'd given him? People used to send for us when they wanted a war stopped. Now we have ranks. We have fleets. We read casualty reports over breakfast and call the numbers acceptable. We take boys who were grown in jars ten years ago and we walk them into droid fire and we say the Force is with them." Her voice had not risen. It had not needed to. "I have stood in the clinic and put the same troopers back together three times so they could be sent out to be broken a fourth. I have watched Masters plan campaigns the way the archivists catalog star charts. The Order has become a weapon that the Senate keeps in a drawer, and I thought, I truly thought, that if the war came home, into our own hangar, into the one place we believed was ours, then perhaps the Jedi would look up and see what they had become."

"And did they?"

"They mourned. They lit candles. Then they went back to the war." Barris's mouth twisted. "And they expelled you."

The rain hissed in the puddles.

"Letta," Ah-soka said.

Barris closed her eyes for a moment. When she opened them, some of that terrible steadiness had gone.

"She was going to tell you it was a Jedi. She was going to say my name, she'd have had to, sooner or later, and she'd asked for you because she thought you might believe her. I was in the upper service passage over the cells. I hadn't planned it. I want you to understand that I hadn't planned it." She raised her right hand a little, looked at it, let it fall. "It was very easy. That was what I couldn't forgive afterward. Not that I did it. That it was easy."

"And then you watched them arrest me for it."

"Yes."

"And the three troopers." Ah-soka heard her own voice go thin and careful, like ice in the yard on Narkis that would hold you right up until it didn't. "CT-2261. CT-4408. CT-9170."

Barris flinched at the numbers. Not much. Enough.

"I took your sabers out of the evidence locker," she said. "I used them because they had to be yours. I left the keycard in your cell because I knew you would run. I knew that if you ran, you would call the only friend you thought you had left in the Temple, and I would tell you where to go." She glanced around the warehouse, the pale squares on the floor, the scorched pillar. "Here. I sent you here. I had the crates moved in the night before. I wore that." A small motion of her head toward the mask in the rain. "I took Ventress's sabers from her while she was on the floor, and I fought you with them, and I left you lying among the crates for the Guard to find."

Ah-soka found that she was shaking. Not visibly, or she hoped not visibly, but deep inside, a fine continuous tremor like a ship's hull in the last second before a jump. The Force pressed close around her, hot and dark at the edges, offering itself. She could feel exactly how easy it would be. She could feel the shape of Barris's throat in it.

She breathed in. She breathed out. The next breath came.

"There's more," Barris said quietly. "You'll want all of it. The night before the vote, Anakin found Ventress in the old district. He was close, very close. She'd begun to tell him things. I came down on them with the mask on and threw her off a ledge, and I cut a pylon with the droids I had left, and a whole hab stack began to fall." She swallowed. "He could have caught me. I was on a catwalk across the platform. He looked right at me. And then he turned around and held up a building with his hands so that a mother and her child could get out from under it. I stood there and watched him choose."

Ah-soka had known it. Anakin had told her himself, in the canyon on Florrum, with the stones lifting around his boots. But she had only ever heard it from his side. She had never pictured the mask up there in the dust, watching him choose, and counting on it. She thought of Anakin in the visiting room on Narkis, thinner, angrier, a steel cup folding in on itself in front of him. Of how he'd never been able to look at her for long.

"Don't," she said.

"You asked for all of it."

"I know what I asked for." She made herself lift her eyes. "Then you came to see me. Before the trial. You healed my wrists."

"Yes."

"I thanked you."

"Yes." Barris's voice cracked, finally, just a little, at the bottom. "You promised you would never name the friend who sent you here. You asked me to light candles for the troopers. You said you couldn't, so would I."

"And on Narkis." Ah-soka could feel the place on her shin where the gash had closed under those green, patterned, steady hands. "You came all that way. You fixed my shoulder. You put your hands on my collar and made the pain stop, and you wrote that note, and they turned it down so I wouldn't seize." She heard herself laugh, a short ugly sound she didn't recognize. "I told you to be careful. I told you there was someone cleaning up, and I was afraid for you."

"I know."

"Was that guilt? All of it? Every time you touched me?"

Barris was quiet a long moment.

"The note was the only honest thing I did all year," she said. "The rest, I don't know anymore, Ah-soka. I don't know which parts of me were lying. I'm not sure there's anyone left in here who could tell you."

Ah-soka turned away from her. She did it before she could stop herself, and then she stood with her back half-turned and stared at the black scorch on the pillar until the edges of it blurred.

"The fourth candle," she said to the pillar.

She heard Barris breathe in.

"I asked you to light three. For the troopers. You lit four. I lay in my cell on Narkis and thought about that fourth candle for days, I thought you'd lit it for Letta. Because you were kind. Because you felt sorry for a woman everyone hated." She turned back. "Who was it for?"

"Jackar Bowmani," Barris said.

The rain filled the silence.

"He fixed lift motors. He had a little scar on his thumb from a coupling that slipped. He used to hum while he worked; I used to hear him from the clinic walkway." She looked down at her hands. "I've tried to light one for Letta. I can't. My hands won't do it. She knew, you see. She chose. Jackar didn't choose anything. I chose for him."

Ah-soka's eyes stung. She hated that they stung. She had been certain, on Florrum, sitting in that dead outpost with the bacta smell in her nose and the pieces falling together one after another, that once she knew everything she would feel something clean, fury or grief, one or the other, something she could hold by the hilt. Instead she felt everything at once, and none of it had a handle.

"Come back with me," she said.

Barris stared at her.

"I mean it. Come in. Tell them what you told me. Tell the Council, tell Master Luminara, tell the tribunal. You were brave enough to do all this; be brave enough to say it." Her voice was rising now; she let it. "I lost everything, Barris. I stood in that chamber and listened to the Chancellor say death, and I believed him, and for one breath I was dead, do you understand that? I was dead, standing up, and then he said life and I didn't even know if that was better. They put a collar on me so I couldn't feel the Force. I broke ice for four months. There are people in that prison who still think I blew up the Temple. My own Master can't look at me. And all you have to do is say it."

"Who would hear it?"

"Everyone."

"No." Barris shook her head slowly. "Ventress? A Separatist assassin with a price on her head, swearing a Jedi stole her sabers? And you, a convicted bomber, escaped in a Separatist raid, believed to have joined Count Dooku? They wouldn't even let you finish the sentence." She drew a breath. "And it doesn't matter, because I wasn't sent here to talk."

The word sent went into Ah-soka like a needle.

"Sent," she said.

"Someone knows." Barris's voice dropped. "Someone with a great deal of power has a recording of my hand in that passage over the cells, at the very second Letta died. Clearer than the one they showed at your trial. He's had it for months." She stopped, and Ah-soka saw her catch the word he and hold it, too late, and decide to let it stand. "They have been very kind to me. That's how it's done, did you know? You'd be amazed how far kindness reaches. Further than threats. A threat you can refuse."

"Who?"

"If I told you, you'd do something brave and stupid about it, and they'd kill you, and it would all have been for nothing. Again." Barris lifted her chin. "If you are cleared, there will be a second trial. A second trial is the one thing that recording cannot survive, and neither can I, and neither can the person who has been keeping it. So I was told, very gently, in a medbay, by someone who never once used the word, that it would be best for everyone if you never testified."

Ah-soka looked at her. At the hood, the rain on its shoulders. At the satchel.

"And you came."

"I came."

"To kill me."

"I came," Barris said, "not knowing which blade I'd light. I walked all the way down here in the rain, past Master Luminara's door, her light was on, she was waiting up for me, and I still didn't know. I thought perhaps when I saw you I would know."

"Do you?"

Barris reached into the satchel. The surgical drape came away and fell to the wet floor, and in her hands were two curved hilts, dark and elegant and wrong. She held them a moment without igniting them, as if weighing them. Her own saber stayed at her belt, untouched.

"These seem more honest," she said. "Don't you think?"

From the south doors, Ventress's presence in the Force flared like a struck match. Ah-soka lifted her free hand, palm out, without looking, stay there, and felt Ventress's fury hang a moment, coiled and furious, and then hold.

"You don't have to do this," Ah-soka said.

"That," Barris said, "is what everyone kept telling me about the war."

The red blades snapped alive.

Scene break.

Ah-soka thumbed the blue blade on as Barris came at her, and the warehouse filled with color.

Barris had always been the better student of forms. Ah-soka had known that since the first time they sparred, the year after Geonosis: Barris in the training salle with her blade drawn in close and level, every parry the minimum, giving nothing away, waiting for you to tire yourself out against her. But Ventress's sabers weren't made for that. They were curved at the hilt, built to swing out wide and come back hooking, and Barris fought with them the way someone speaks a language learned from books, correct, precise, and with every word a half-beat late.

Ah-soka took the first strike high on the blue blade, turned the second low, and felt the jolt of it all the way up her arm into the sore place in her shoulder. Hondo's saber was heavier than hers. Five months heavier. There was no shoto in her other hand, nothing to catch the second red blade but air and the Force, and she spun out of its path and felt it pass so close the heat dried the rain on her cheek.

They broke apart. Circled.

Rain came down through the roof holes in columns, and every time a red blade swept through one it went up in a hiss of steam. The pale crate-squares on the floor slid past under Ah-soka's boots. She kept her saber in reverse grip, tight along her forearm, and watched Barris's feet, because Barris's face was giving her nothing.

Barris came again, a feint high, a real cut low, the second blade looping in behind it. Ah-soka dropped under the first, jumped the second, and slashed backhand as she landed. Barris caught it on both red blades crossed. For a moment they were close enough to see each other's breath. The blades ground together, blue between the red, throwing sparks that died in the puddles.

"You're slower," Barris said, almost gently.

"I've been busy," said Ah-soka, and kicked her in the knee.

Barris staggered back, recovered, and reached out with her left hand. Above them something groaned. Ah-soka looked up in time to see the old crane hook, a great rusted thing on a slack chain, come swinging down out of the dark toward her head. She threw herself flat. The hook went over her with a sound like a door slamming in the sky and crashed into the scorched pillar, and the pillar rang like a bell, and plaster and rust came down in a gritty rain.

She rolled to her feet in the debris and pushed.

A fallen section of the old mezzanine railing, three meters of bent durasteel, tore itself up off the floor and flew at Barris end over end. Barris cut it in half without looking at it. The two pieces clanged away to either side of her and skidded into the dark.

"You were always good at that," Ah-soka said, breathing hard.

"So were you."

They came together again, and this time neither of them held anything back.

It was fast after that, fast and loud and all light. Ah-soka went up a stack of broken shelving and Barris followed, both red blades scything at her ankles; the shelving gave way under them and they rode it down into the shallows by the old loading ramp, blades still moving, water exploding up white around them. Barris pressed her back across the ramp with a relentless overlapping rhythm, red on the left, red on the right, red on the left, and Ah-soka took every blow on Hondo's blade and felt her wrist begin to burn. A cut came in faster than she could turn; she twisted away and it skated across her left forearm, through the sleeve of her coat, and the pain was a white flash and a smell of scorched cloth. She hissed and kept moving.

She could feel Barris through the Force now, the way you always can in a real fight. Not the calm water. Under it. Something churning and exhausted and desperate, a current going two ways at once.

She doesn't want this either, Ah-soka thought. She just doesn't know how to stop.

Barris feinted left. Ah-soka bit. It was a good feint, the best she'd ever thrown at her, and Ah-soka's blue blade swung wide after it, and Barris's right-hand saber came around and up in one smooth curve toward the open side of Ah-soka's neck, toward the raw ring where the collar had been.

And stopped.

It stopped a hand's width from her skin. Ah-soka felt the heat of it against the burn under her jaw. She saw Barris's face above the red light, her eyes wide, her mouth open, as if she'd heard someone call her name from very far away. For one breath, one only, the blade hung there and did not move.

Ah-soka didn't wait to understand it.

She dropped. The red blade passed over her mon-trahls. She reached out with her left hand and the Force together and pulled, and the curved hilt tore out of Barris's right hand and went spinning away across the warehouse, its blade guttering out as it flew, and clattered somewhere near the foot of the loading ramp. Barris swung the left-hand saber down at her. Ah-soka met it with the blue blade, caught it in a bind, turned her wrist the way Anakin had drilled into her a thousand times on a hundred decks, don't fight the blade, Snips, fight the hand, and wrenched.

The second hilt flew. It struck the fallen crane hook with a crack, went dark, and dropped into the water at its base.

Barris stood empty-handed in the rain.

Ah-soka kicked her feet out from under her.

She went down onto her knees in a puddle, hard. Ah-soka was over her in the same motion, the blue blade turned and lowered so that its humming edge sat just beneath Barris's chin, close enough that the rain falling between them burst into steam. With her left hand, her burned arm protesting, she reached and called Barris's own saber off her belt. It slapped into her palm. She hooked it onto her own belt without taking her eyes off Barris.

For a long moment the only sounds were the rain, and the blade, and the two of them breathing.

Barris's hood had fallen back. Water ran down her face in lines across the diamond markings, and Ah-soka could not tell which of it was rain. She was looking past the blue blade at Ah-soka, and there was nothing on her face now at all, no defense, no argument, nothing calm. Just tiredness, all the way down.

"Do it," Barris said.

Ah-soka didn't move.

"Please." Her voice was very small. "You have every right. You know you do. No one would ever blame you, not Ventress, not Anakin, not the Council. Not anyone." She swallowed, and the movement brought her throat a little closer to the blade, and she didn't pull away. "I can't go back up there. I can't light those candles again tomorrow morning. Please, Ah-soka. Finish it."

The Force was very loud. It was all around Ah-soka, inside her, roaring like the wind across the ice yard on Narkis at shift change, and in the middle of it she could feel exactly how simple it would be. One small turn of the wrist. She had lost everything she was to this woman; the Order, her rank, her Master's trust, her name, four months of the Force. She'd had the word death said over her in a full chamber. Six Jedi and a man who hummed while he fixed lift motors and three troopers who'd never even seen it coming. Letta, alone in a room with no one to help her breathe.

She thought about the silent footage. The tribunal had watched it frame by frame, a woman dying in a cell, and Ah-soka Tano standing in front of her doing nothing to stop it. They had looked at that and seen someone who would kill a helpless person because it was convenient. Tarkin had said it in his clean, dry voice. Nine senators had believed it, and the rest of the Republic after them.

They'd been wrong.

They'd been wrong, and she had spent four months in the ice holding onto that one fact like a heat-pack in her coat. If she turned her wrist now, here, in this room, with a woman on her knees before her who couldn't fight back, then they would have been right after all. Not about the hangar. Not about Letta. About her.

"No," Ah-soka said.

Barris closed her eyes.

"They called me a murderer," Ah-soka said. Her voice was shaking now, and she let it. "They looked at me and decided I was someone who would kill a person who couldn't fight back. You made them think that. You built it piece by piece. And I'm not going to finish it for you." She lifted the blade away from Barris's throat, a handspan, then another. "I won't become what they convicted. Not for you. Not for anyone."

She thumbed the switch. The blue blade folded down into the hilt and was gone, and the dark came back into the warehouse all at once, grey and wet and quiet.

Barris made a sound, not a word, and bent forward over her knees until her forehead almost touched the water. Her shoulders shook. Ah-soka stood over her, the cold hilt in her hand, and found that she was crying too, without noise, and that she couldn't have said for which of them.

Over by the south doors, Ventress had stepped out of the shadows. She stood a few paces into the room, her gaze moving from the dark hilt by the crane hook to the other one lying at the foot of the ramp, and back. She didn't go to them. She looked at Ah-soka instead, a long unreadable look, and stayed where she was.

High overhead, in the cab of the crane gantry, the little taped-over light was still recording.

It was over. Ah-soka let the breath go out of her, the long breath she felt she'd been holding since the night they'd found her lying on this floor. It was over, and it was true, and somebody had heard it.

Then the Force changed temperature.

It came down from somewhere above them like the first breath of air from an opened airlock on Narkis, a cold so sudden and complete that the hair on Ah-soka's arms rose and the burn on her forearm went numb. The rain seemed to slow. Across the floor, Ventress's head snapped toward the west door.

Footsteps rang on the old iron stair outside. Not hurrying. Not careful either.

And Ah-soka knew them. She knew that tread the way she knew her own heartbeat, had followed it down a hundred gangways and across a hundred battlefields, had listened for it every day for four months on a moon past the end of every map. She knew the presence coming down the stair behind it, too, bright and familiar and dear.

Only now it was burning black.

End of chapter.

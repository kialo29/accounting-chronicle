Part Two: The Deep.

Chapter 8. The Weight of Gratitude.

The third gun on the Ordaine cliffs had been firing for nine hours when Anakin Skywalker decided he was done waiting for it to stop.

"Bombers are forty minutes out, sir," the gunship pilot said over his shoulder. Rain sheeted across the canopy in grey ropes. Below them the sea threw itself at the base of the cliffs, and above the spray, cut into the black rock like the eye sockets of a skull, three Separatist artillery batteries took turns pounding the beach where the 212th and the 501st were dug in behind their own dead walkers. "General Kenobi's orders are to hold at altitude until, "

"I heard General Kenobi's orders." Anakin unbuckled his harness and stood. The deck bucked under his boots. "Take us down to the second ledge. Under the gun's depression angle."

"Sir, that's inside the flak envelope."

"Then go fast."

The pilot went fast. The gunship dropped out of the clouds so steeply that the troopers in the bay grabbed the overhead straps, and for three long seconds the whole world was black cliff filling the open hatch, close enough that Anakin could see water streaming out of the cracks. Flak burst above them. Something punched through the rear stabilizer with a sound like a hammer on a pot.

"Sir!" Rex's voice crackled in his ear from the beach. "Your gunship is off its heading."

"No, it isn't."

He jumped.

It was not a long fall, as falls went. He had fallen farther on worse planets. The ledge came up wet and slick, and he landed in a crouch with his saber already lit, the blue blade hissing as the rain hit it. Over his head the third gun fired. The sound was physical, a wall of pressure that went through his chest and rattled his teeth, and a few seconds later he heard it land on the beach, and heard the comm channel fill with voices.

Two B1s in the access tunnel turned around. One of them said, "Uh, " and that was all it got to say.

He went up the tunnel at a run. The droids were slow in the wet, their joints grinding, and he went through them the way rain goes through a gutter. A super battle droid stepped out of an alcove with its arm cannon raised, and Anakin didn't slow down, didn't deflect, just went under the bolt and opened the droid from hip to shoulder as he passed. The bolt hit the wall behind him and splashed molten rock across his back. He felt it through his tunic, a line of heat, and ignored it.

The gun chamber was enormous, a cavern hollowed out of the cliff, with the barrel run out through a slot in the rock toward the sea. The loading crew were droids. The fire-control station was droids. All of it was droids, and all of it turned toward him at once.

He did not count them. Later, Rex would count them from the helmet recordings and say the number in a flat voice, and Anakin would shrug.

What he remembered afterward was not the fighting. It was the elevation gear: a great toothed wheel as tall as two men, turning with a slow grinding patience to lower the barrel for the next shot. He remembered putting his saber through the axle, and then reaching out with the Force and taking hold of the barrel itself, all those tons of durasteel, and pulling it down, not toward the beach, but toward the rock face beneath the slot.

He remembered thinking, quite clearly, She'd tell me this is stupid.

The gun fired into the cliff.

The chamber came apart. He was already running, but not fast enough, and the blast wave caught him in the tunnel and threw him the last ten meters out onto the ledge. Something hot and heavy slid across his ribs on the left side. He rolled. Rock rained past him into the sea. Above him the first and second guns had gone quiet, their crews no doubt wondering what had just happened to the third.

He lay on his back in the rain for a moment with his saber still in his fist. The burn along his side felt very far away.

"General Skywalker." Rex, on the comm. "Was that you?"

"Bombers can take the other two," Anakin said. "Send someone to pick me up."

There was a pause just long enough to be a comment.

"Yes, sir," said Rex.

Scene break.

Kix cut his tunic away on the beach under a field awning that was failing to keep out the rain. The burn ran in a long ragged stripe from his left armpit to the bottom of his ribs, and the medic looked at it for a while without saying anything, which from Kix was a speech.

"It's fine," Anakin said.

"It's a second-degree plasma burn with rock fragments in it, sir." Kix began picking the fragments out with a pair of fine forceps. "But if the General says it's fine."

"The General says it's fine."

"Then I'll just do this for fun." Another fragment. Anakin didn't flinch. He had stopped flinching at most things in the last six weeks. It was surprising how little effort it took, once you had decided that nothing that happened to you counted.

Out on the water the bombers came in low and the remaining two batteries went up in sequence, orange flowers under the cloud, the sound arriving late and soft across the bay. A cheer went down the trench line. It was a tired cheer. The 212th had lost a lot of men on this beach before the 501st arrived, and the 501st had lost some too.

Not as many as they would have, Anakin told himself. Not as many as if he'd waited.

He was still telling himself that when Obi-Wan ducked under the awning, his robes black with rain, his beard dripping. He looked at the burn. He looked at Kix. Kix discovered an urgent need to fetch more bacta from somewhere else.

"I gave you an order," Obi-Wan said.

"You gave the pilot an order."

"Anakin."

"The bombers were forty minutes out. That gun was firing every ninety seconds." Anakin pulled what was left of his tunic around himself. "Do the arithmetic, Master. I did."

"I have done the arithmetic. I do it every time you go out ahead of your men." Obi-Wan didn't sit. He stood with his hands folded into his wet sleeves, the way he had stood in a hundred briefing rooms, and his voice stayed level, and only someone who had known him for thirteen years would have heard what was under it. "That was the fourth time in six weeks. Kelvrin Reach, the convoy at Brenn, the refinery. And now this. Each time you've survived by a margin I would not care to measure."

"And each time it worked."

"Each time it happened to work. That isn't the same thing, and you know it isn't." Obi-Wan's gaze dropped to the burn again, and then came back up. "You aren't sleeping. Your men have noticed. My men have noticed."

"Then your men should mind their own business."

It came out harder than he meant it to. He didn't take it back.

Obi-Wan was quiet for a moment. Rain drummed on the awning. Somewhere down the beach a trooper was laughing in the high, cracked way men laughed after a bad day.

"There's something else," Obi-Wan said at last. "I'd hoped to say it somewhere drier. The Council has asked that you not petition the Judiciary for contact with Ah-soka."

Anakin went still.

"Asked," he said.

"For her sake, as much as yours. Master Windu feels, we feel, that any appearance of the Order intervening in a Republic sentence would only harden the Senate against her. And there are those in the Senate who would be very glad to say the Jedi cannot let go of a convicted murderer. If there is ever to be a review of her case, "

"There won't be. You made sure of that when you handed her over." Anakin stood. The burn pulled, and he let it. "And the Council doesn't get to ask me anything about her. It gave that up. It gave it up in the Council chamber, in front of her, and Master Windu took her beads off her himself so everybody would know it was done properly."

"I argued against it. You know I did."

"I know you lost." Anakin reached for his cloak. "That's all anyone remembers, in the end, Master Kenobi. Who lost."

Obi-Wan flinched. It was very small, a tightening at the eyes, gone at once. Anakin saw it and felt nothing he could name. Or rather, he felt something, and buried it under the burn, where it would keep.

Obi-Wan left without another word.

Rex was waiting outside the awning, helmet on, rain beading on the blue paint. He had obviously heard all of it. He fell into step beside Anakin as they walked down the line toward the landing zone, and for a while neither of them said anything.

Then Rex held out his hand, palm down, as though offering a ration bar. Anakin took what was in it. A datachip, small and warm from Rex's glove.

"Fives went back down to the munitions district," Rex said, eyes front. "Twice. Off duty. The crates in that warehouse had their Republic stencils ground off, but somebody got lazy with one of them. There was a reseller's tag on the underside. Inventory sticker. Fives traced it."

Anakin turned the chip in his fingers. "To who?"

"Rodian salvage broker named Dalo Pesk. Stall on the Kesh Arcade, lower levels, about two hundred floors under where the platform fell. Moves surplus. Doesn't ask questions." Rex paused. "If someone bought nano-droids off the black market, sir, it's a fair bet they bought them from him. Or someone he knows."

Anakin closed his hand on the chip. For the first time in six weeks, something in his chest moved that wasn't anger.

"Rex. I owe you."

"Fives found it, sir." Rex's helmet turned slightly toward him. "And we weren't down there. Officially."

"Officially," Anakin said, "I don't even know what a Rodian is."

Scene break.

The Kesh Arcade was a long gallery of shuttered stalls under a ceiling so low that the advertising holos had to hang sideways. It smelled of fried grubs and coolant and old rain. Anakin came down into it two nights later in a brown civilian cloak with the hood up, his saber clipped out of sight at the small of his back, and his left side still stiff under the bacta patches Kix had made him promise to change.

He had come straight from the hangar. He had told no one but Rex he was coming. He had told no one at all when.

Dalo Pesk's stall was near the far end, between a shop selling counterfeit comlink casings and a stall that seemed to sell nothing at all. Its shutter was rolled up halfway. A light was on inside.

Anakin knew before he ducked under the shutter. He couldn't have said how. The Force didn't shout it. It was more as though the Force had stopped talking about that corner of the arcade altogether, the way a crowd goes quiet around something it doesn't want to look at.

The Rodian was sitting in a chair behind his counter. His hands were folded in his lap. His head was bowed a little, as if he'd nodded off over his accounts, and his antennae drooped. He was dead. He had been dead for some hours. There was not a mark on him that Anakin could see.

Anakin stood in the doorway and did not touch anything.

The stall was small, cluttered in the way of every salvage stall in every lower level of every world: shelves of gutted droid parts, bins of power cells sorted by charge, a rack of secondhand blaster barrels, a workbench under a magnifier lamp. It ought to have been filthy. Pesk's counter was spotless. The workbench was spotless. The floor had been cleaned, and recently. The sharp sweet smell of solvent hung in the air underneath the smell of the arcade.

He moved in, slowly, keeping his hands under his cloak.

On the wall behind the counter was a shelf of datapads, the cheap kind, the kind brokers used for inventory because nobody bothered to steal them. There were faint clean rectangles in the dust along that shelf. Seven of them. The datapads that had sat there were gone, and the ones left behind had been nudged along to cover the gaps, but not quite enough.

There was a security cam above the door, a small cracked dome. Anakin reached up and popped the housing with one finger. The memory core was gone. Not smashed. Not ripped out. The four retaining screws sat in a neat little row on top of the cam's casing, where whoever had removed them had set them down, one beside the next, with the care of someone who had done this many times and would do it many times again.

Anakin looked at those four screws for a long time.

A thief would have taken the credits from the box under the counter. He checked; they were there. A rival would have left the place wrecked. CSF would have left tape, holo-markers, a droid. An angry customer would have left blood.

This was none of those. This was someone who had come in, done what they came for, and then put everything away. Even Pesk. Especially Pesk. His hands hadn't fallen in his lap like that. Somebody had folded them.

Someone's cleaning up, Anakin thought, and the cold that went through him had nothing to do with the arcade.

He crouched beside the chair. Up close the Rodian looked even more peaceful. Anakin made himself reach out with the Force, carefully, the way he would have touched a wound, and found nothing. Not the faint fading trace death usually left, like the warmth in a chair after someone stood up. Nothing at all. As though the room had been scrubbed of more than fingerprints.

Behind him, someone cleared their throat.

Anakin was on his feet with his hand on his saber before he'd decided to move. It was the Ugnaught from the next stall, the one that sold nothing, peering under the half-shutter with small suspicious eyes.

"He dead?"

"Yes."

The Ugnaught considered this without visible grief. "Figured. Didn't open this morning. Pesk always opens."

"Did anyone come by last night? Late?"

"Nobody." The Ugnaught shrugged. "Light went off at the usual time. Came back on at the usual time. Nobody came, nobody left. I'd have heard. Shutter squeaks."

Anakin looked at the shutter. It was half up. He looked at its runners. They had been oiled. Fresh oil, still glistening.

"Thank you," he said.

He left before the Ugnaught could decide to call anyone. At the end of the arcade he stopped at a public comm booth with a cracked screen and put an anonymous report through to CSF, deceased Rodian, Kesh Arcade, stall forty-one, and then walked four levels up before he let himself stop and lean against a wall and breathe.

He had not told anyone but Rex. Rex had not told anyone but Fives. And still someone had been there first. Someone had known which thread he would pull before he pulled it, and had cut it, and had tidied up the ends.

Either that, or someone had simply been more thorough than him from the very start. Someone who'd been cleaning up since the night of the hangar, one loose end at a time, and Pesk had merely been next on a list Anakin had never seen.

He thought of the masked figure on the catwalk above the Dovrin platform, standing still in all that chaos to watch him hold the stack. He thought of the turn the figure had made with Ventress's sabers. The tight, economical circle of a Temple defensive form.

He had told no one about that turn but Ah-soka. Not Fox. Not Tarkin. Not Padmay, not really. Not Obi-Wan.

He told himself, standing in a lower-level stairwell with rain dripping down the wall beside his face, that it was because he didn't trust any of them to do the right thing with it. And that was true. It was just not the whole truth, and he was too tired to look at the rest.

Scene break.

Padmay had the denial on her desk when he came in. She didn't need to say anything. He saw it in how she sat, shoulders squared, chin up, the way she sat in the Senate when she was losing a vote and refused to let anyone see it.

"They wouldn't hear it," she said.

He crossed the room and picked up the datapad. The seal of the Judiciary Appellate Panel glowed at the top, and beneath it ran three paragraphs of language so smooth he could feel his eyes sliding off it. No reviewable error. No new evidence of sufficient weight. The petition is respectfully declined. Three judges. Three signatures. Eleven days from filing to refusal.

"Eleven days," he said.

"They usually take four months to say no." Padmay took the datapad back from him and set it face-down, as if that helped. "I argued that the Dovrin attack happened while Ah-soka was in a cell, and that the tribunal never seriously examined who else could have had access to those nano-droids. I argued that the security footage of Letta's death has no sound and no angle on the door. I argued everything I had."

"And?"

"And there's a second paragraph." She didn't look at it. She'd plainly memorized it. "The panel notes that the sentence presently being served is not the sentence the tribunal imposed. It is a sentence of the Chancellor's clemency. An act of executive grace, they call it. And an act of grace, they say, is not a judgment and cannot be appealed."

Anakin stared at her. "So because he saved her life, "

"Because he saved her life, the law considers her case closed." She rubbed her eyes. "It's not malicious, Ani. I don't think it's malicious. It's just the way the law is shaped. Once mercy enters a case, nobody in the courts wants to touch it, because touching it means questioning the Chancellor."

"Then nobody can do anything."

"The courts can't."

He went to the window. Core-uh-sant spread out below in its endless glittering layers, and far down, under all that light, was an arcade where a Rodian sat in his chair with his hands folded. He hadn't told her about Pesk. He found he didn't want to.

"I applied for a visit," he said instead, to the window. "Two weeks ago. Narkis said no. Prisoners at that facility get no visitors in their first ninety days, at the Commandant's discretion, and the Commandant was very clear about her discretion."

Padmay was quiet for a moment.

"She asked you not to come," she said.

"She asked a lot of things." He heard how that sounded. He didn't take it back either. It was becoming a habit. "She asked Rex to look after me. She asked me not to visit. She didn't ask anyone to get her out, because she didn't think anyone would. I'm not going to prove her right."

Padmay came to stand beside him. She didn't touch him. She knew him well enough to know when he would flinch.

"Something happened," she said. "Today. You've got the same look you had during the trial, when I asked you about the figure in the mask."

"A lead went cold."

"How cold?"

He said nothing. She waited. She could outwait a Senate committee; she could certainly outwait him. But he had learned something, these last weeks, about how long he could stand inside a silence, and it turned out to be quite a long time.

At last she let out a breath.

"Whatever you're keeping from me," she said, "I hope you're keeping it because it's dangerous to know, and not because it's dangerous to say."

He turned and looked at her. In the light from the city she looked tired, and fierce, and he loved her so much it frightened him.

"Who's left?" he said. "If not the courts."

She hesitated. He watched her decide to answer honestly.

"The Council could petition the Senate to reopen the inquiry. They won't. And the Chancellor could do anything he liked." She held his eyes. "Ani. He's been very kind to you. Be careful how much of it you take."

"He saved her life."

"I know. I was there." Her voice was soft. "That's why I'm saying it."

Scene break.

The Chancellor's office was as warm as ever. Anakin had stopped noticing it after the first few visits. Now he noticed only that his burn stopped aching when he stood in that red room, the way it did beside a heater, and that he never quite wanted to leave.

"Ordaine," Palpatine said, coming around his desk with both hands out. "My boy. I read Admiral Yularen's report. Three batteries, and the third one you took, I am told, more or less by hand."

"The bombers took the other two."

"After you took the one that mattered." Palpatine clasped his forearm, mindful of the side. He always seemed to know where Anakin was hurt. "Sit, please. You look as though you have not slept since the last time I saw you."

Anakin sat. It was a deep chair, and he sank into it further than he meant to.

They spoke for a while about the campaign. Palpatine asked good questions; he always did. He wanted to know what the men thought of the new gunship armor, whether the supply lines on the Rim were holding, whether General Kenobi's strategy on Ordaine had been, in Anakin's honest view, a little too cautious. Anakin said that it had. He didn't mean to say it with quite so much feeling. Palpatine nodded as though he'd long suspected as much.

Then, gently, as though turning to something minor: "And your Ah-soka? I understand Senator Amidala's appeal was heard."

"It was refused."

"Ah." Palpatine sat back. For a moment his face was very old. "I was afraid it might be. I'm sorry, Anakin. I'm genuinely sorry. The courts do not like to look at a case once I have put my hand on it. I hoped they might be braver."

"The panel said it can't be appealed because you commuted it."

"Yes." The Chancellor looked down at his own folded hands. "Do you know, I lay awake over that? Whether by sparing her I would bind her. But a living prisoner may hope, and a dead one may not, and I could not let them take her life. I would make the same choice again tomorrow."

"I know." Anakin's throat was tight. "I didn't mean, "

"Of course you didn't." Palpatine waved it away. Then he was quiet for a little while. Outside the great window, the traffic lanes ran in their slow shining rivers.

"There is a thing I should not say," he said at last. "I should not say it because it is not a promise, and I do not make promises I cannot keep. But I think you need to hear that the door is not shut."

Anakin looked up.

"In wartime," Palpatine said, "the office I hold is a small, frightened thing. Every power it has must be justified to the Senate, debated, renewed, apologized for. Clemency in wartime looks like weakness. I spent a great deal of what little I had to spend on her life, and I will not pretend to you that I could spend any more of it now." He leaned forward. "But wars end. When this one ends, and it will end, Anakin, sooner perhaps than the Council believes, a Chancellor will have a great deal of latitude. There are always pardons at the end of a war. Amnesties. A whole galaxy wanting to put its wounds away and begin again. A great many sentences passed in the heat of the fighting will be looked at once more, in a cooler light."

"Hers," Anakin said.

"I could not single out one case. You understand. It would look like favoritism." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "But a general amnesty would not need to single anyone out."

Anakin found he was gripping the arms of the chair. His mechanical fingers had dug into the upholstery. He made them let go.

"The Council would never allow it," he said. "Not for her."

"The Council would not be asked." Palpatine said it mildly, as if pointing out the weather. "The Jedi gave her up to the Republic. Very publicly, as I recall. They made her a matter of Republic law and not Jedi discipline, and in doing so they made her a matter for me, and not for them. I don't believe they quite understood that when they did it." He sighed. "Master Windu, as it happens, has written to my office suggesting that I deny any request you might make concerning her. For your own well-being, he says. He feels contact would only distress you."

The words landed in Anakin like stones dropped into deep water. He felt them go down and down.

"He wrote to you."

"A very courteous letter." Palpatine's mouth thinned in what might, on a less kindly face, have been distaste. "I have not yet answered it. I find I am in no great hurry."

Anakin stood up. He didn't know why; he only knew he couldn't sit any longer with that in the room. He went to the window and put his flesh hand flat against the glass and looked out at the city without seeing it.

They had taken her beads. They had taken her name off their rolls. They had stood in a circle and voted her out of the only home she'd ever had and then gone back to their meditation. And now they were writing letters, careful letters, to make sure no one who still loved her was allowed to say so.

"How long?" he said to the glass. "Until the war ends."

"That," said Palpatine behind him, quietly, "depends a great deal on people like you."

Anakin closed his eyes.

When he opened them again, the Chancellor had returned to his desk, and was reading something, and had the air of a man who had said all he meant to say and perhaps a little more. Anakin thanked him. Palpatine smiled at him as he left, the warm, tired, grandfatherly smile that Anakin had known since he was nine years old, and told him to get some sleep.

He didn't sleep.

He went back to his quarters at the Temple, passing the memorial wall where the candles for the hangar dead burned in their long rows, and he did not look at them. He changed the bacta patches on his side the way Kix had shown him. He sat on the edge of his bunk in the dark for a long time with his elbows on his knees, thinking about Pesk's folded hands and four screws in a row and a door that was not shut, only very far away, at the end of a war that could last years.

Close to midnight his door chimed.

There was no one in the corridor when he opened it. Only a courier droid, already rolling away, and on the floor at his feet a slim Judiciary dispatch case, sealed in grey.

He opened it standing in the doorway.

Inside was a single datapad. When he thumbed it on, the seal of the Republic Judiciary rose up in pale blue light, and beneath it a single page of plain text.

VISITATION PERMIT. Republic Penal Facility Narkis-Nine. Prisoner NK9-2214. One (1) visit, not to exceed two standard hours, under supervision of facility staff. Granted by executive authority, the objection of the facility Commandant noted and overruled.

At the bottom, where a Judiciary clerk's mark would usually go, there was a signature he knew. He had seen it on medals. He had seen it on the order that commuted a death sentence to a life.

Below the signature, in the same hand, someone had added four words.

So she knows., P.

Anakin stood in the doorway of his quarters and held the datapad in his mechanical hand until the screen dimmed.

Don't come again, she'd said. She had said it in a cell, with her chin up, the night before they took her away. She had meant it. He knew she had meant it.

He was already calculating the jumps to Wild Space.

End of chapter.

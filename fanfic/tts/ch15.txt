Chapter 15. The Admiral's Visit.

For twenty-one days the Force had been coming back to Ah-soka in pieces, and none of the pieces was big enough to hold.

It came while she was cutting ice, a pressure at the edge of her hearing, like voices in the next room through a durasteel wall. It came at night as she lay on her bunk in 4-17, a faint tug toward the warmth of Tess sleeping on the other side of the left wall. Once, in the mess, it came so clearly that she turned before the tray dropped behind her. Then it was gone again, and the tray clattered on the floor, and she could not have caught it if her life had depended on it.

That was the part nobody had warned her about. Total silence she had learned to live in. This was worse. It was like being dragged up toward the surface of a frozen lake, close enough to see the light, never close enough to breathe.

She had not understood any of that the morning Vorne stood at the foot of her cot in Ring Two with the black cylinder in her hand. Ah-soka had woken to the sight of it and gone very still. For one wild moment she had believed it was over: a pardon, a mistake found, Barris's doing somehow.

"Healer Offee has filed a note," Vorne had said, in the same flat voice she used for quotas and meal times. "Prolonged dampening at full intensity, nerve inflammation, risk of seizure. Judiciary medical regulations oblige me to act on the written recommendation of an accredited healer." She had thumbed the cylinder twice. At Ah-soka's throat the status light had faded from hard blue to something paler, the color of the ice at the bottom of the cut. "This is a recalibration. Not a release. And not a kindness, Twenty-two fourteen. If you have a seizure in my facility, it goes on my record."

So that was all it was. The headaches eased. The ache in the tendons of her neck faded. And the whisper started.

Scene break.

On the day of the collapse the whole Deep smelled of solvent, because Admiral Tarkin was coming.

The inmates had learned it the usual way, from the guards' tempers. Floors that had not been scrubbed in years were scrubbed twice. The heat strips in the mess were repaired. Sergeant Holt had shaved so close that his jaw had a red rash along it, and he spent the morning shift on the terrace above Cut Five shouting at prisoners to stand up straight while they swung their picks.

"Two days," Tess said under her breath, setting her chisel against the next block. "Two days of this. Then he looks at us for half an hour and flies away, and we go back to being invisible."

"I've met him." Ah-soka levered her block free. Thirty-one. "He won't look at us at all. He'll look at the numbers."

"You've met the Admiral." Tess snorted. Her breath smoked in the blue air. "Of course you have. Is there anyone important in the galaxy you haven't annoyed?"

"Give me time."

Cut Five lay at the bottom of the Yard, deeper than the others, where the ice turned old and glassy and the refinery's pipes ran along the walls. Most of the pipes were cold. One of them, the thick return line wrapped in cracked grey insulation, was not. It carried coolant back from the tibanna stills after the coolant had spent the morning drinking their heat, and the ice around it was always slick, always weeping. The crews called it the Kettle and ate their ration bars with their backs against it on the days the guards were not looking.

Today the guards were looking. Commandant Vorne had come down onto the terrace herself, walking the length of the cut with Holt a respectful pace behind her, inspecting the shoring posts one by one. Her coat was buttoned to the throat. The collar control rode on her belt as it always did, a small black shape against grey wool.

Krasso was working two stations down. He had not spoken to Ah-soka since his fourteen days in the Well, beyond a nod in the mess that might have meant anything. Pell was on the sled line at the far end, hauling blocks with the bent-shouldered patience of a nineteen-year-old who had been told he would be doing it for thirty years.

Ah-soka was reaching for block thirty-two when the whisper changed.

It did not get louder. It sharpened, like a single thread pulled tight in a slack net. Something under the ice was straining. She did not know how she knew. She turned toward the Kettle before she had decided to.

"Tess."

"What?"

"Get away from the wall."

Tess had survived sixty-one years by knowing when not to ask questions. She stepped back. Two prisoners further along did not.

The return line split with a sound like a blaster bolt fired inside a barrel.

Steam erupted from the insulation in a white column, scalding, roaring, so thick that the far end of the cut vanished. Someone screamed. The prisoners near the wall threw themselves flat. Hot coolant sheeted down the ice, and where it ran the ice softened and sagged and opened in long dark seams.

Above them, the overhang groaned.

It was a shelf of old ice forty meters long that leaned out over the lower terraces like the brim of a hat. Ah-soka had looked at it every day for three months and never once thought about its weight. Now it was all she could think about. A crack ran along its base, fast as thought, and the whole shelf dropped a hand's breadth and stopped with a jolt that shook the floor.

"Out!" Holt bellowed from somewhere in the steam. "All crews out! Move!"

They moved. But the cut had only one ramp up to the terrace, and the ramp was at the far end, past the steam, under the shelf. Prisoners stumbled into each other in the white murk. A guard Ah-soka didn't know was trying to wave people forward with his stun baton and getting knocked aside for his trouble. The shelf dropped again, another hand's breadth. Chunks of ice the size of a crate began to rain down at the edges.

One of them landed on Krasso.

Not squarely; if it had, there would have been nothing to do. It caught his legs and pinned him face-down beside a shoring post, and the post, knocked from its footing, slanted across his back. He roared and clawed at the floor and could not move.

Tess had stopped halfway to the ramp. She was looking back at Ah-soka, and at the shelf above them, and Ah-soka saw in her face the arithmetic that everyone in the Deep did without thinking. There were twenty people under that ice. The ramp could take three at a time.

The overhang would not wait for that.

Ah-soka looked up at the terrace. Vorne stood at the rail, very straight, one hand already resting on her belt. Steam rolled up around her. She was looking down, not at the prisoners or at the shelf, but at Ah-soka, and her face was the face of someone reading a regulation she had never expected to need.

"Twenty-two fourteen." Her voice cut through the roar without ever being raised. "If I take it off, can you hold that?"

Ah-soka did not let herself think about what she was answering.

"Yes."

"Then do it."

The cylinder came off the belt. Vorne's thumb moved.

The blue light at Ah-soka's throat went dark, and the Force hit her like a wave hitting a seawall.

She had forgotten. That was the first thing she understood, on her knees on the wet ice with her hands spread: she had forgotten how much there was. Every life in the cut flared up at once, bright and close and terrified. Tess's heartbeat, hard and fast. The young guard's panic. Krasso's fury, hot as the steam. Pell somewhere far off at the sled line, his fear thin and high. And under all of it the ice itself, which was not dead the way she had always assumed, but full of strain and slow movement and old pressure. She could feel the shelf above her the way she could feel her own arm. She could feel exactly where it was going to break.

She reached up and caught it.

It was heavy. It was so heavy. Not the weight of a speeder or a droid, nothing she had trained for; it was the weight of a hillside, and for a terrible moment it pressed her down toward the floor and she thought she had lied to Vorne. Then she stopped trying to lift it and simply refused to let it fall. That was different. That she could do.

The shelf stopped moving.

The ice stopped raining. In the sudden quiet the steam hissed on, and twenty people under the overhang looked up at the shelf, then at the kneeling girl with her arms raised, and did not move.

"Go," Ah-soka said through her teeth. "Go."

They went. Tess first, grabbing the nearest two prisoners by their coats and hauling them toward the ramp, her voice cracking orders over the hiss. Then everyone at once. Ah-soka felt each of them pass beneath the ice, felt the shelf shift a hair as their footsteps shook it, and adjusted. Her shoulders burned. Blood ran warm from her nose and over her lip.

Krasso was still down.

She could not let go of the shelf to go to him. She did not need to. She found the slanted shoring post with a part of herself she had not used in four months, and pushed. It swung away and slammed into the wall. The block across his legs was harder; it was wedged, and the shelf above her trembled as she split her attention. She lifted the block anyway, a finger's width, then two, and Krasso dragged himself out from under it on his elbows with a hiss of pain.

"Up," she said. "Up, Krasso, or I swear I'll drop it on you myself."

He got up. He limped. He did not look back at her until he reached the bottom of the ramp, and then he did, once, his yellow eyes wide.

Holt and the young guard came last, half carrying a prisoner who had been scalded across the arms. Then the cut was empty except for Ah-soka.

She could feel everyone on the terrace above. She could feel Vorne at the rail, watching, the cylinder in her hand. She could feel the guards' rifles. She could feel the dome over the Yard, and the hangar beyond it, and the landing pad, and a cargo hauler sitting on it with its engines cold. For one long, bright moment she knew with total certainty that she could put the shelf down, walk up that ramp, and no one in the Deep could stop her.

Somewhere far away, very faintly, she thought she felt someone she loved. She couldn't tell who. It was gone before she could reach for it.

She lowered the shelf.

She did it slowly, guiding it down onto the empty floor of the cut where the shoring had been, letting the ice settle against ice with a sound like a long exhale. Powder rose in a cloud and drifted, glittering, in the blue light. When it cleared, Cut Five was half its old height, and nobody was under it.

Ah-soka stood up. Her legs shook. She turned and looked up at the terrace and met Vorne's eyes.

Vorne pressed the cylinder.

The silence came back. Not the whisper; for a moment not even that. It came down like a lid on a box, and every life she had just been holding went out at once, Tess and Krasso and Pell and the young guard, all of them gone, as if she had walked out of a crowded room into a sealed vault. She staggered. Then the recalibrated collar settled, and the faint pressure returned, just beyond hearing, thinner than she remembered. Thinner than it had been that morning.

She had thought she knew what losing the Force felt like. She had done it once already, on the transport, and survived it. She had not known that losing it a second time, after she had just had it in her hands, would feel like learning the first time all over again.

"Four minutes and nine seconds," Vorne said into her comlink, to someone in Ring One. "Log it. I'll file the report myself."

Scene break.

They put the scalded prisoner in medical and everyone else back in their cells two hours early, which was the closest thing to a holiday the Deep had ever had.

Ah-soka lay on her bunk with a wad of gauze pressed under her nose and stared at the ceiling. The blood had stopped. The shaking had not. Every so often her hands twitched toward the ice they were no longer holding.

A tap on the left wall. Two short, one long. Tess's knock.

"Still alive in there?"

"Mostly."

"Good." A pause. Through the vent slot near the floor, Ah-soka heard the rustle of Tess sitting down with her back against the wall, close enough that their voices didn't need to carry. "That was something, kid. I've been in this rock nine years. I've seen people die in that cut. I never once saw the ice stop."

"It didn't stop. I stopped it." She closed her eyes. "Then I gave it back."

"Mm." Tess was quiet for a while. "You know what you just did, though. Besides the obvious."

"Saved your life?"

"Besides that." A dry laugh. "Rule four. Never show them what you can do. You just showed every guard on that terrace, and Holt, and the Commandant, and forty prisoners who'll be telling it in the mess tomorrow. By next week the story'll have you lifting the whole dome."

"I didn't have a choice."

"I know you didn't. I'm not scolding." Something shifted in Tess's voice, slower now, thoughtful in a way Ah-soka didn't quite like. "I'm saying you're worth something different than you were this morning. To a lot of people. That's all."

Ah-soka didn't answer. After a while Tess knocked once more, a soft single tap that meant goodnight, and the rustle of her coat moved away from the wall.

Later, when the lights had gone down to the dull blue of the night cycle, someone stopped outside 4-17. Not a guard. Guards walked heavier, and they didn't breathe like that, a slow rasp through a scaled throat.

"Krasso," Ah-soka said, without sitting up. "You're out of your cell."

"Holt is drunk on the Commandant's good mood." His voice was low, careful with the Basic. "The leg is not broken. The medic says so."

"I'm glad."

"You are not glad. You are polite." A long silence. "Twice now, Toe-groota. In Cut Three you could have let me fall. Today you could have let the ice keep me. Garnac would have let me."

"I'm not Garnac."

"No." He made a sound she couldn't read. "It was easier to hate you before. A debt this size is heavy. I do not know where to put it down."

"Then carry it," Ah-soka said. "That's what everyone else here does."

He was quiet so long she thought he had gone. Then the footsteps moved off down the walkway, uneven, favoring the left leg, and somewhere below, faint through the floor, she heard a door slam and Holt's voice raised in a laugh.

She turned over and pressed her forehead against the cold wall and reached, as she did every night, for the whisper. It was there. It was so faint. She could not tell anymore whether it was the Force or only her own memory of it, playing back like a recording she couldn't stop.

Scene break.

The Admiral's cruiser came down two days later in a storm of ice crystals, and an hour after that, two clone guards came to 4-17 and took Ah-soka up to Ring One.

They sat her on a bench outside the Commandant's office with her wrists in binders and stood on either side of her in silence. The corridor was warm. She had forgotten that too; Ring One was heated properly, for the staff, and after four months in the Yard the warmth made her skin prickle and her mon-trahls ache.

The office door opened, and a guard led Merrick Tal out.

Until now he had been two things to her: a shape at the bottom of the shaft, saluting, and a voice through the vent in the Well. He was smaller than his voice. Thin, pale from fifteen months under lights that never went off, in the same high-collared tunic stripped of its insignia. He squinted at the corridor's ordinary light as though it were too dim to see by.

He saw her and stopped. The guard let him; this was a day when the guards were being careful about everything.

"The Jedi," he said. "The one who held up the mountain. I heard it through the floor. Holt couldn't stop talking."

"Former Jedi."

"Yes. They do insist on that, don't they." He glanced back at the closed door. "I'm being transferred. Core-uh-sant-ward, the Admiral says, for an exchange that has been about to happen for fifteen months. In a few days, when the next Judiciary hull comes through." His mouth twisted. "Do you know, I think I'll miss the lights. One grows attached to anything."

"You don't believe them."

"I believe they've found a use for me," Merrick said. "That's not the same thing as an exchange." He looked at her a moment longer, at the collar and the binders and the gauze still taped under her nose. "Be careful in there, Tano. He's very good at telling the truth. It's the thing that makes him dangerous."

The guard touched his elbow, and he went.

Then it was her turn.

Vorne's office was as Ah-soka remembered it: grey, spare, with a desk squared to the wall and a single window overlooking the Yard. What she had not expected was that Vorne would not be in it. The Commandant stood outside the door as Ah-soka was brought through, and closed it behind her, and her face as the door slid shut showed nothing at all, which on Vorne might have meant anything.

Tarkin stood at the window with his hands clasped behind his back. He did not turn around.

"Sit," he said.

She sat. The clone guards had stayed in the corridor. It was the first time since the tribunal that she had been alone in a room with him, and she was surprised to find she wasn't afraid. She was too tired to be afraid. She just watched the narrow line of his shoulders under the olive-grey uniform and waited.

"I've read the Commandant's report," Tarkin said. "Four minutes and nine seconds. Twenty-three lives, by her count, including two of her own personnel. She tells me she reviewed the regulations governing emergency suspension of restraint before she came down to the cut that morning, as part of her preparation for my visit." He sounded faintly pleased. "That is why I recommended her for this post. Most wardens would have hesitated. Most wardens would have had to look it up."

"Is that why I'm here? So you can tell me she did well?"

"You're here because I wished to see you." He turned at last. His face was exactly as she remembered it across the tribunal floor: long, hollow-cheeked, the eyes pale and patient. He looked her over the way he might have looked over a damaged cargo manifest. "The Senate has held a hearing on your collar, you know. Several of the more sentimental members feel it constitutes cruelty. I expect the Commandant's report will be very useful to me."

"Because I saved people?"

"Because you saved people and then allowed it to be switched back on." He came around the desk and leaned against its edge, close enough to look down at her. "Your collar can be lifted when the Republic requires it, and replaced when the Republic is finished. It does exactly what it was designed to do. And the prisoner wearing it chose to stay. That is a far better argument for the program than any I could have made."

She thought of the moment at the bottom of the cut, the dome and the hangar and the landing pad and the cold hauler all laid out in her mind like a map. She had not told anyone about that moment. She felt, absurdly, as if he had read it off her face.

"I'm not your argument," she said.

"You are precisely my argument. You are the first Force-user to wear one of these for longer than a week. There will be others." He said it the way another man might read out a supply schedule. "Whatever you do here, Miss Tano, becomes evidence. I'd advise you to keep behaving well."

Ah-soka looked at her bound hands for a moment. Then she looked up at him.

"You know I didn't do it."

She had not meant to say it. It had been sitting in her for four months, under the ice and the quotas and the long nights, and it came out flat and tired and true.

Tarkin regarded her for a long moment. Then, to her surprise, he answered.

"I know that it doesn't matter," he said. "I'm not certain anyone could convince me you did, if you want the truth. I'm not certain anyone could convince me otherwise. I've never found it a very interesting question."

"Six Jedi died. Clones died. Three troopers in the detention level. And you don't find it interesting?"

"I find the consequences very interesting." He folded his arms. "For four days the Holo-net showed the Jedi Temple burning. Every citizen on Core-uh-sant watched the Republic's guardians fail to guard their own front door. The public needed someone to be answerable, and quickly, and visibly. The Order offered no one. Your former Master chased shadows in the lower levels. And you were on the footage, and your blades were in the troopers, and you ran." He gave a small shrug. "The Republic needed an answer. You were the most useful answer available. You still are."

"And whoever really did it is still out there."

"Very possibly."

"Doesn't that frighten you?"

"Not nearly as much as the alternative." His voice did not change. "If the real culprit walked into my office tomorrow with a full confession, I would have to explain to the Senate, the Holo-net, and every grieving family in deep blue why the Republic sent the wrong girl to the ice. The verdict would be wrong. Every verdict after it would be questioned. It is far better for everyone, Miss Tano, that you remain correct."

She had imagined this conversation a hundred times. In the Well, in the cut, at night with her face to the wall. She had imagined shouting at him, or saying something clever and cutting that made him flinch. Now that it was here she found she could not say anything at all.

Because he was right. Not about her guilt; about what she was. She had spent four months thinking of herself as a prisoner, a person who had been wronged, someone with a name and a case and a history that could be untangled if the right people only looked. And the right people had looked, and they had decided that the name and the case and the history did not matter. What mattered was the shape she made on the Holo-net. The Temple Bomber. The Jedi Who Fell. The first prisoner in the collar. The Senate could debate her and the Commandant could log her and the Admiral could use her, and none of them needed her to be anyone in particular for that to work.

She was a symbol. Symbols didn't get appeals.

"Thank you," she said finally. Her voice was very steady. "For being honest. I don't think anyone else has been."

For the first time Tarkin seemed faintly, genuinely surprised. Then the look smoothed away, and he pushed off the desk and went back to the window.

"Commandant," he said, not raising his voice. The door opened at once. "We're finished here. Return Twenty-two fourteen to her cell."

Scene break.

The cruiser lifted off at midday. The inmates watched it go from the Yard, leaning on their picks, and nobody pretended to work until it had vanished into the blue overhead. The heat strips in the mess went cold again that same evening. Holt's rash began to heal. The Deep, which had held its breath for three days, let it out.

Ah-soka didn't. She went back down to Cut Four, since Five was buried, and cut her forty blocks, and said almost nothing, and Tess watched her sideways the entire shift without asking a single question. It was the kindest thing anyone had done for her in days.

At shift change Tess caught her sleeve on the way to the tool shed.

"Come here. Quick. Before Holt does his count."

The tool shed was a prefab hut against the dome wall where the crews racked picks and chisels at the end of each shift. Tess went straight to the back, where a stack of broken sled runners leaned against the wall, and knelt, and pulled a loose panel off the bottom of the rack. Inside, wrapped in a heat-pack sleeve, was a small, ugly device: the cracked casing of an ice-density sounder, held together with wire and sealant, with a leak-detector coil soldered onto one end and a scratched readout the size of Ah-soka's palm.

"Tess. What is that?"

"Nine years," Tess said, "is a long time to go without knowing who's in your sky." She thumbed a switch. The readout flickered green. "Smuggler's habit. Sounder picks up the dome's own sensor pulses and bounces them. Coil filters the noise. It's terrible. It's also better than nothing, which is what they give us."

"If Vorne finds that, "

"Thirty days in the Well. I know. Look."

She tilted the readout. A rough circle in the center, the dome. A bright smear at the edge, which Tess tapped with a frostbitten finger: "That's the Admiral's cruiser, climbing out. Gone in a minute." Then she moved her finger to the opposite side of the screen, low down, near the curve where the moon's horizon would be.

Three faint marks. Close together. Not moving.

"They came in an hour after the cruiser landed," Tess said quietly. "Low and slow, around the far side, where the dome's sensors don't reach. If I hadn't been watching the cruiser I'd have missed them. No transponders, or none I can read. They're not Judiciary, they're not supply, and they're not the Admiral's escort, because his escort left with him." She looked up, and her face in the green glow was older than Ah-soka had ever seen it. "And they're still there."

"Pirates?"

"Pirates don't come to the Deep. There's nothing here to steal." Tess turned the device off, and the shed went dark around them except for the blue light from the door. "Except people."

Ah-soka thought of Merrick Tal saying I believe they've found a use for me. She thought of Tarkin saying you still are. She thought of the cut, four minutes and nine seconds, and forty prisoners telling it in the mess, and how many guards and supply pilots and hauler crews would have heard that story by now and carried it out of here to wherever they went next.

Somewhere beneath her thoughts, faint as frost on glass, the whisper of the Force went suddenly, perfectly still, the way a small animal goes still in tall grass when something large has started to move nearby.

End of chapter.

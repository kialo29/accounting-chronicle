Chapter 6. Good Soldiers.

The order came down an hour after the sentencing, one line on a datapad: Remove TANO, A. (former Commander) from 501st command roster, effective immediately. Captain Rex had been looking at it for twenty minutes, thumb resting over the confirm key, when Fives threw his helmet across the barracks.

It hit the far bulkhead hard enough to leave a white scar in the paint and came back across the deck spinning on its crown. Nobody moved to stop it. It rocked to a halt against Tup's bunk, the black visor staring up at the ceiling like a man who'd been shot and hadn't finished being surprised about it.

"Life," Fives said. "Life. And we're supposed to call that mercy."

"Lower your voice, Fives."

"Why? Who's listening, Captain? The Chancellor?" Fives turned in a slow circle, arms out, taking in the long room: the two rows of bunks, the weapons racks, the strip of blue tactical lighting along the floor that never quite went off. "Hey, Chancellor. Thank you. Thank you so much. Very generous."

"Fives."

"She's seventeen. She held the line on Christophsis when she was fourteen and didn't know which end of a scope to look through. She pulled Jesse out of a hole on Umbara with her own two hands, "

"I remember," Jesse said from his bunk. He was lying on his back with his boots crossed at the ankle and his forearm over his eyes, the tattoo on his face hidden. "I was there. In the hole."

"Then say something."

"I am saying something. I'm saying I remember." Jesse didn't move his arm. "Also, I'm saying the Republic just told every trooper on this planet what we're worth, and it's less than one thin Toe-groota girl. Which I could have told you for free."

Kix sat at the end of the medic's bench with his kit open in front of him, inventorying bacta patches he had inventoried that morning. He had not said a word since they'd come back from the Senate district. He counted twenty patches, closed the kit, opened it again and started over.

Rex set the datapad face-down on his footlocker.

"That's enough," he said, not loudly. He had learned a long time ago that loudly was for battlefields. In a barracks, quiet carried further. "All of you. Fives, pick up your bucket. Jesse, sit up when your captain's talking. Kix, "

Kix looked up.

Rex found he had nothing for Kix. "Carry on," he said.

Fives didn't pick up his helmet. He stood over it, breathing hard through his nose, and for a moment Rex thought he was going to have to make it an order. Then Tup leaned off his bunk, scooped it up by the chin guard, and held it out.

"You dented it," Tup said. "Nice work. That'll scare the clankers."

Fives took it. Some of the stiffness went out of his shoulders, not all. "Thanks."

Tup sat back and pressed the heel of his hand against his right temple, hard, the way a man pushes on a door that won't stay shut.

"You all right?" Kix said. It was the first thing he'd said in three hours.

"Fine. It's the lights." Tup squinted at the blue strip along the deck. "Somebody in maintenance thinks we're fish. Been giving me a headache since the briefing this morning."

"Let me scan you."

"It's a headache, Kix, not a blaster bolt. I'll sleep it off." Tup lay down and turned toward the wall. "Wake me when the galaxy makes sense."

"You'll be asleep a long time," Jesse said.

Kix watched Tup's back a moment longer, then went back to his bacta patches. Rex marked it the way he marked everything, filed it, and let it go. There were too many other things tonight. A trooper with a headache was the least of them.

He picked up the datapad again.

Remove TANO, A.

He had removed names before. That was part of the job, and the job had never once been kind about it. Hardcase. Waxer. Boil. Echo. Each time the roster software asked if he was sure, and each time he was sure, because the dead were dead and the living needed accurate numbers. You did it quickly. You did it yourself, so that no one under you had to. You did it and then you went and ran drills until your legs gave out.

But she wasn't dead. That was what made his thumb stay where it was. She was alive, and in a cell eight kilometers from here, and the Republic had decided to remove her anyway.

"Captain." Fives had come up beside him, helmet under his arm. His voice had dropped. "You don't have to do it tonight."

"It's an order."

"Orders keep. She's still, " He stopped. "She's still ours until tomorrow."

Rex looked at him. Fives held his gaze. ARC training put a kind of steel in a man, but Fives had been stubborn before Ka-mee-no ever gave him a pauldron, and the steel had only given the stubbornness something to stand on.

"Get some sleep," Rex said. "That's an order too."

Fives went. Rex waited until the bunks had settled, until Jesse's breathing evened out and Kix finally put the kit away and Tup stopped shifting against the wall. Then he took his helmet off and set it on the footlocker beside him, so that whatever his face was doing, it was doing it alone, and he sat there in the blue dark with the datapad and did not press the key.

Scene break.

He found General Skywalker where he'd expected to: in the Temple's rebuilt hangar, under the belly of a fighter that did not need any work.

The new hangar smelled of fresh sealant and ozone. The scorch marks were gone. The memorial candles had been moved to an alcove near the east doors, a few hundred small flames in a recess of pale stone, and the maintenance crews walked around that part of the floor without seeming to think about it. Rex had been here the day after the bombing. He walked around it too.

"General."

A hydrospanner clanged against something. "If this is about the roster, I already know."

"It's not about the roster, sir."

Skywalker rolled out on the mechanic's creeper and looked up at him. He looked like he'd been awake since the verdict and possibly since the trial began. His right glove was still split across the knuckles, and the metal showed through, dull under the work lights. He'd been using that hand on the fighter. There were scratches on the plating that hadn't come from any tool.

"Then what is it?"

"The transfer's at dawn, sir. Platform Six at the Military Operations Center. I'd like to see her before it goes."

Skywalker sat up. For a moment he didn't say anything. Then he wiped his hands on a rag, slowly, with care, as if this mattered.

"They won't let you in," he said. "Fox has the Guard on it. Nobody but counsel and the Judiciary."

"Yes, sir. That's why I'm asking you."

"You think they'll let me in?"

"I think they'll let in anyone you sign for, sir. You're a Jedi General, and you just watched the Chancellor do you a favor in front of the whole Senate. People will remember that for about a week." Rex paused. "I'd like to use it while it lasts."

That got something. Not a smile, not quite. The ghost of the expression that used to come before a really bad plan on a really bad day. "Rex. Was that a joke?"

"Observation, sir."

Skywalker pulled a datapad from his belt and keyed something into it, thumb moving fast. He signed it with his mechanical hand. Rex watched the bare metal fingers press the stylus and thought about the nosebleed the General had come back with from the Dovrin platform, the way those same fingers had locked up and Kix had needed a servo driver to open them.

"Here." Skywalker held out the pad. "Authorization for one visit, escort of the 501st command staff. Thirty minutes. If Fox argues, tell him to comm me. Better yet, tell him to comm the Chancellor's office." His mouth twisted. "Apparently that works now."

Rex took it. "You're not coming, sir?"

The General looked past him, at the alcove of candles. "I saw her last night. After."

Rex waited.

"She asked me not to come again." Skywalker's voice was very level. "She said once was enough. She said if I came to the platform she'd have to be brave twice, and she didn't think she had it in her." He laughed, short and without humor. "She's always been better at strategy than me."

"Sir, "

"Go, Rex. Tell her, " He stopped. His jaw worked. The metal hand closed around the rag and kept closing until the fabric tore. "No. Don't tell her anything from me. She knows. She'll just have to keep knowing."

He lay back down on the creeper and rolled under the fighter, and Rex stood there for a little while listening to the hydrospanner work on a bolt that was already tight.

Scene break.

The detention level at the Republic Center for Military Operations was all white light and right angles, and Commander Fox was waiting at the end of it with his arms folded.

"Captain."

"Commander."

Fox read the authorization. He read it again. Under the red-trimmed helmet his posture said nothing; Rex had always found that the most irritating thing about Fox, and also the thing he respected most. A good officer did not let you see him think.

"Thirty minutes," Fox said finally. "Weapons stay with me. You'll be monitored."

Rex unholstered both DC-17s and handed them over butt-first. "I'd expect nothing less."

"She's been no trouble." Fox said it to the pistols, not to Rex. "Since the verdict. Ate what we gave her. Slept, or lay still enough that the monitors said so. Asked for water once." He paused. "I've had senators give my men more grief over a parking violation."

It was, Rex understood, the closest thing to an apology the Core-uh-sant Guard was capable of. He didn't say anything. Fox keyed the door.

The cell was three meters by three. A bench, a sink, a ray shield where the fourth wall should have been. Commander Tano, he could not make his mind call her anything else, sat on the bench with her back straight and her hands loose in her lap. There were no binders on her wrists. Someone had healed the raw places the binders had made during the trial, and the skin there was a little paler than the rest, new and clean.

Her mon-trahls were bare. He had known about the beads. Every trooper in the 501st had heard about Master Windu taking them; Jesse had said a few things about it that Rex had pretended not to hear. But knowing it and seeing it were different. The place where the chain used to hang was just a stripe of her own pattern now, white and blue, and he kept looking at it, and then making himself not look at it.

She stood when the ray shield dropped.

"Rex."

"Commander."

"You know I'm not, right?" Her voice was light, nearly the old voice. "They had a whole ceremony. There were forms."

"I didn't fill out any forms, sir."

That earned him the smallest flicker at the corner of her mouth. She looked him up and down, all of him, the armor with the blue markings and the scuffed plate on his left thigh where a droid had clipped him on Anaxes, and he realized she was doing what she always did before a mission: taking inventory. Checking that her people were whole.

"Take your bucket off," she said. "I want to see if you've been sleeping."

He took it off. She looked at his face for a long moment.

"You haven't."

"Neither have you, sir."

"I've been sleeping fine. It's very quiet here. Nobody's shooting at me." She sat back down on the bench and nodded at the far end of it, and after a second he sat too, helmet on his knee. The bench was too short for two people in armor and a prisoner. Their shoulders almost touched. "How are the boys?"

He could have lied. He thought about it. Then he thought about how many times she'd caught him at it, on how many worlds.

"Fives threw his helmet at a wall. Jesse's angry. Kix has counted the same bacta patches about forty times. Tup's got a headache and won't let Kix look at it." Rex turned the helmet a quarter turn on his knee. "We're all right."

"That's not all right, Rex. That's a squad report."

"It's a squad report that says we're all right."

She laughed. It was short and a little ragged at the edges, but it was real, and the sound of it in that white room did something to his chest that he did not care for at all.

"Tell Fives he owes the quartermaster a helmet," she said. "Tell Jesse he's allowed to be angry, but not at you. Tell Kix to make Tup get scanned, and tell Tup I said so, and that if he argues I'll come back and do it myself." A pause, very slight. "He won't know I can't."

"I'll tell him."

"Good."

They sat. Down the corridor a door hissed, and boots went by, and the sound faded. The monitor in the corner of the ceiling blinked its small red light.

"They're sending me to Narkis," she said. "Did you know that?"

"I heard."

"I looked it up. They let me have a datapad for the trial, and nobody took it back until this morning." She was looking at the ray-shield emitters, not at him. "It's an ice moon at the edge of Wild Space. The prison's cut into a glacier. They mine tibanna there, or refine it, or something. It's always cold, and it's always twilight, and nobody's ever been released from it, because nobody's ever been sent there for anything you get released from." She smiled at the emitters. "So. Bring a coat."

"Commander."

"I'm all right, Rex."

"With respect, sir, that's a squad report."

She turned and looked at him then. Her eyes were very bright, and she held them that way, the way you hold a position you know is going to be overrun: not because it'll matter in the end, but because the people behind you need the time.

He understood, then, that she was doing this for him. That she had decided, sometime in the night, how she would be when he came, and she was going to be that way if it killed her. She was composed because a commander is composed in front of her troops. And she still thought of him as one of hers, even if the roster didn't.

He looked down at his helmet. He had never in his life wanted so badly to put it back on.

"I need you to do something for me," she said.

"Anything."

"Don't say that. People keep saying that." Something passed over her face and was gone. "It's an order. My last one, so you'd better follow it."

"Sir."

"Look after Anakin."

He'd been expecting something else. He wasn't sure what. Find who did this. Clear my name. Get me out. He would have tried any of those. He'd been half-hoping she would ask.

"He's going to tear himself apart," she said. "He already is. He thinks this is his fault, because he stopped to save those people on the platform instead of chasing whoever it was. He'll never say it, but he thinks it. And he's going to go looking for someone to blame who isn't him, and he's going to find someone, and it'll be the wrong person, and he'll do something stupid." She drew a breath. "He's not as strong as he thinks he is, Rex. He's strong. Stars, he's strong. But not the way he thinks. Somebody has to be standing next to him when he finds that out, and it can't be me."

"He's a Jedi General," Rex said, and heard how thin it sounded.

"I was a Jedi Commander. Look how well that went." She shook her head. "Promise me."

Rex thought of the General under the fighter, tightening the tight bolt. The torn rag. Apparently that works now.

"I promise," he said.

"Good." She let out a breath, and with it some of the straightness went out of her spine, just for a moment, so that she was a tired girl on a short bench in a white room. Then she gathered it back up. "Good. That's done, then."

"Is there anything else? Anything you need? Anything I can, "

"Rex." Gently. "There isn't."

The red light on the monitor blinked. Fox's voice came over the speaker, flat. "Five minutes, Captain."

They didn't use them, in the end. There was nothing else that would fit. They sat side by side and listened to the quiet, and when the four-minute mark passed she stood, and he stood, and she put her hand on his vambrace the way she used to on the gunships before a drop. Two quick taps. Ready.

"Take care of them," she said. "Take care of you."

"Yes, sir."

He stepped back to the line of the ray-shield emitters. He put the helmet under his left arm. His right hand started to come up, the way it had come up a thousand times on a thousand decks, and stopped halfway, because she was not his commander. The Jedi Council had seen to it, and the tribunal, and the Chancellor, and the roster software with its little confirm key. There were regulations about rendering honors to a convicted prisoner. He knew them. He had taught them to shinies.

She saw his hand stop. She gave him a small nod, as if to say: That's all right. I understand.

Rex finished the salute.

He held it square, palm flat, the way they'd drilled it into him on Ka-mee-no before he was old enough to understand what any of it meant. He held it until the ray shield hummed back to life between them and painted her in faint blue. She didn't return it. Prisoners couldn't. But she stood very straight, and her chin came up, and that was enough.

Fox didn't say anything when he came out. He handed back the pistols. If the monitors had recorded the salute, and they had, it never appeared in any report Rex ever saw.

Scene break.

Platform Six was on the windward side of the Military Operations Center, high enough that the morning air came in cold and smelling of the upper traffic lanes. Rex stood at the edge of the restricted line with his helmet on. He was not supposed to be there. Nobody told him to leave.

The prison transport was a squat grey Judiciary hull with no markings except a registry number and the Republic crest, small, beside the boarding ramp. It looked like what it was: a box built to move people who would not be coming back. A platoon of Core-uh-sant Guard formed a corridor from the detention lift to the ramp. Red armor, stun batons, rifles at port arms. Rex counted them out of habit. Twenty-four, for one unarmed girl in binders.

They brought her out at first light. She'd been given grey thermals and a heavy grey coat with a blank stencil plate across the back, where a Judiciary number would go. She walked between two guards without being pushed. At the foot of the ramp she stopped and looked up, once, at the sky over the city, as though she were memorizing the color of it.

She did not look toward the restricted line. He had not expected her to. She'd said once was enough, and she'd meant it for him too.

Then she went up the ramp, and it closed behind her.

The engines lit. Heat came across the platform in a slow wave, and the hull rose and turned on its repulsors and began to climb, and Rex watched it the way he had watched a hundred gunships lift toward a hundred bad drops: not because watching did anything, but because the people inside deserved to have somebody see them go.

He thought about the lower levels. He had been down there with the General when she ran. He had been the one who set the squads to cover the drainage outflows; he'd drawn the grid himself, carefully, the way he did everything. Stun settings, he'd told them. She's ours. Bring her in safe. He had said it as though it were a kindness. As though bringing her in had ever been anything but handing her to this.

He had followed every order. He had followed them well. And every one of them, every clean, correct order, had been a step on the road to this platform.

It wasn't the first time he'd wondered what a good soldier was for. It was the first time the question had a face.

"Captain."

Fox had come up beside him without a sound. He'd taken his helmet off, which he almost never did on duty. His face was Rex's face, more lines at the mouth, a thin scar along the jaw. He was watching the transport too, now a dark shape against the brightening clouds.

"She'll be all right," Fox said. "For a while. Narkis is clean. The warden runs it by the book."

"That supposed to comfort me?"

"It's supposed to be accurate." Fox was quiet a moment. "There's one other thing. You'll hear about it eventually. Better from me."

Rex turned his head.

"The Judiciary sent a crate down this morning, before the prisoner came out. Sealed. Medical-grade handling, two technicians, a Senate courier to sign it over." Fox's eyes stayed on the sky. "It's a new device. For Force-users in long-term custody. The warden on Narkis requested it by name. They're fitting it on her during the flight."

"Fitting what?"

"A collar." Fox's jaw tightened. "The technician called it a dampener. He was very proud of it. He said once it's switched on, a Jedi can't feel the Force at all. Not a whisper. He said it was like, " Fox stopped, and Rex saw him decide to finish it anyway. "He said it was like going deaf. That they don't even hear themselves anymore."

The transport was a speck now. Then it was a glint. Then the clouds took it, and there was nothing over Core-uh-sant but morning.

Rex stood on the platform for a long time after Fox had gone. When he finally walked back to the barracks, Fives was awake, sitting on his bunk with his dented helmet in his lap, and he looked up as Rex came in and didn't ask.

Rex picked up the datapad from his footlocker. Remove TANO, A. The confirm key glowed under his thumb.

He set the pad down again, unconfirmed, and turned to Fives.

"Tell me again," he said quietly, "everything you remember about that night in the lower levels. Every single thing."

End of chapter.

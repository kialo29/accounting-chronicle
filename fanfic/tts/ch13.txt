Chapter 13. What Fives Found.

Tup died at three-forty in the morning, Tipoca City time, with the rain drumming on the recovery ward's dome and Fives holding his hand through a sterile glove that was supposed to keep them both clean.

There was no noise to it. That was the part Fives would keep. On Ringo Vinda there had been blaster fire and the sun coming round and Tiplee's blade lighting across the hub, and here there was only the soft chime of a monitor shifting from amber to a color that didn't have a name, and the rain. For the last hour Tup had been somewhere far away behind his own eyes. Near the end they had opened, clear for the first time since the station, and found Fives, and for a moment Fives had thought he was going to say something. Tup had frowned, the way he used to frown at a sabacc hand he didn't understand. Then the frown smoothed out, and his fingers loosened, and the monitor sang its one long note.

The Ka-mee-noans had told him it was a growth. They had told him with great gentleness that it had been removed, that the removal had been successful, that the patient's neural tissue had simply been too compromised to survive it. Regrettable. They used that word four times. Then two assistants in grey came with a gurney and a sealed sample canister no bigger than a ration tin, and they took Tup, and they took the canister, and Nahla Say turned her long neck toward Fives and suggested he rest.

Fives went to find the droid.

Ay Zee Eye Three was where he'd left it, in the diagnostic bay at the end of the quarantine wing, polishing instruments that were already clean. It was a slender medical unit with a bulbous head and a voice that apologized for things before they happened. Two days ago, when Fives had first stood over Tup's bed and said something is wrong in his head, I don't care what Ka-mee-no ordered, it had been the only thing in Tipoca that hadn't told him to calm down.

It had also run the scan. Unauthorized, unlogged, while Nahla Say was in conference with the Prime Minister. Fives had watched the image build line by line on the screen: Tup's skull, Tup's brain, and in the right temporal lobe, exactly where he'd been pressing his fingers since the night of the verdict, a small dark shape with filaments trailing from it like roots. Not ragged, the way the training holos showed tumors. Neat. Deliberate. Grown, Ay Zee Eye Three had said, and then, oh dear, I shouldn't have said that.

Two hours after that scan, Tup had been taken into surgery. Seven hours after that, he was dead.

"He's gone," Fives said now.

The droid set down the forceps it was holding. "I know. I monitor the ward. I am very sorry, C T five five five five."

"Fives."

"Fives. I am very sorry."

Fives climbed onto the examination table and lay back. The ceiling lights were too white. "Scan me."

"I am not authorized to, "

"You weren't authorized last time either. Don't log it."

Ay Zee Eye Three hesitated for a long, humming second. Then the scanner arm swung over him, and the light ran down from his forehead to his chin, and Fives lay very still and listened to the rain and did not let himself think about anything at all.

The screen built its picture. Fives turned his head to look.

There it was. Right temporal lobe. The same small dark shape, the same delicate roots. Quiet. Not swollen like Tup's had been at the end, not inflamed, not anything. Just there, the way it had evidently been there his whole life.

"It's in me," he said.

"Yes." Ay Zee Eye Three's voice had gone very small. "I took the liberty, while the scan was running, of checking the embryonic records. The structure is introduced at an early developmental stage. It is in the template. It is in every one of you."

Every one of you. Rex. Jesse. Kix, who counted bacta patches when he was upset. Echo, if anything of Echo was left. Hardcase, buried on Umbara with this thing still curled in his skull. Three million brothers walking around with a stranger living behind their right eye.

"What does it do?"

"I don't know. I truly don't. It interfaces with the regions that govern decision and impulse. In your brother it was, " The droid stopped. "It had become very active."

Fives thought of Tup on the hub, the sun sliding round, the rifle coming up without any hurry at all. Tup saying the same words over and over afterward, flat, like a recording. It was the order. Like something had reached into him and pulled a lever.

"Take it out," he said.

"The procedure killed, "

"Tup was already sick. His was already running. Mine's asleep." He sat up. His hands were steady, which surprised him. "If I go back out that door with this still in me, they'll put me in a ward next to him by morning and call it a virus. You know they will. Take it out, and give it to me, and then at least one of us walks out of here knowing what it is."

The droid did not answer at once. Then it went to a cabinet and began laying out instruments on a tray, small precise ones, and said in a thin voice, "You will need to stay awake. I'm afraid it will be unpleasant."

It was unpleasant. Fives would not remember most of it afterward, only the cold of the anesthetic on the side of his head, a pressure that went on and on, the smell of something hot and clean, and a strange moment near the end when the room tilted and he felt, very clearly, a door somewhere inside him swing shut that he hadn't known was open. Then Ay Zee Eye Three was holding up a sample vial to the light, and inside it, in a bead of clear fluid, was a grey-pink thing the size of a fingernail clipping, with filaments drifting from it like hair in water.

"That's it?" Fives said. His voice sounded strange to him. "That's all?"

"That's all."

He took the vial and held it in his palm and looked at it for a long while. He'd expected to feel something. Rage, maybe. Instead he felt like a man who had found the hand of a stranger resting on his shoulder and didn't know how long it had been there.

They came at dawn, and they came smiling.

Lama Su did most of the talking. The Prime Minister stood very tall in the doorway of the diagnostic bay, with Nahla Say at his shoulder and two Ka-mee-noan technicians behind, and explained in that unhurried, musical voice that an unauthorized procedure had been performed, that the droid would be wiped, that C T five five five five had clearly been exposed to the same parasitic agent that had damaged C T five three eight five, and that it was a credit to Ka-mee-no's diligence that the infection had been caught in time. The trooper would of course be confined for observation. The sample would of course be surrendered.

"It's not a parasite," Fives said. "It's in the template. Your droid checked."

"The droid is malfunctioning," Nahla Say said.

"The droid's the only thing in this city that's told me the truth."

He had the vial in his fist and his back to the wall and no weapon, and he was working out which of the technicians to go through first when Master Shahk Tee arrived.

She came in quietly, the way she did everything, robes the color of the storm outside, and she looked at Fives, and at the vial, and at the bandage on the side of his head. She didn't tell him to calm down. That was the first thing he noticed. She asked him what he had found, and she listened while he told her, all of it, Tup and the scan and the embryo records, his voice going ragged in places. When he was done she turned to Lama Su and said, quite mildly, that she would like to see those records herself.

There followed a conversation conducted entirely in courtesy, in which nobody raised their voice and Fives understood maybe a third of what was really being said. At the end of it, Lama Su informed them that the Supreme Chancellor's office, having been made aware of the incident, had requested that the trooper be transported to Core-uh-sant for examination by the Republic's own medical authorities. Nahla Say would accompany him. Master Ti might observe.

"The Chancellor will hear you," Shahk Tee told Fives afterward, in the corridor, while the Ka-mee-noans prepared the shuttle. "I'll see to that. You have my word."

Fives nodded, and thanked her, and meant it. He liked her. She'd looked at him like a man and not a malfunction.

But he had been in the barracks the night of the verdict. He'd thrown his helmet into a bulkhead hard enough to dent it, and he had never had the dent fixed, because every time he put the helmet on he wanted to remember. Commander Tano had been promised she'd be heard too. She'd been given a hearing, and counsel, and a tribunal with all the right furniture, and she'd walked into it believing that if she told the truth plainly enough the truth would hold her up. They'd put a collar on her and sent her to the ice. Rex had stood at Platform Six and watched the transport go, and come back to the barracks without a word.

You didn't hand the only copy of the truth to the system and trust it to give it back. She'd taught him that, without ever meaning to.

He went back to the diagnostic bay. The technicians hadn't come for Ay Zee Eye Three yet. The droid was standing perfectly still in the corner, the way droids stood when they had been told what was going to happen to them.

"I need you to send something," Fives said. "Before they wipe you."

"I am not, "

"Authorized. I know."

They did it in nine minutes. Everything Ay Zee Eye Three had: Tup's scan, Fives's scan, the embryonic template records, the droid's own analysis, high-resolution images of the chip in its vial. At the end, Fives recorded forty seconds of himself, bandage and all, speaking into the droid's optical sensor in a low fast voice. Rex. It's in all of us. It's what made Tup do it. They're calling it a virus and they're taking me to the Chancellor. If I come back, good. If I don't, and they tell you I turned, don't you believe it. Don't take my word for any of this either. Get Kix. Look for yourself.

Ay Zee Eye Three folded it all into a routine medical telemetry packet addressed to the Resolute's medbay, attention Captain Rex, sealed it with the cipher from the check-in channel Rex had given Fives at the shuttle ramp, and fired it off in a single compressed burst on the hospital's outbound relay, sandwiched between a growth-rate report and a requisition for nutrient gel.

"It's gone," the droid said. "I don't know whether it will arrive."

"It'll arrive." Fives put a hand on the droid's cold shoulder. "Thank you."

"I hope you are right about everything," Ay Zee Eye Three said. "And I hope you are wrong about what they'll do to you."

Scene break.

On Core-uh-sant they put him in a medical suite three floors under the Chancellor's offices, a white room with no window and a cot that smelled of disinfectant. Nahla Say gave him an injection for the transit sickness, she said, though he hadn't been sick. Shahk Tee was asked to wait in the anteroom. Two Core-uh-sant Guard troopers in red-marked armor stood outside the door. Fives sat on the cot with the vial in the breast pocket of his fatigues, the pocket buttoned, his hand over it like a man protecting a heartbeat, and waited.

The drug came on slowly. That was the clever thing about it. First his hands felt heavy, then his tongue. By the time the troopers came to walk him upstairs, the corridors had acquired a faint shimmer at the edges, as if everything were being seen through warm water, and he had to think about each step before he took it.

The Chancellor's office was red. He'd heard that from Rex, who'd heard it from General Skywalker, and it was still a shock: red carpet, red walls, red light through the tall windows at the end of the day. It was warm in there. Too warm. Fives felt sweat start under his collar.

Palpatine came around the desk to meet him. He was smaller than Fives had expected, and older, with a soft voice and a soft handshake, and he said Fives's number and then, kindly, his name, as if he'd taken the trouble to learn it. He thanked him for his service. He said he had heard about Ringo Vinda and was grieved. He said Fives must be very tired.

"Sir." Fives had to stop and line the words up. They kept sliding. "Sir, there's something, in us. In all of us. Ka-mee-no put it there. I've got it. I've got mine." He fumbled the pocket open and set the vial on the edge of the desk, and it rolled a little, and the grey thing inside it turned slowly in its fluid. "It made Tup kill a Jedi. I don't know who it answers to. But it isn't a virus, sir, it isn't, it was grown."

The Chancellor looked at the vial. He picked it up between two fingers and held it to the window, and the red light came through it.

"How extraordinary," he said. "And who else has had the privilege of seeing this?"

Something in Fives, some animal thing underneath the drug, went still.

He had told Shahk Tee. He had told the droid, and the droid was being wiped. He'd sent everything to Rex. The true answer was a list of names, and the question had been asked so gently, and he didn't want to give it.

"Sir," he said, "I'd like Master Ti to be here."

"Of course," said the Chancellor. He set the vial down on his side of the desk. Then he stepped back, quite calmly, and touched something on the desk's edge, and raised his voice for the first time. "Trooper, no. No. Put that down!"

Fives hadn't moved.

He was sure, afterward, that he hadn't moved, but the drug made the room lurch, and his hand was on the desk to steady himself, and the doors were opening behind him, and in the red window glass he saw his own reflection leaning over the desk at the Chancellor with his arm out, and it looked exactly like an attack. It looked exactly like a man going for the throat of the head of state.

The Chancellor's face, over his shoulder, was not afraid. That was the last clear thing Fives saw before the training took over. It was watching him the way you'd watch a game piece slide into the square you'd left open for it.

Then the troopers had him, and he did what a lifetime had made him do. One went into the desk; one went down with his wind gone. Fives came up with a blaster that wasn't his and a red-marked helmet rolling at his feet. Somewhere Shahk Tee was saying his name sharply. Somewhere an alarm had started. The vial was on the far side of the desk, in the Chancellor's reach and not his, and there were boots in the corridor, a lot of them.

He left it. He hated himself for that for the rest of the night, but he left it. He took the downed trooper's comlink off his belt, jammed it into his collar, and went out the side door at a staggering run.

Scene break.

The comlink told him what he was before he'd made it four levels down.

All units, all units. Clone trooper C T five five five five has attempted to assassinate the Supreme Chancellor. Suspect is armed and considered deranged. Core-uh-sant Guard has authority to use lethal force.

He heard it in a maintenance lift, with his back against the wall and his eyes closed and the blaster cradled against his chest. Deranged. He'd been a traitor to Ka-mee-no for a night and now he was a madman to the whole Republic, and it had taken them about eight minutes. He thought of Commander Tano in the detention center, with three dead Guards outside her cell and her own sabers in the evidence locker, and he understood now what that had felt like from the inside. Not the fear. The speed. The way the story was already written before you'd even caught your breath.

He went down.

The drug was thinning, but slowly, and he moved through the lower levels in fits, a stretch of clear, cold sense, then a stretch where the neon swam and the walls leaned in. It was raining down here, the dirty runoff rain that fell from a thousand levels up and arrived warm and smelling of metal. Speeders hissed past overhead. A Toydarian vendor shouted at him for walking through his awning. He kept his head down and his arm across his chest and walked like a drunk, which wasn't hard.

He passed 79's without meaning to.

He came around a corner and there it was across the plaza, the light in the windows, the number over the door. Through the glass he could see a table of troopers with their helmets lined up beside their drinks, shore leave, off-duty, laughing at something one of them had said. One of them had a 501st marking. Fives didn't know him. He stood in the rain on the far side of the plaza and looked at them, three or four brothers, with that thing curled asleep behind every one of their right eyes, and none of them knew.

He could go in. He could stand on a table and tell them.

And they'd hear it on their comlinks inside a minute, armed and deranged, and they'd look at the bandage on his head and the stolen blaster in his hand, and they'd believe the comlink. He would have. Yesterday, he would have.

He turned away from the window and kept walking.

He knew where he was going, though he didn't let himself think about it until he was nearly there. The old munitions district. Rex had walked him through it street by street after the trial, the two of them in civilian coats, retracing the night Commander Tano had been run to ground: where she'd crossed the transit line, which alley Ventress had used, the warehouse with the nano-droid crates where they'd found her. Rex wanted to know if anything had been missed. Fives had wanted to know how a person ended up cornered. You learned a district fast when you were looking at it with that question.

Two blocks from her warehouse there was another one, older, an abandoned coolant refinery with half its roof gone and the floor of its main hall made of open grating, because underneath it was the sump, a drainage shaft that went down farther than anyone had ever bothered to measure. He and Rex had stood on that grating and dropped a bolt through it and listened, and never heard it land.

He went in through the loading bay. Rain came through the broken roof in long silver threads. He found a corner where he could see both doors, slid down the wall onto the grating, and set the blaster across his knees.

He didn't have the chip anymore. He didn't have anything, except a bandage, a blaster that wasn't his, and a burst of data somewhere in the fleet's medical traffic that might or might not ever be opened.

It would have to be enough. He sat and shook, and waited, and didn't know for what.

The footsteps, when they came, were only two sets.

"Fives."

He knew the voice before he knew he'd raised the blaster. He lowered it. Rex stood in the loading bay door, helmet under his arm, rain running off his pauldrons. Behind him, a darker shape, cloak soaked, hands empty: General Skywalker.

"Knew you'd come here," Rex said quietly. "You always liked this place. Gloomiest building in the district."

Fives laughed, or something did. It hurt his head. "Did you get it?"

"I got it." Rex came a few steps in, slow, careful on the grating. "I've got a medical file I can't read without Kix and forty seconds of you looking like death. I've played it eleven times."

"Then you know."

"I know you believe it."

"That's not, " Fives pushed himself up the wall to his feet. His legs held, mostly. "Rex. It's real. It's in your head right now. Right there." He touched his own temple, beside the bandage. "Tup didn't turn. Something turned him. And I went to the Chancellor, I did it properly, I did everything right, and he, "

"Careful," General Skywalker said.

It wasn't a threat. It was almost gentle. Fives looked at him and saw a man who hadn't slept in a long time, gloved hands half-closed, something stretched very thin across his face.

"Sir, I didn't touch him. I swear on Tup, I didn't lay a finger on him. They drugged me. Nahla Say drugged me and he set it up, he wanted me to, "

"The Chancellor," Skywalker said, slowly, "is the only person in this whole government who lifted a finger for Ah-soka. The only one. Do you understand me? When the Council threw her out, when the Senate wanted her dead, "

"I know what he did for her, sir."

"Then don't tell me he, "

"I'm telling you what happened." Fives's voice cracked across the hall. "That's all I've got. That's all she had, too. Nobody believed her either."

Skywalker flinched. It was small, a hitch in his shoulders, as if the words had landed somewhere already bruised. For a moment nobody said anything, and the rain kept falling through the roof.

"Come in with us," Rex said. "Right now. Me and the General. Not Fox. We walk you in, we get Kix to look at those scans, we get Master Ti to say what she saw, we do it in the open where there are too many people watching for anything to, "

"That's what she did," Fives said. "She walked in."

Rex stopped.

"You remember. You were there. She told the truth, and she sat in a chair, and she waited for them to believe her. Where is she now, Rex?"

Rex didn't answer. He didn't have to. Fives watched it go through him, watched the captain's jaw set, and his eyes drop for a moment to the grating, and come back up with something in them that hadn't been there when he came in.

"Give me the blaster," Rex said. "Just the blaster. Then we'll talk about the rest. Brother. Please."

Fives looked down at the weapon in his hand. He didn't want it. He'd never wanted it. He began to turn it, to offer it grip-first, Light flooded the hall.

They came through both doors at once, red-marked armor, a full squad, rifles up. Floodlights on the shoulders turned the rain into falling sparks. At the center of the line was Commander Fox, the red on his helmet very bright, his pistol already level.

"C T five five five five," Fox said, loud and flat, the voice of a man reading from a card. "Drop the weapon."

"Fox, stand down," Skywalker snapped. "We have this, "

"With respect, General, you don't. I have orders from the Chancellor's office." The pistol didn't waver. "Drop it, trooper."

Fives's hand was already open. The blaster was turning in it, grip outward, falling. It was halfway to the grating when he saw Fox's finger begin to tighten. He saw it the way you see things in the last half-second of a fight, very clear and very slow: the finger, the barrel, the line from the barrel to the center of his own chest.

And he saw Rex move.

Rex didn't shout. He just went, one long lunge across the grating, helmet flying out of his hand, shoulder driving into Fox's outstretched arm a fraction of an instant before the shot.

The bolt took Fives high in the left shoulder instead of the heart.

It didn't hurt at first. It was more like being shoved very hard by someone he couldn't see. He went back a step, then another, and his boot came down on the grating at the edge of the hall where the rain had been eating it for decades, and the grating wasn't there.

It gave with a groan like an old ship. He felt it fold under him. His hand went out and closed on nothing.

The last thing he saw from the top of the world was Rex: on his knees on the grating with Fox's arm still pinned under him, helmet gone, face naked in the floodlight, staring straight at him. Rex's mouth was open. His hand was out across the gap, reaching. He was too far away. He'd always been going to be too far away.

Then the hall went up and away from Fives like a light pulled up a well, and he was falling into the dark.

Scene break.

He hit things.

Pipes, mostly. A cable that caught him across the chest and spun him and let him go. Something that rang like a bell when he struck it. Then a long nothing that went on far too long, and the cold of falling air, and the floodlights shrinking to a pale coin above him and then to a star and then to nothing.

Then water. Or something like water, thick, cold, stinking of coolant and rust, and he went under it and came up and went under it again, and his left arm wouldn't work at all, and he got his right arm over something hard and round and hung there in the black, breathing.

He didn't know how far down he was. Farther than the bolt he and Rex had dropped. Farther than anyone had ever bothered to measure.

Somewhere above him, faintly, a voice was shouting his name. Not his number. His name. The shaft took the sound and bent it and smeared it until it was only a shape, and then it stopped.

His shoulder had begun, at last, to hurt. He was very cold. The cold was coming into him through the wound, he thought, like water into a breached hull. He couldn't feel his left hand. He could feel the comlink jammed in his collar, though, pressed against his jaw, still somehow alive. It clicked, and hissed, and spoke.

Fox to all units. Stand down the search. The fugitive is dead.

A pause. Static. Somebody saying something Fives couldn't make out.

Negative. He went down the refinery sump with a bolt in him. There's no bottom on our scanners. Log the body as unrecoverable.

Another pause. Then, quieter, as if Fox had turned away from the pickup: General Skywalker, I'm sorry. Captain, get off the edge. Captain, that's an order. Get him off the edge.

The comlink clicked once more and went silent.

Fives hung in the dark and listened to the silence where it had been. Dead. Unrecoverable. Already in the log. He thought, from very far off, that he ought to be angry, and found he didn't have anything left to be angry with. He thought about Tup's frown at the end, puzzled, as if at a hand he didn't understand. He thought about a grey-pink thing turning slowly in a red window. He thought about Rex on his knees with his hand out. I got it. I've played it eleven times.

Rex had it. That was something. Rex had all of it.

The water moved against him, slow, pulling. His fingers were slipping on the pipe. He told them not to. He wasn't sure they heard.

Somewhere far above, the last of the floodlight went out, and the dark closed over the shaft like a hand, and Fives, C T five five five five, ARC trooper of the 501st, officially deceased, let go of the one thought he had left and did not know, as the cold came up to meet him, whether he had let go of the pipe as well.

Chapter 14. Narrow Paths.

When the official report reached Anakin, Fives had been dead for nineteen hours. According to the report he had also been sick for weeks.

It said so in the second paragraph. A neurological infection, picked up during the Ringo Vinda engagement, the same one that had broken C T five three eight five. Aggressive and rare. Its symptoms were paranoia, fixation and sudden violence. The Ka-mee-noan medical authority regretted that it hadn't been identified sooner. Every other trooper of the 501st had been screened and was clear. The matter was closed.

Anakin read it standing in the Chancellor's outer office, because he couldn't make himself sit, and when he reached the end he started again from the top. There was still grit under the nails of his left hand from the refinery grating. He had washed twice. It hadn't come out.

"General Skywalker." Dace Orrin stood at the inner door. He was rubbing his left wrist, the way a man touches an old injury that no longer hurts. "The Chancellor will see you now."

The office was red and too warm, as it always was. The Chancellor wasn't at his desk. He stood at the tall window with his hands folded behind him, watching the traffic lanes slide past in the grey morning, and he didn't turn around right away. When he did, Anakin saw that he looked older than he had a week ago. A faint yellow bruise showed on the back of one hand, where somebody had steadied him, or failed to.

"You've read it," Palpatine said.

"Yes, sir."

"I asked them to send it to you first, before the Holo-net had it. You were there. You shouldn't have to hear it from a newsreader." He came away from the window and lowered himself into one of the low chairs by the fire-screen, and after a moment Anakin sat too, because it would have been strange not to. "Please tell me you've slept."

"Some."

"Liar," the Chancellor said kindly.

Anakin almost smiled. Then he didn't. He looked down at the datapad on his knee. Aggressive. Rare. A virus didn't need anyone to have been wrong. It was something that happened, like weather or a bad landing. It had happened to Tup, and Tup had been sick, everyone had seen it, the hand at his temple for days and the headaches he blamed on the lights. Then it had happened to Fives, who went to Ka-mee-no as an escort and came back as something the Core-uh-sant Guard hunted through the lower levels with orders to fire. Nobody had chosen any of it. No one had failed to see it. It had just come.

He wanted that so badly his chest hurt.

"The Ka-mee-noans tell me it attacks the parts of the mind that govern loyalty," Palpatine said. "I don't pretend to understand the medicine. But I keep thinking about the moment he came through that door." He looked at the door, a plain panel of dark wood, and Anakin looked with him. "I've had people come at me before, Anakin. It's rather a hazard of the office. They look at you the way a slicer looks at a lock: a problem in their way. He didn't look at me like that. He looked at me as though I had broken a promise to him personally." He was quiet a moment. "I suppose that's what the illness does. It finds the deepest loyalty a man has and turns it inside out. There's nothing quite so painful as when the loyal turn out to be false."

The fire-screen ticked as it warmed.

Anakin didn't move. Inside the glove, his mechanical fingers had closed on the edge of the datapad, and he heard the casing creak before he felt it. He made the fingers open one at a time.

Palpatine saw. Anakin watched him see it. The Chancellor's face changed, the warmth going out of it and something like dismay coming in.

"Oh," the Chancellor said. "Oh, my boy. Forgive me. That was thoughtless. I wasn't speaking of her." He leaned forward and put his bruised hand briefly on Anakin's sleeve. "You know where I stand on that matter. I sat in that chamber and listened to Tarkin ask for her life, and I couldn't allow it. I wouldn't have done what I did if I believed she was some kind of traitor. That was a clumsy remark from a tired old man, and I'm sorry for it."

"It's all right."

"It isn't. But you're generous to say so."

Anakin couldn't remember the last time anyone had apologized to him. He tried to think of the last time and couldn't find it. Not the Council. Not Obi-Wan. Ah-soka had, once, for something small, a lost hydrospanner on Christophsis, and he had told her to forget it, and now he couldn't forget it himself.

"Did he say anything?" Palpatine asked. "At the end. I only ask because the delusions can be persuasive. I'm told they often come with a whole story attached. I'd hate for anyone in your legion to carry it around afterward."

Anakin was back in the refinery hall: the floodlights, the rain coming through the broken roof, Fives with his hands open and empty, talking too fast. Something in their heads. Something that had been put there before any of them were born. Orders they didn't know they had. General, please, you have to listen. You of all people. You know what it's like when they've already decided.

Then Fox's arm coming up. Rex's shoulder knocking it sideways. Then the bolt, and the grating, and the dark.

"Nothing that made sense," Anakin said.

"No," said Palpatine. "I don't suppose it did."

He didn't mention Rex's lunge. He hadn't put it in his own statement, and he had read Fox's report twice and seen that Fox hadn't put it in his either. Two commanders had left the same three seconds out of the record without saying a word to each other. Anakin told himself it didn't matter. Fives was down a shaft with no bottom on the scanners, and nothing in a report would change that. It would only cost Rex.

That was the reason, and it was a good one. He didn't look too closely at whether it was the only one.

"Go and rest," Palpatine said as he rose. "And Anakin, have you had any word from Narkis?"

"Not since my visit."

"Then I'll make some quiet inquiries. I'd like to know she's warm." He smiled, and the smile was so tired and so plainly fond that Anakin felt it in his throat. "Someone ought to be asking."

Scene break.

The rebuilt hangar still smelled new. Anakin hated it for that. The durasteel was too clean and the deck plating too bright, and the memorial wall by the east doors was polished so well you could see yourself standing in front of the names. Tutso Mara. Five other Jedi. The clones and the workers, listed in smaller letters underneath. Every time he came in here he read them, and every time he caught his own face looking back over them.

He had his interceptor's port stabilizer open and was retorquing a coupling that didn't need it. R2 sat beside the strut, whistling in a low, disapproving way every time the hydrospanner went round again.

"I know," Anakin said. "I heard you."

R2 whistled again.

"Then go and tell someone who cares."

The droid rotated its dome away from him, rolled three meters, stopped, and stayed exactly there. Anakin put the spanner on the coupling and turned it until it wouldn't go any further.

He heard Obi-Wan before he saw him. Obi-Wan had a way of walking in hangars, careful of cables, as if he expected the deck to do something treacherous. It used to make Anakin laugh.

"Rex filed his report this morning," Obi-Wan said. "It was very short."

"Rex's reports are always short."

"Not this short." Obi-Wan stopped on the other side of the stabilizer. Through the open access panel Anakin could see the hem of his robe and one boot. "I'm sorry about Fives. I know what the 501st means to you."

"Do you." Anakin didn't look up. "That's good of you. Did the Council send you, or did you come on your own? I'd like to know which version of you I'm talking to."

"Anakin."

"Because I'd guess the Council's relieved. It's very neat. A sick clone, a sick trooper before him, a rare infection and a report from Ka-mee-no. Nobody's fault." The spanner slipped off the coupling and rang against the strut. "You must love that. A verdict where nobody has to be guilty. Maybe you could have used one of those three months ago."

He heard Obi-Wan breathe in, slow, the way he did before saying something he had decided to say whatever it cost him.

"I don't believe she did it," Obi-Wan said.

Anakin stood up so fast that he hit his head on the stabilizer housing. He didn't feel it. He came round the strut, and Obi-Wan was standing very still in his plain brown robe with his hands tucked into the sleeves, looking at him with no particular expression at all.

"Say that again."

"I don't believe Ah-soka bombed this hangar, or killed Letta Turmond, or those three troopers." Obi-Wan kept his voice low. Across the hangar, two techs had stopped work on a gunship and were finding reasons to stay near it. "I've read the tribunal transcript more times than I'd care to admit. It's an elegant case, Tarkin's best work. Every piece fits. That's what troubles me. In my experience the truth is untidy. It has loose ends and things that don't fit and witnesses who remember the wrong colors. Tarkin's case had none of that. Somebody built it."

The deck seemed to tilt a little under Anakin's boots, the way it had in the Dovrin gallery when the stack began to lean.

"When?" he said.

"When what?"

"When did you stop believing it?"

Obi-Wan was silent for a moment. "I'm not certain I ever fully did."

That was the worst thing he could have said. Anakin felt it go through him like the plasma had on Ordaine, a white line of heat from one side to the other. For a second he couldn't speak at all.

"You sat in the gallery," he said at last. "For three days. I saw you. Every time I turned around, there you were with your hands in your sleeves, exactly like that. And you never stood up. Not once."

"I wasn't called."

"You didn't need to be called. You're on the Council. You argued against expelling her, I was there for that too, and then you lost the vote and you just let it happen. You let them take her beads. You let them put her in front of Tarkin. And then you sat and watched him ask for her life, and the whole time you didn't believe a word of it?"

"Yes," Obi-Wan said.

He didn't argue or explain. He just said it, and the plainness of it made Anakin want to put his fist through the memorial wall.

"Then go and say it now," Anakin said. "Go and say it to Windu. Say it to the Senate. Say it to the Holo-net, I don't care."

"Say what, exactly? That I have a feeling? That the evidence is too neat?" Obi-Wan's mouth tightened. "Tarkin would thank me for it. He'd have it read into the record as proof that the Jedi refuse to accept a lawful verdict on one of their own. It would cost her the last friends she has in the Senate, and it would not open a single door on Narkis. I've thought about it, Anakin. Believe me, I've thought of very little else."

"Then think less and do something."

"Like you?" Obi-Wan said quietly.

Anakin went still.

Obi-Wan didn't press it. He just looked at him for a long moment, the way he had looked at Anakin when he was nine and had broken something and was standing in front of the pieces. "If there's anything you know that I don't," Obi-Wan said, "anything at all, I would very much like to hear it. I'm not asking as a member of the Council."

Anakin remembered the masked figure on the catwalk above the Dovrin platform, the turn it had made coming out of his overhead strike. That pivot was taught in the Temple's third-year training halls to children not much bigger than the younglings who had once followed Ah-soka around on Ilum. He had seen it, and he had told Fox, and Tarkin, and the whole tribunal, that he hadn't recognized the style. He had kept his silence too. He had his reasons, and they had seemed good. That made it worse.

"I know she's on an ice moon wearing a collar," he said. "That's what I know."

He picked up the hydrospanner from the deck, ducked back under the stabilizer, and fitted it to a coupling that didn't need it. After a while Obi-Wan's boot went away. R2 rolled back, slowly, and settled beside the strut without a sound.

Scene break.

The ice was blue, and she was under it.

He knew it was Narkis, though he had only ever seen one grey room on Ring One. In the dream there were no rooms. There was the ice cut, terrace after terrace going down into blue twilight, and Ah-soka was beneath the surface of it the way a leaf is under a frozen pond. She lay on her back with her eyes open and her hands flat against the underside, as if she had been pushing.

The collar's status light glowed at her throat. That was how he found her. It was the only warm color anywhere, and it was blue too.

He went down on his knees and put his hands on the ice. The left one burned with cold. The right one felt nothing, and then it locked: the fingers curled and seized the way they had at Dovrin with the whole hab stack hanging off them, and he couldn't open them. He tried to reach for her through the Force, and the Force was there, it was everywhere, roaring, but where she should have been there was nothing at all. It was like reaching for a stair in the dark that isn't there.

She looked up at him through the ice. Her lips moved. He thought she said Skyguy. She had called him that only once since the hangar, in a grey room on Ring One, to make him promise.

Then the ice began to thicken. He could see it happen, a slow white cloud blooming inward from the edges, closing over her like a lid. Somewhere behind him a man was falling down a shaft and calling out, and Anakin couldn't turn around because if he looked away from her she would be gone, and he couldn't look at her either, because the light at her throat was going out.

It went out.

He woke with his right hand closed so hard on the sheet that the fabric had torn.

The bedroom at 500 Republica was dark except for the slow traffic lights crossing the ceiling. For a moment he didn't know where he was. Then Padmay's hand was on his chest, and her voice was low and close, saying his name, the name only she used.

"Ani. Ani. You're here."

"I know." His voice came out wrong. He tried again. "I know."

She found his right hand in the dark. She didn't try to pull it off the sheet. She worked her fingers in beside his and began to open the mechanical fingers one joint at a time, patiently, as if she'd done it before. She had.

"Was it Fives?"

"No." He stared at the ceiling. A speeder's running lights passed over it, blue, then gone. "It was her."

Padmay's hand stopped, then went on.

"She was under the ice," he said. "In the yard. I could see her. I could see the light on the collar, it's the only thing that shows down there, and I couldn't..." He stopped. "It went out."

"It was a dream."

"I've had dreams that weren't."

She didn't answer that. She knew what had happened on Tatooine. She was one of two people alive who did. She lay down against his shoulder instead, careful of the burn along his ribs, and for a while they listened to the city.

"I saw the Chancellor this morning," he said.

"I know. It was on the Holo-net. 'Chancellor shaken but unharmed.'" She was choosing her words; he could hear her doing it. "How was he?"

"Kind." Anakin turned his head on the pillow. "He said a strange thing. He was talking about Fives, about how the illness turns loyalty inside out. And he said there was nothing as painful as when the loyal turn out to be false. And then he saw my face and he apologized. He said he hadn't meant her."

Padmay was quiet for a long time.

"He compared her to a trooper who tried to kill him," she said.

"He apologized."

"That isn't what I said."

"He's the one who kept her alive, Padmay. He's the only one in this whole city who's even asking whether she's warm." His voice had risen. He brought it down. "He's an old man. He'd been attacked. He said a careless thing."

"Yes," she said. "Maybe he did."

He knew that tone. It was the one she used in committee when she had stopped arguing and started taking notes. He thought about telling her the rest: the three seconds on the grating, Rex's shoulder in Fox's arm, the bolt going wide of where it should have hit and the dark swallowing what was left. Obi-Wan's Like you? The Soresu turn on the catwalk. He thought about it for so long that her breathing began to slow against his shoulder, and he still hadn't said anything.

"I'm going to ask for another permit," he said instead.

Her breathing changed. She wasn't asleep after all.

"She asked you not to come, Ani."

"She asked me not to do anything stupid, too."

"And what do you call this?"

He didn't have an answer. He lay with his hand open on the torn sheet and watched the lights cross the ceiling. Every so often a blue one went by, the same blue as the collar, and every time it vanished at the edge of the window he caught himself holding his breath until the next one came.

Scene break.

On Seh-renno it was raining, the soft, persistent rain that came down out of the hills every autumn. Count Dooku had liked it since he was a boy.

He sat in the long gallery with a glass of Seh-renno red he had not yet touched and watched the Republic talk to itself.

The Holo-net feed was one of the Core's sober ones, which meant the anchor wore dark grey and paused before anything she considered important. Tonight the important thing was a Senate Judiciary subcommittee hearing on what the Republic was pleased to call restraint devices for Force-sensitive detainees. A senator from somewhere in the Mid Rim was asking, with commendable indignation, whether the devices had been adequately tested before their first long-term use. A Judiciary official was answering that they had, and that the subject was doing well.

Then they ran the file footage of the subject, as he had known they would.

It was the sentencing. He had watched it at the time, idly, in a dull week. The tribunal chamber, the senators in their tiers, the Chancellor in his high seat pronouncing death in a voice of great sorrow. Then came the pause, and the clemency, and the girl's face. She had stood through death without moving. At life she broke.

He froze the image there and sat looking at it while the rain ran down the tall windows.

Ah-soka Tano. Seventeen, or near it. Toe-groota. Trained since childhood by the Temple, and for the last several years by Anakin Skywalker personally, which meant she had learned to fight from the most dangerous and least disciplined swordsman the Order had produced in a generation. Dooku had crossed blades with Skywalker more often than he cared to count. Whatever the boy had poured into her, she had survived it, and the reports from a dozen campaigns suggested she had sharpened it. She had held a commander's rank since she was fourteen and had a legion's loyalty. Then the Order she had given her life to put her on a table for the Republic to carve.

He knew something about being handed back by the Jedi. He had at least walked out on his own.

Then the feed moved on to a clone trooper who had attacked the Chancellor, and some illness out of Ka-mee-no, and the anchor's careful voice wondering whether public confidence in the Grand Army had been shaken by two such incidents in so short a time. Dooku let it play without listening. Clones did not interest him. Clones were the Republic's to break.

The girl did interest him.

He had tried this twice before. Neither attempt was a thing he liked to remember. There had been Ventress, gifted and furious and devoted, whom he had been obliged to cast off at the moment she was becoming truly useful, because his Master had asked it and Dooku had not yet been ready to refuse him anything. Then there had been the Nightsister's brute, Savage, who was all strength and no mind, and who had told Dooku, more clearly than any failure in his life, that raw power was a poor foundation. What he needed was not a weapon. He had weapons, whole factories of them. He needed a student: a Force-user with the discipline of the Temple and none of its faith, someone who already knew that the Jedi would sacrifice anyone who became inconvenient, because she had been sacrificed herself.

He needed someone his Master did not know existed.

Dooku turned that thought over carefully, the way a man handles a blade he has just finished sharpening. Even in the privacy of his own mind there were things he had learned to think quietly. His Master saw a great deal. His Master took an interest in Skywalker that Dooku had never been invited to share and had never asked about. But a convicted girl on an ice moon at the edge of Wild Space, a girl the Republic itself had locked away and forgotten, was beneath anyone's notice. That was precisely her value.

She would hate him, of course, at first. Ventress had hated him at first, and so had he once hated the man who taught him. Hatred was not an obstacle. It was material. What mattered was what she would come to understand, given time and the right instruction: the Republic had needed her guilty, the Jedi had let it, and only one man in the galaxy had troubled to come for her.

He set down the untouched glass, rose, and went to the small console at the end of the gallery. It was not connected to any Separatist network or to his Master's channels. Its relays ran through a series of commercial beacons in Hutt space that had cost him a great deal and had never once been used.

The call took some time to connect. When it did, the hologram that rose over the console was blue and slightly unsteady. The broad brim of the hat came first, then the long narrow face under it. The breathing tubes at the cheeks hissed faintly in the transmission. In the blue of the holo Bane's eyes were only darker points of light, but Dooku knew their color well enough.

"Count," said Cad Bane. "Been a while. Figured you'd forgot my frequency."

"I forget very little, Bane."

"That a fact." Somewhere behind the bounty hunter a droid chattered and was ignored. "This a Confederacy job? 'Cause their rates went down last time I checked, and mine didn't."

"This is a private matter. It will remain one." Dooku touched a control, and beside Bane's flickering figure the frozen sentencing image rose: the girl's face, the collar not yet on her throat. "Ah-soka Tano. Convicted three months ago of the Temple hangar bombing and several murders. She is serving a life sentence in the Republic Penal Facility on Narkis. It is the ninth such facility, on an ice moon past the edge of the charted lanes. The inmates call it the Deep."

Bane looked at the image for a while. He chewed on something; Dooku had never wanted to know what.

"Skywalker's kid," he said. "Heard about that." He tipped his head. "Heard she got herself a fancy new necklace, too. Keeps the Jedi tricks in."

"It does. I'd prefer it stayed on until she reaches me. If you can obtain the warden's control along with her, so much the better. If not, my people can deal with it. You will have the Judiciary's schematics of Narkis within the hour, and the collar's full specification with them. The Senate entered most of it as exhibits when it debated the device's funding, and a Judiciary contractor was kind enough to sell me the pages it redacted, including the terminal in the warden's office that the collar answers to."

"Alive, then."

"Alive and unharmed. I want that understood. Not a mark on her that she didn't already have."

"Prison breaks cost extra," Bane said. "Republic prisons cost extra on top of extra. There's a clone detachment, Judiciary guards, a warden who don't take bribes, from what I hear, and a whole lotta ice between the landing pad and anywhere worth bein'." He spread his long hands. "Folks are gonna get hurt on that moon, Count."

"Republic folks."

"Just so we're clear on what you're buyin'."

"We are." Dooku named a figure. Bane's head tilted a few degrees, which in a Dur-ose was very nearly astonishment. "Half when you accept. Half on delivery. There is one more thing. Among the prisoners there is a Confederate officer, Commander Merrick Tal of the Third Raxus Picket. He has been held for more than a year for an exchange the Republic never intended to make. Bring him as well."

"Sentimental."

"Practical. If anyone ever asks what you were doing on Narkis, you were hired to rescue a loyal officer of the Confederacy. That is a story everyone will believe, because it is the sort of thing everyone expects me to do."

Bane's mouth stretched slowly into something that might have been a smile. "And if anybody asks you?"

"No one asks me anything," Dooku said.

Bane considered him through the wavering blue. Then he touched the brim of his hat.

"Give me three weeks," Cad Bane said, and the hologram folded in on itself and was gone.

Dooku stood alone in the gallery. Outside, the rain had thickened, and the lights of the palace grounds blurred into soft gold against the dark. On the wall screen the girl's face was still frozen at the moment of life, her mouth just open, the whole of her caught between one verdict and the next.

He stood before the frozen image a while longer.

"Let us see," he said to no one, "what the Jedi have thrown away."

Somewhere past the charted lanes, under a dome of durasteel and a sky of ice, a prisoner with a grey collar at her throat had just become the most valuable thing on her moon. Nobody there knew it yet.

End of chapter.

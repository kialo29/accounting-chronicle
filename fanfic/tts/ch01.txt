Part One: The Verdict.

Chapter 1. The Masked Jedi.

The Jedi Council had given Anakin Skywalker until dawn to save his Padawan, and he had spent the first four hours of it wading through runoff in the old munitions district, following a trail he could not see.

The water came up past his boots. It was warm, which was worse than cold, and it smelled of coolant and rust and something sweet that had gone bad a long time ago. Six hundred levels above him the senators were asleep, and the Council was asleep, or meditating, which in Anakin's experience was mostly the same thing with better posture. Admiral Tarkin was probably asleep too. Men like Tarkin slept very well.

The Council will give the Admiral its answer at first light, Obi-Wan had said in the corridor outside the chamber, with that careful, reasonable voice he used when he already knew Anakin was going to do something stupid. Go and rest. There is nothing more to be done tonight.

There was one thing. There was Ventress.

She had been in the warehouse when they found Ah-soka on her knees among the nano-droid crates, staring at her own empty hands. She had been there, and then she hadn't, and nobody but Ah-soka seemed to think that mattered. Fox's people had written her into the report as an accomplice. Tarkin had read the report aloud to the Council in a tone that suggested he had enjoyed every line.

Anakin stopped at a junction where three drainage tunnels met under a cracked lumen strip. He closed his eyes and let the sounds go: the drip, the distant thunder of a cargo line, the hum of the city pressing down on everything below it.

He had fought Asajj Ventress more times than he could count. On Yavin. On Tatooine. In the dark, on ships, in places he didn't like to remember. Her presence in the Force had always felt to him like a bruise that had gone cold, an ache you could locate with your eyes shut. It was faint now, fainter than he'd ever felt it. She was hurt. She was trying to be small.

But she was there. Up and to the left.

He opened his eyes and went up.

Scene break.

The maintenance gallery hung over the Dovrin Line transit platform like a balcony nobody was supposed to stand on. It was a narrow lattice of grating and old cable runs, strung between the support pylons that held up a hab stack twenty storeys tall, a whole crooked tower of apartments bolted to the underside of the level above. Through the grating, Anakin could see the platform below: a long slab of ferrocrete under flickering signal lights, and more people than he would have expected at this hour. Shift workers. Haulers. A Gran woman with a sleeping child on her shoulder. A pair of loader droids arguing in binary about a crate.

Ventress was sitting with her back against a pylon at the far end of the gallery, one knee drawn up, a chemical cold pack pressed to the side of her skull. She did not look up when he dropped onto the grating.

"Skywalker." She said his name the way other people spat. "If you've come to arrest me, you'll have to wait. I'm in the middle of being humiliated."

"I'm not here to arrest you."

"Then you're here to fight me, and I'll warn you now, you'll find it disappointing." She lowered the cold pack. A long purple bruise ran from her temple down across her cheekbone, and there was dried blood at the corner of her mouth. Her hands were empty. Her belt was empty. Anakin had never once seen her without the curved hilts at her hips, and the absence of them was so wrong that for a moment he simply stared.

She saw where he was looking. Her mouth thinned.

"Yes," she said. "Go on. Enjoy it."

"Who took them?"

"If I knew that, they'd be dead and I'd be armed." She pushed herself higher against the pylon, wincing. "Someone in a mask came out of the rafters in that warehouse and put me on the floor before I saw them move. When I woke up, my sabers were gone and your little Padawan was being dragged out by a dozen of Fox's toy soldiers. A very productive evening for everyone."

Anakin crouched, putting himself at her eye level, keeping his hands loose and away from his own saber. "They're going to expel her," he said. "At dawn. The Council's going to hand her to a military tribunal so Tarkin can try her for the bombing, and for Letta Turmond, and for the three clones who died outside her cell. He wants her executed."

For a long moment Ventress just looked at him. Something moved behind her face and was put away again.

"And you want me to stand in front of a Republic court," she said, "and tell them she's innocent."

"You were with her. You know she didn't do it."

"I know she couldn't have done it. That's not the same thing, and no court in this galaxy cares about the difference." She laughed, a short dry sound that turned into a cough. "Your Padawan couldn't plan a bombing if you drew her the schematics. She spent half of our time together lecturing me about trust. Do you know how tedious that is, Skywalker? Being lectured about trust by someone who's been betrayed by everyone she trusts?"

"Then help her."

"Why would I?"

Because you like her, Anakin thought, and had the sense not to say it. "Because whoever framed her took your sabers," he said instead. "And I'm the only person on Core-uh-sant who wants to find them as badly as you do."

That landed. He saw it land. Ventress turned the cold pack over in her hands, her long pale fingers working at the seam.

"Whoever was in that mask," she said slowly, "knew where we would be. They were already there, waiting in the dark, with a story set up and nano-droids stacked in crates like a gift. Nobody followed us. I would know. And there's only one reason they could have known where to wait, because your girl told, "

Anakin felt it a heartbeat before he heard it. A shift in the air above them, like a held breath let go.

He threw himself sideways. Ventress was already moving.

Two blades of red light came down through the place where her head had been and cut the pylon at her back a hand's width deep. Molten metal sprayed across the grating. The figure that had dropped from the cable runs above landed in a crouch between them, cloak settling, and rose.

It wore a mask. A bone-white thing with dark slanted eye-slits, made to look like something out of a Dathomiri nightmare, and a deep hood over it. The two lightsabers in its hands had curved hilts.

"Oh," said Ventress, very softly. "Oh, you thief."

Scene break.

It went at her first.

That was the thing Anakin would replay for a long time afterward: that it didn't care about him at all. It turned its back on a Jedi Knight with a lit saber to go after an unarmed woman with a head wound, and it moved so fast and so cleanly that Ventress, who had been killing people with those blades for half her life, barely got out of the way.

She didn't try to block. She had nothing to block with. She flung out a hand and the Force went with it, a shove that should have put the masked figure through the railing. The figure leaned into it like someone walking into wind, and kept coming.

Anakin's saber snapped on. He went in low and hard, and the figure turned, one red blade dropping to catch his, the other still chasing Ventress across the gallery.

"Don't let it corner you!" he shouted.

"Thank you, General," Ventress snarled. "I would never have thought of that."

She ripped a coil of cable loose from the ceiling and lashed it across the figure's mask. It staggered. Anakin pressed, cutting high, low, high again, driving with his shoulders, using his weight the way he did when he wanted a fight over fast.

The figure gave ground, but it gave it the way water gives. Each of his blows met a blade that was already there. Circles, tight and closed and patient, both red sabers turning in short economical arcs that never wasted a centimetre, never reached, never showed off. It didn't fight like Ventress. Ventress fought like a storm, all flourish and fury. This was something else.

This was the training salle. This was every morning of his childhood, a line of initiates in grey tunics drilling the same defensive sequence until their arms shook, while a Master walked the rows and corrected the angle of their wrists.

The thought chilled him so badly he almost missed the next cut.

"Who are you?" he said.

The mask gave him nothing. The figure feinted at his face, he parried, and it used his parry to spin away from him toward Ventress, who had circled behind it with a length of broken conduit in both hands like a club.

Ventress swung. The figure ducked under it and drove one red blade up, and Ventress twisted aside so that the saber only took the conduit and not her ribs. She stumbled. The figure's free hand came up, open-palmed, and Anakin felt the Force gather in it like a fist closing.

It struck Ventress in the chest.

She went backward through the gallery railing as if the railing weren't there. Anakin lunged, reaching, his own hand outstretched, feeling for her in the Force. For one instant he had her. He could feel her weight, the pull of her falling, the fury in her.

Then something hit him from the side, a second shove, and his grip broke.

Ventress fell into the gap between the gallery and the next pylon, past the edge of the transit platform, down into the dark beneath it where the old cargo lines ran. He saw her pale face turned up toward him. She didn't scream. She was gone before she could have.

"No, "

He reached for her again and found nothing. No presence. No cold bruise in the Force. Either she was dead, or she was hiding from him the way she'd been hiding from everyone all night, and from up here he could not tell which.

When he turned, the masked figure was already running.

Scene break.

It went down, not up. That surprised him. It vaulted the railing on the platform side and dropped three storeys onto the roof of a stalled transit car, rolled, and came up sprinting along the car's spine toward the far end of the platform.

Anakin went after it.

He landed hard on the car roof and felt the impact in his knees. Below him, people were starting to look up: at the noise, at the red light, at the Jedi running across the top of the train. Somebody screamed. The Gran woman pulled her child tighter. The two loader droids stopped arguing.

The figure reached the end of the car and leapt, not to the platform but to the nearest of the great support pylons that held the hab stack up, catching a maintenance rung with one hand. The red sabers had gone dark. It clung there for a second, and Anakin saw it press something against the pylon's base, something small and silver that caught the signal lights.

He knew what it was before his mind supplied the word. He had spent the last week staring at pictures of them.

Nano-droids.

"Everybody off the platform!" he roared. "Now!"

The figure kicked away from the pylon and dropped onto the catwalk on the far side, and the silver thing on the pylon flared white.

There was no fireball. That was the horrible part, how quiet it was. A hiss, a crawling glitter like frost spreading across metal, and then the pylon's base simply came apart, durasteel sloughing off it in sheets as if it had been eaten from the inside. The pylon bowed. The ones on either side of it groaned with the new weight.

Above the platform, twenty storeys of hab stack began to lean.

It came slowly at first. A few bolts sheared off the ceiling with sharp cracks like blasterfire, and dust and old paint rained down across the crowd. Then a balcony tore loose from the third level of the stack and fell, and hit the ferrocrete twenty metres from the Gran woman and her child, and the whole platform began to scream.

Anakin looked up at the tower of apartments. Lights were on in some of the windows. There were people in there. There were people asleep in there.

He looked across the platform at the catwalk.

The masked figure was standing at the end of it, beside an access hatch into the service tunnels. It wasn't running anymore. It had stopped, half turned, and it was watching him.

Waiting, he thought. It was waiting to see what he would do.

He could reach it. Three jumps. Maybe four. He could have it on the ground with its mask torn off in less time than it would take the hab stack to finish falling. He could drag it up six hundred levels by the hood and throw it on the floor of the Council chamber and say, There. There's your bomber. Now give her back.

Somewhere in the hab stack, a child started crying.

Anakin lifted his hands.

He had held up a great many things in his life. Ships. Walls. A gunship's weight on Christophsis while Rex's men climbed out of it. He had never held up anything like this. The Force came to him in a rush, and he flung it upward and caught the stack, felt the impossible mass of it settle against him the way a mountain might settle on a man's shoulders. Ferrocrete and durasteel and plumbing and furniture and sleeping people, all of it straining toward the ground, all of it pressing down on the inside of his skull.

The leaning stopped.

He heard himself make a sound through his teeth. His right hand, the metal one, had locked open with the fingers splayed so hard that the servos whined. His left hand trembled.

"Move!" he shouted, and his voice cracked. "Get out from under it! Go! Go!"

They went. He couldn't watch them, not properly, but he felt them: a tide of panic flowing toward the far exits, a knot of people at the stairs, an old Ithorian who had fallen and someone dragging him up by the arm. Doors in the hab stack banged open. Figures in sleep-clothes came pouring down the external stairways, carrying children, carrying nothing, carrying a caged bird, carrying a holo-projector, of all things. The Gran woman ran past beneath his outstretched hands with her child pressed to her shoulder and did not look back.

The weight shifted. Something inside the stack gave, a floor dropping onto the floor below, and the whole tower slid half a metre toward him. He dropped to one knee. Blood was running warm out of his nose and over his lip. He could taste it.

Not yet. Not yet. Not yet.

He did not look at the catwalk. He did not let himself.

When the last of them had cleared the stairs, a pair of dock workers who turned at the bottom to stare up at him, he found the empty end of the platform with his mind. He began to let the stack down. Slowly. A centimetre at a time. Easing it away from the remaining pylons and onto the bare ferrocrete where no one stood, letting each floor settle onto the one below it with long groaning crunches that shook the platform under his knee.

The last of it came to rest with a sound like a ship's hull taking a hit. Dust rolled across the platform in a grey wave and swallowed him.

Anakin let go.

For a while he just knelt there in the dust, breathing. His hands were still raised. He lowered them. The metal fingers would not close properly; he had to force them with his other hand.

Then he made himself look at the catwalk.

The access hatch at the end of it was open. The catwalk was empty.

Of course it was.

Scene break.

The Core-uh-sant Guard arrived nineteen minutes later, which Anakin knew because he had nothing better to do than count.

They came down the platform stairs in a disciplined red-and-white stream, rifles up, sweeping corners, and spread out across the ruin as if they expected the ruin to open fire. Medics broke off toward the injured huddled at the exits. Two troopers started cordoning off the collapsed stack with glowing tape, which struck Anakin as optimistic.

Commander Fox walked straight to him. He didn't take his helmet off.

"General Skywalker." Fox looked from him to the wreckage and back, and the angle of the helmet suggested a man doing sums. "Sir, are you injured?"

"I'm fine."

"You're bleeding, sir."

Anakin wiped his lip with the back of his glove and looked at the smear. "Then I'm fine and bleeding."

Fox let that go. He had a datapad in one hand and a recorder clipped at his collar, its small light glowing red. "I'll need your account, General. For the record."

So Anakin gave it to him. Ventress in the maintenance gallery. The masked figure dropping from the cable runs. Red lightsabers with curved hilts. The fight, the fall, the charge on the pylon. The stack.

Fox listened without interrupting. When Anakin finished, there was a short silence filled by the hiss of a medic's spray and someone, somewhere, sobbing.

"A masked figure," Fox said, "armed with two red lightsabers. Curved hilts."

"Yes."

"Sir, with respect. That is the description on file for Asajj Ventress."

"Ventress was standing next to me, Commander. It threw her off a ledge."

"You saw her body?"

Anakin opened his mouth, and shut it again.

"No," he said.

Fox made a note. Anakin could not see the note, but he could see the way the trooper's thumb moved on the datapad, short and efficient. "Then it's possible," Fox said, "that what you witnessed was a staged separation. Ventress and an accomplice. The accomplice draws you off, Ventress drops out of sight and goes to ground, and the two of them meet up later. She's done similar things before."

"That's not what happened."

"Yes, sir. I'm only noting the possibility." Fox's helmet tilted toward the ruined pylon. "The accomplice used nano-droid explosives. Same type as the Temple bombing. Same type we recovered from the warehouse where Commander Tano was apprehended."

"Former Commander, by morning," said another trooper, quietly, from somewhere behind Fox.

Fox turned his head a few degrees. The trooper found something very interesting to do with his cordon tape.

Anakin stood up. It took him longer than he would have liked.

"Commander Tano," he said, "is locked in a cell at the military base under your own guard. She didn't plant that charge. She didn't throw Ventress off that gallery. Whoever did is still out here, and they fought like, " He stopped.

Fox waited. "Like what, sir?"

Like a Jedi. The words were right there, and he could not say them. Not here, not to a Core-uh-sant Guard recorder with Tarkin waiting to read the transcript over breakfast. Not when the Council was going to vote at dawn on whether a Jedi had already done exactly this.

"Like someone who knew what they were doing," Anakin said.

"Yes, sir." Fox made another note. "It'll go in the report. All of it."

He said it as if it were meant to be reassuring.

Anakin looked past him at the dust still drifting over the platform, at the people huddled under foil blankets near the stairs, at the Gran woman sitting on a bench with her child asleep again in her lap as if nothing at all had happened. Nobody was dead. The medics had said so, twice, as if they couldn't quite believe it themselves. A broken arm, a lot of cuts, an old Ithorian with a cracked rib. Nobody was dead.

He had done that. He knew he had done that.

And he knew, with a clarity that made him feel sick, that the one person on Core-uh-sant who could have proved Ah-soka innocent had been standing at the end of that catwalk, watching him, and he had let it walk away.

His comlink chimed. He looked at it. Kenobi. The fourth call tonight. Above the city, somewhere far beyond all those levels of durasteel, the sky would be starting to grey.

He didn't answer. He didn't know how he would say it.

I had it, Master. I had them, and I chose.

He turned off the comlink and walked toward the stairs, and behind him Fox's recorder went on glowing, small and red and patient, taking down every word.

Scene break.

Three levels up and half a kilometre east, in an alley that smelled of hot grease and wet stone, Barris Offee stopped running because her legs would not let her run any further.

She stood with her back against a wall, beneath a vent that was breathing out steam in slow, damp gusts, and listened. Nothing. A speeder's whine, far off. The drip of condensation off a broken gutter. No boots. No shouts. No Anakin Skywalker coming down out of the dark with his saber lit and his face like a thunderhead.

He had not come. She had known he would not. She had built everything on knowing it, and still she had run as though he were a step behind her the whole way.

Her hands were shaking so badly she had to try three times to get her fingers under the edge of the mask.

It came away with a soft sucking sound. The night air hit her face, and she tipped her head back against the wall and breathed it in, cold and filthy and sweet, as if she had been drowning. Her skin was slick under the hood. The diamond markings on her cheeks itched where the mask's padding had pressed against them for hours.

She looked down at the thing in her hand. Bone-white. Eye-slits. A monster's face, made to be remembered. She had chosen it because it would make people think of Ventress, and it had. It had worked. All of it had worked.

In her other hand were the two lightsabers.

They were heavier than her own, and the curve of the hilts sat strangely against her palm, pulling her wrist into angles her training had never asked of it. She could feel the crystals inside them, even dark. They were not like her own crystal. They did not sing. They throbbed, low and sullen, like a wound that had healed wrong, and every time she had ignited them tonight she had felt the red light go into her arms like poison into a vein.

She should not have used them on Ventress. She should not have needed to. The woman had been sitting in the open, talking, and Barris had crouched in the cables above her and listened to her talk about the warehouse and about who had known where to wait, and had understood exactly where that sentence ended. With a name. With a holo-call to a friend in the Temple, made by a frightened girl in the dark who had trusted the one person she shouldn't.

So she had dropped. And Ventress had gone over the railing. And Barris had not looked down to see where she landed, because if she had looked, she would have had to know.

She closed her eyes.

It was necessary. She had been telling herself that for weeks, in a voice that sounded more and more like someone else's. The Jedi had become soldiers. The Jedi had become generals. The Jedi sent clones to die by the thousand and called it peace, and nobody would see it, nobody would ever see it, unless someone made them. Unless someone held up a mirror so terrible they could not look away.

She thought of the platform. Of the pylon coming apart under the nano-droids, and the hab stack leaning, and the lights on in the windows.

She had counted on him. That was the part she could not stop turning over. She had stood on that catwalk and watched Anakin Skywalker raise his hands, and she had known, known down to her bones, that he would choose the people over her. Because whatever the Order had made of the rest of them, there was still that much Jedi left in him. She had wagered twenty storeys of sleeping strangers on the goodness of a man she was trying to destroy the student of.

And she had won.

Barris bent over, abruptly, and was sick against the alley wall.

When it was over she wiped her mouth on her sleeve and straightened, and found she was still holding the sabers. She had not let go of them even then.

At the end of the alley, set into the wall beneath a flickering hazard light, was a refuse chute. She knew where it went. All the chutes in this district fed the incinerators on the bottom level, great furnaces that burned day and night and turned everything the city threw away into ash and heat. She walked to it. She pulled the hatch open. A hot breath rose out of the dark, stinking of scorched plastoid.

She held Ventress's lightsabers over the opening.

All she had to do was open her hand. Two curved hilts, gone. The only thing in the galaxy that tied the masked figure in the warehouse to the masked figure on the Dovrin platform to a Padawan of Luminara Unduli who had been in the Temple clinic, as far as anyone knew, all night. Gone. Ash by morning.

Her fingers did not open.

She stood there with her arm outstretched until it began to ache. She told her hand to let go. She thought of Tarkin, of the tribunal, of the evidence lockers and the investigators and the questions. She thought about how simple it would be.

She thought about Ah-soka, alone in her cell, sitting with her back straight because she would not give anyone the satisfaction of seeing it bent.

Her fingers did not open.

Slowly, Barris drew her arm back. She let the chute's hatch swing shut. She wrapped the sabers in a fold of her cloak, and then wrapped them again, and pushed them down to the bottom of the healer's satchel on her hip, under the bacta patches and the wound-sealer and the small neat rolls of bandage. The mask went in after them.

Tomorrow, she told herself. She would get rid of them tomorrow, when her hands were steady.

She pulled up her hood and walked out of the alley, toward the lift banks that would carry her up and up, through six hundred levels of light, to the Temple. There were memorial candles in the Hall of Remembrance for the dead of the hangar bombing. She had lit one every morning since it happened. She would light one this morning too.

And then, when the sun came up, she would go and stand quietly among the healers while the Council decided what to do with her best friend.

End of chapter.

Part Three: The Hunt.

Chapter 17. Fugitive.

The Council had put her prison number on the holoprojector.

NK9-2214, in square Judiciary lettering, floated above the center of the chamber beside an intake image from Narkis: Ah-soka in grey thermals against a grey wall, chin up, the collar's status light a hard blue dot at the base of her throat. Somebody had picked that image. Somebody had decided this was what the Jedi Council should be looking at while it decided what to do with her.

Anakin stood in the circle where petitioners stood, and he looked at it, and he did not let himself look at anything else.

His second request for a visitation permit had come back four days before the raid, stamped pending review by the Chancellery and nothing else. He had filed it twice more by hand. Nobody had answered those either.

"Six members of the facility staff are dead," Mace Windu said. The morning came in low and gold through the tall windows behind him and put his face in shadow. "The Commandant was shot. She identified the leader of the raid as Cad Bane. A Separatist officer held for exchange was taken in the same assault. The attackers knew where the power couplings were, where the cells were, and which door to open." He let the list sit a moment. "Bane works for whoever pays him. This time, it is reasonable to believe that was Count Dooku."

"Reasonable," Anakin said.

"Reasonable." Windu didn't blink. "We have to assume she is with Dooku now. Whether she went willingly is a question we can't answer from here. Until we can, she is a fugitive from a Republic sentence and a potential asset to the Separatist command."

"She's a seventeen-year-old in a Force collar."

"She is a trained combatant who knows our fleet movements, our codes as of four months ago, and the habits of every officer in the 501st." Kee Ah-dee Mundi folded his long hands. "If Dooku has her, he has all of that."

"If Dooku has her," Anakin said, "then she's a prisoner who got stolen from one cell and put in a nicer one. That's not the same thing as joining him."

Plo Koon had turned his masked face a little toward the windows. Shahk Tee was very still. Yoda sat low in his chair with both hands on his stick and his ears drawn back, and he had not said a word since Anakin came in, which in Anakin's experience was never a good sign.

Windu went on as if Anakin had not spoken, which was worse than if he'd argued.

"She is to be recaptured," Windu said. "Alive, if it can be done. If she resists with force, she will be met with force."

"Say what you mean."

The chamber went quiet in the particular way it did when someone had broken a rule nobody had written down.

"I'll say exactly what I mean, Skywalker." Windu leaned forward. "If it comes to her life or the life of a Jedi or a trooper in the field, the Knight in command is authorized to end it. The Council will not send people after a fugitive and tell them to die rather than defend themselves."

Anakin heard something tick behind him. One of the glow-panels set into the floor had begun to buzz, its light stuttering. The intake image wavered and pulled sideways in the projector, her face stretching, snapping back.

"You expelled her so a court could kill her," he said. His voice came out level, which surprised him. "The court didn't manage it. The Chancellor didn't let it. So now you'll do it yourselves, and you'll call it a field decision so nobody in this room has to vote on it twice."

"Anakin." Obi-Wan, from his chair to Windu's left. Not loud.

"No. She held up a thousand tons of ice so twenty-three people could walk out of a mine. It's in the Commandant's report, I've read it, so has everyone here. She could have run that day. She put it down. And now some bounty hunter drags her out of her cell in the middle of the night and you're telling me she went with him because she wanted to?"

The glow-panel cracked. A thin line ran across it, and the buzzing stopped, and it went dark.

Nobody looked at it. That was the Jedi way. You noticed everything and acknowledged nothing until it was convenient.

"Heard you, we have," Yoda said at last, quietly. "Wrong, you may be. Right, you may be. Know, none of us do. That is the trouble, hm? Always, the trouble."

"Then let me go and find out."

"That," Windu said, "is exactly what you'll do."

Anakin had a whole second argument loaded and ready, and it took him a moment to realize he wouldn't get to use it.

"You and Master Kenobi," Windu said. "The 501st is released from rotation for the duration. You'll begin at Narkis and follow whatever the Commandant's people can give you." He paused. "You know how she thinks better than anyone in this Order. That's the reason. The other reason is that I would rather you were hunting her where Master Kenobi can see you than somewhere he can't."

"How generous."

"It wasn't meant to be."

At least that was honest. Anakin almost respected it.

"One more thing." Windu's mouth thinned, and for the first time that morning he looked like a man reading out words he hadn't written. "The Chancellor's office has recommended that Master Unduli and her Padawan be attached to the search. Offee treated Tano at Narkis. She filed the medical note on the collar's nerve damage. If the collar has been tampered with, or if Tano is injured, she may need a healer who already knows her condition." He set the datapad down. "The Council has agreed."

He didn't say the Council has been asked to agree, but it was in the way he set the datapad down, square to the edge of the armrest, as if putting it down very carefully would keep it from being somebody else's idea.

Anakin found he didn't mind. Barris had been to Narkis. Barris had seen her, touched her, had come back and written the note that got the collar turned down. He'd heard about that later, from a Judiciary summary, and had sat with it in the dark at 500 Republica for an hour, grateful to a Padawan he barely knew. If Ah-soka was hurt, Barris would be the one she'd trust to put hands on her.

"Fine," he said.

"Fine," Windu agreed, and the holo with her number on it went out.

Obi-Wan caught up with him afterward in the long corridor, where the light came in bars between the columns and the tourists' gallery two levels up was closed for the morning.

"You don't believe she went to Dooku," Anakin said, without turning.

"No."

"Then why didn't you say that in there?"

"I did. At some length. Before you arrived." Obi-Wan fell into step beside him. "It was very persuasive. You saw how well it went."

Anakin almost laughed. It caught somewhere around his ribs, near the stiff place the Ordaine burn had left, and turned into something else.

"They authorized killing her, Master."

"They authorized a Knight in the field to defend his life." Obi-Wan's voice was mild, and very tired. "I know how it sounds. I don't like it any better than you do. But the Knight in command of this search is going to be you, or me, and I don't intend to kill her. Do you?"

"That's not funny."

"No," Obi-Wan said. "It wasn't meant to be either. We seem to be having a morning of that."

Scene break.

The rebuilt hangar still smelled of new sealant under the fuel and the ozone. The memorial alcove on the eastern wall had candles in it again; somebody kept them lit, and Anakin had never once seen who. He'd stopped looking at it months ago. He looked at it now, at the little flames standing straight in the still air, and thought about the six names cut into the stone above them, and the one name that would never be there, that had been put on a prison number instead.

The 501st was loading a pair of gunships for the transfer up to the Resolute. Rex stood at the foot of the near ramp with a datapad, checking off crates, and he looked up when Anakin came in, and Anakin saw him register Anakin's face and decide not to ask.

"General."

"Rex. We're on it."

"Heard, sir. The men heard too." Rex lowered the datapad. "Holo-net's running the Tarkin quote every hour."

"I know."

"Jesse put his fist through a locker door."

"Is he all right?"

"The locker isn't." Rex paused. "Sir. The orders we got say recapture."

"That's what they say."

Rex looked at him a moment longer through the visor, and then he nodded once, and went back to his crates. Something had changed in Rex since the refinery. Anakin hadn't worked out what. The captain was as precise as ever, the reports clean, the men squared away. But there was a stillness in him now, a held-back quality, as if he'd put part of himself in a pocket and buttoned it shut.

Anakin didn't ask. He had his own pockets.

Luminara Unduli came across the deck a few minutes later, her dark robes hardly moving, Barris a step behind her and to the left, the way Padawans walked when they had been taught well. Barris had her hood down. She looked rested, which surprised him. The last time Anakin had seen her up close, at the Temple, months ago, she'd had the hollowed look of someone who'd given up sleeping as a bad habit. Now her face was smooth and her eyes were clear and her hands, folded in her sleeves, were still.

"Skywalker." Luminara inclined her head. "We are glad to help, if we can."

"Thank you, Master." He turned to Barris. "You saw her. At Narkis."

"Yes." Barris's voice was quiet and steady. "Once, in the infirmary. She'd hurt her shin pulling an inmate out of a chute. And there was the shoulder, from before."

"How was she?"

Barris considered it. She was the kind of person who considered things, Anakin remembered; Ah-soka used to say it about her with exasperated fondness, that Barris would think about whether to sit down for so long that the chair would leave.

"Thin," Barris said. "Tired. Stubborn. She asked about the Temple. She asked about you." A small pause. "She was trying very hard not to hate anyone."

Anakin's throat closed. He made it open again.

"The collar," he said. "If it's still on her."

"Headaches. Pain down the neck and into the shoulder. If it's been damaged in the raid it could be worse, seizures, possibly." Barris's gaze stayed on his. "If it's still on her, she will need it off. Carefully. It should not be cut by someone who doesn't know where the leads run."

"Then you'll be there when we find her."

"Yes," Barris said. "I'll be there."

Luminara was looking at her Padawan. Not at Anakin; at Barris, with a kind of stillness Anakin didn't understand and didn't have time to understand, as if Luminara were listening for something under the words and not finding it. Then she turned her attention to the gunships and the moment was gone.

The hangar's main doors opened again.

Admiral Tarkin walked in as if the deck had been laid for him personally. He had eight people with him, and they were not Core-uh-sant Guard and they were not Republic Navy. They wore close-fitted grey armor with no unit markings, and they moved in the loose, unhurried way of people who were used to being the most dangerous thing in a room and didn't need to prove it. The woman at their head was in her forties, lean, black hair cut close to the skull, a pale old scar running down through her left eyebrow and into the lid. She carried nothing in her hands. The trooper behind her carried a small hard case with a Judiciary seal.

"General Skywalker." Tarkin stopped at a precise conversational distance. "Master Unduli. I won't keep you. As a courtesy, I wished to inform the Jedi personally that the Senate Judiciary will be conducting its own recovery operation. Major Teva Rusk, Judiciary Special Recovery. Her team will operate independently of yours."

Rusk nodded once. She looked at Anakin the way Anakin looked at an engine he'd been told was faulty: thoroughly, without opinion, already thinking about where to put the spanner.

"Independently," Anakin said.

"The prisoner was taken from Judiciary custody. Six Judiciary personnel died in the taking. The Senate considers her recovery a Judiciary matter." Tarkin's mouth moved slightly. "The Jedi Council, I understand, considers it a Jedi one. Two searches will be twice as likely to succeed. I'm sure we can all agree on the value of efficiency."

"What's in the case?"

Tarkin glanced back, almost lazily. "A replacement collar, General. The one she wears is controlled by a single device, which the bounty hunter now holds. Should the original be removed or fail, she cannot be left unrestrained. Not with the Senate's eyes on the restraint program." He let the next words come out cleanly, one at a time. "She is, after all, the program's most prominent demonstration."

The case was small. That was the thing Anakin couldn't stop looking at. It was no bigger than a field medkit. You could carry it in one hand.

Beside him, Barris had gone very still. Anakin felt it more than saw it, a tightening in the Force at his elbow, and when he glanced at her she was looking at the case too, her face bloodless. She had treated the damage that thing did. She had felt it under her hands. He thought, with a rush of something like kinship, that she was the only other person in this hangar who knew what was inside that box and hated it as much as he did.

"She's not a demonstration," Anakin said. "She's a person."

"In my experience, General, those are not mutually exclusive." Tarkin turned to go, then paused. "Major Rusk's rules of engagement are set by the Judiciary, not by your Council. I mention it only so there are no misunderstandings in the field. Her people are very good at what they do. They are not burdened with the scruples that so often slow the Jedi down."

"Is that a threat, Admiral?"

"It's a description." Tarkin smiled thinly. "Good hunting."

He left. Rusk lingered a heartbeat longer, long enough to look at Rex, at the captain's sidearm, at the 501st's markings on the gunship, at the candles in the alcove. Then she followed him out, and her people went with her, and the hangar seemed larger afterward and colder.

Rex had come up beside him without Anakin noticing.

"Sir," Rex said, low, "permission to say something."

"Go ahead."

"I'd like to find her before they do."

Anakin looked at the doors where the grey armor had gone.

"Get in line," he said.

Scene break.

The Chancellor's office was too warm. It was always too warm. Anakin had stopped noticing it after the first month and only noticed it now because the day had been so cold, the hangar, the Council chamber, the sealed little case.

Palpatine stood at the window with his back to the room, watching the evening traffic slide past in its lanes, red and white and red. He didn't turn when Anakin came in. He said, "Sit, my boy, please," to the glass, and Anakin sat.

"I tried," Palpatine said.

Anakin waited.

"When I heard what the Council intended, I sent a message to Master Windu. I asked him, in the plainest terms I'm permitted to use with the Jedi, to reconsider the language. To insist on her capture alive and nothing else." He turned from the window at last. He looked older in the evening light than he did in the Senate. The yellow bruise Anakin had seen on the back of his hand weeks ago had faded to nothing; Anakin found himself looking for it anyway. "Master Windu replied, very courteously, that the internal decisions of the Order are not subject to review by the Chancellor's office. He is quite right, of course. The Senate and the Jedi have their separate spheres. That's how it should be." He sat down across the desk, slowly, an old man's sitting. "I simply wish, this once, that it were otherwise."

"You recommended Barris Offee."

"I did. Was that wrong?" Palpatine looked genuinely worried. "She went to Narkis with the healer circuit, and when she came back her report was the only document in that entire file that treated Ah-soka as someone who could be hurt. I thought, " He spread his hands. "I thought if the worst happened, she should have a friend nearby who could do something about it. The Council would never think of that. They think of assets."

"No," Anakin said. "It wasn't wrong. It was right."

"I'm glad." Palpatine let a breath go. "And the Admiral?"

"You knew."

"I learned an hour after you did, I'm afraid. Wilhuff doesn't ask my permission for things he can get from the Judiciary committee." A small, rueful shake of the head. "He believes very deeply in his collar program. More deeply than in most people, I sometimes think. I've asked him to be restrained. I don't know that he will be."

Anakin's hands were on his knees. He found that the right one had closed, and he opened it, and the glove creaked over the metal knuckles.

"They're going to kill her," he said. It was the first time he'd let himself say it out loud. "One way or another. The Council will tell itself it was self-defense and Tarkin won't bother telling himself anything. And everyone will say it was a tragedy, and light a candle, and move on."

Palpatine was quiet a long moment.

"Anakin," he said. "Look at me."

Anakin looked at him.

"I could not stop the Council's order. I could not stop the Admiral. I am the Chancellor of the Republic, and today I discovered, not for the first time, how little that is worth when the people with weapons have made up their minds." His voice was low and even, a voice for telling someone something true. "But there is one person in this search who loves her. One person who knows her well enough to find her before the others do. Who would not, I think, stand back and watch while someone else decided she was too dangerous to bring home."

The traffic lanes went on sliding past the window. Red, white, red.

"Find her first," Palpatine said. "Before Master Windu's Knights, before the Admiral's people. And when you find her, don't take her to the Temple. Don't hand her to the Judiciary. Bring her to me."

"To you."

"I kept her alive once, when no one else would. Let me do it again." Palpatine reached across the desk and laid his hand over Anakin's closed one, the warm ordinary weight of it, an old man's hand on metal. "I can't promise you what happens after. I can promise she'll be safe in this building while we find out. No one will put her in a cell on the edge of nowhere. No one will put her down for resisting arrest. You have my word."

Anakin's eyes stung. He was embarrassed by it, and then too tired to be embarrassed.

He thought of the warehouse. He thought of the Dovrin platform, of the hab stack groaning on its pylon, of a pale mask on a catwalk turned toward him, watching him choose. He'd held up twenty stories of durasteel so nobody would die, and nobody had, and it had cost her everything. He'd been living inside that arithmetic for five months. Every night he did the sum again, and every night it came out the same.

He was not going to stop and save anyone else this time.

The thought arrived whole and he didn't push it away. It was steadying. It was the first solid thing he'd stood on all day.

"I'll find her," he said. "Before any of them. And if anyone tries to stop me, "

"Anakin." Gently. "I don't need to know the rest."

Anakin stopped. He nodded. He stood, and Palpatine stood with him, and walked him to the door, a hand on his shoulder, the way he had since Anakin was a boy and the Chancellor was only a senator who took an interest.

"Do give my regards to Master Kenobi," Palpatine said at the door. "He has a difficult task too. I don't envy him."

"He'll be fine."

"I'm sure he will," Palpatine said, and smiled, and the door slid shut between them.

In the turbolift going down, Anakin leaned his forehead against the cool panel and closed his eyes and let the car fall. Somewhere out there she was wearing a collar that a bounty hunter held the key to, and there were three hunting parties coming after her, and only one of them was going to bring her home.

It would be his. He'd make sure of that. Whatever it took.

He didn't call Padmay. He told himself it was because it was late.

Scene break.

The barracks had gone quiet by the middle watch, the kind of quiet that wasn't sleep. Somewhere down the row Jesse was still awake; Rex could hear him not moving. The 501st shipped up to the Resolute at dawn. Nobody had said much at evening mess. Nobody had needed to.

Rex sat on the edge of his bunk in his blacks with the cracked cadet datapad on his knee, the one with the network chip dug out, the one that held everything he'd written down since Ringo Vinda. Tup's headaches. The Ka-mee-no order logged nine minutes before his own report. Pesk. The refinery. The edge of the shaft and the grating and Fox's arm under his own, too late by half a second. He'd written that part the night it happened and had not been able to read it back since.

He was adding a line about Major Rusk, scar left brow, no unit markings, eight personnel, case w/ Judiciary seal, when his comlink chimed.

Not the command channel. Not the 501st net. The tone was the flat double note of a city-band relay, and the routing header on the little display said it had come in through the Core-uh-sant Guard's maintenance traffic: an automated equipment-fault ping from a Guard-issue comlink, reporting a low power cell. Nineteen digits of serial and status code. The kind of thing that came in by the thousand every night and got filtered out by the thousand. Rex's personal unit shouldn't have received it at all.

He nearly deleted it.

His thumb was on the key when he looked at the status code again.

Nineteen digits. Fault class, cell level, a unit serial, a timestamp. Except the timestamp was wrong, it was twelve hours and some minutes off local, and the cell level read as a percentage no power cell ever reported, and the fault class was one that hadn't been used since the Guard switched suppliers two years ago. Garbage. Somebody's corrupted unit spitting out nonsense into the city net.

Or somebody who knew that nonsense was the only thing nobody would bother to read.

Rex set the comlink down on the bunk very carefully, as if it might go off.

He knew the shape of that string. He'd built half of it himself, on Ka-mee-no, in the long wet nights before their first deployment, two cadets bored out of their skulls inventing a code nobody else would want: the check-in cipher. Twelve-hour offsets for the timestamp. Cell level as a running count. The dead fault class as the signature, because, Fives had said, who checks the dead ones? They'd used it on Ringo Vinda. They'd used it for the medical burst that had come in through the Resolute's medbay, the one Rex had watched eleven times.

He pulled the datapad closer. His hands were steady. He was very aware of his hands being steady, the way you were aware of your footing on a bad slope.

He worked the cipher out by rote, digit against digit. It didn't take long. It had never been a very good code. It had only ever needed to be good enough for two people.

Unit serial: a Guard comlink, registered to a trooper of Fox's. A comlink that had gone missing off a belt in the underlevels on the night an ARC trooper had run from the Chancellor's office past 79's and into an abandoned refinery.

Cell level: a count. Not of hours. Of check-ins. The number of twelve-hour check-ins a man would have missed, if he'd been ordered to check in every twelve hours, and had fallen down a drainage shaft, and had spent some long time after that unable to reach a comlink, or to lift one.

Rex counted it twice. It was right. It was exactly right.

And the signature, the dead fault class, carried the one tag they'd agreed on the day they made the code, the one Fives had insisted on and Rex had called childish and agreed to anyway: the ARC's own number, in the cipher, with the last digit flipped to show the sender was hurt.

Five-five-five-four.

Down the row, Jesse turned over on his bunk. A gunship engine ran up somewhere outside and spooled down again. Rex sat in the dark with a cracked datapad on one knee and a stolen signal on the other, and he did not breathe for what felt like a long time.

Then he picked up the comlink, and opened a reply on the same dead fault class, and his thumb found the old acknowledgment code without his having to think, as if it had been waiting under his skin.

Fives was alive.

End of chapter.

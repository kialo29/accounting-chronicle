Chapter 10. The Chancellor's Friend.

The summons came on Chancellery flimsi, folded once and sealed with a disc of red wax, and for the length of three breaths Barris Offee was certain it was a warrant.

She stood in the doorway of the Temple medical clinic with the flimsi open in both hands while a youngling with a blistered palm waited on the cot behind her. She read it twice before the words would hold still. The Office of the Supreme Chancellor requests the attendance of a Jedi healer at the Senate Office Building, on a staff matter, at the healer's earliest convenience. Below the seal, in the clinic's duty script, someone had scrawled: Offee, you're nearest the landing platform. Go when you can.

Not a warrant. A staff matter.

Her hands did not stop shaking, so she put the flimsi in her sleeve where no one would see them shake, and went back to the youngling.

"Training remote?" she asked.

"I was holding it wrong," the boy said. He was Rodian, perhaps eight, and furious with himself in the way only very young Jedi could be, as if the burn were a moral failing. "Master Drallig says a remote can't hurt you if you're paying attention."

"Master Drallig says a great many things." She took his hand and laid her fingers over the blister and reached, and the Force came to her the way it always had, clean and willing, as if it did not know what she was. The angry skin cooled under her touch. "There. It will be pink for a day. Try not to pay so much attention that you forget to breathe."

He flexed his fingers and grinned at her and ran. She sat on the edge of the empty cot for a moment longer than she needed to.

That morning, before the clinic opened, she had lit four candles in the memorial alcove off the eastern corridor, as she had every morning for nearly two months. Four flames, no names. One for Jackar Bowmani, who had carried death into the hangar without knowing it. Three for troopers whose numbers she had memorized from the evidence list read aloud at the tribunal: CT-2261, CT-4408, CT-9170. Ah-soka had asked her to light candles for them. Ah-soka had thanked her for it.

She had not slept more than three hours together since the hangar. She had grown used to the grey edge it put on everything, the way sounds arrived slightly late, the way her own face in the refresher mirror had begun to look like a portrait of someone she used to know.

A staff matter, she told herself, and stood, and collected her satchel.

Scene break.

The Senate Office Building was a canyon of polished stone turned on its side, and the Chancellor's suite sat near the top of it like a held breath. Two guards in long red robes and faceless helmets flanked the outer doors. They did not move as she approached, and they did not need to; she felt their attention settle on her like a hand on the back of the neck.

The staff matter turned out to be a young human aide named Dace Orrin, who had caught his left wrist in the closing door of a turbolift and was sitting in an antechamber with it cradled against his chest, white around the mouth and apologizing to everyone who passed.

"It's nothing," he told her. "I said it was nothing. They wouldn't hear of a med droid. The Chancellor said, he said if one of his people is hurt, they should have the best." He tried to laugh. "I don't know why he thought that was me."

"Let me see."

It was a sprain, and a small fracture along the outer bone. She could have splinted it and sent him to the district med center with a bacta wrap. Instead she knelt and closed her hands around his wrist and let the Force run through her and into him, coaxing the bone to remember its shape. It took longer than the youngling's burn. When she opened her eyes Dace Orrin was staring at her as if she had pulled a bird out of his sleeve.

"That's, it doesn't hurt."

"It will ache tonight. Don't carry anything heavy until tomorrow." She was already rising, already turning toward the door, already rehearsing the walk back to the platform, when a voice spoke from the inner office.

"Padawan Offee."

It was not loud. It did not need to be. The inner doors stood open, and beyond them the Chancellor's office glowed a deep, rich red, and Palpatine himself stood just inside the threshold with his hands folded into his sleeves.

"Thank you for coming so quickly," he said. "Young Orrin is very dear to this office, though he would never believe it. Would you indulge an old man for a moment? I have tea, and I so rarely have the chance to speak with the Order's healers. You see the war from a side the rest of us are spared."

There was no possible answer but yes. She knew that before he finished speaking. She also knew, in some cold far room at the back of her mind, that the staff matter had never been the matter at all.

"Of course, Chancellor."

The doors closed behind her with a soft, expensive sound.

The office was warm. Not comfortably warm, warm the way the inside of a closed hand is warm. The carpet was the color of old wine, and the tall windows looked out on a Core-uh-sant that seemed very far away, the traffic lanes moving past in silence. He poured tea from a squat black pot into two cups and set one in front of a chair across from his desk, and he waited until she sat.

"You work at the Temple clinic," he said. "And, I'm told, you've spent a good deal of time in the field hospitals. Geonosis. The Ryloth campaign. Master Unduli speaks very highly of your gifts."

"Master Unduli is kind."

"Master Unduli is accurate. I've found that's rarer." He lifted his own cup and did not drink from it. "Tell me, I ask everyone in your line of work, and the answers are always so different, what is the hardest part of it? The healing?"

She thought of lying. She thought of the polite answer, the one about triage and fatigue. Instead, because she was so tired, she heard herself say, "Knowing what they'll be sent back to."

He nodded slowly, as if she had confirmed something he had long suspected and grieved over.

"Yes," he said. "I imagine that would be the hardest thing in the galaxy. To mend a man so that he can be broken again. To mend a Jedi so that she can go back to being a general." He set the cup down. "I have asked myself many times, these last years, what this war has made of the Jedi. Whether the Order that went to Geonosis is the same Order that will come home. I confess I don't like the answers I arrive at. I suspect you don't either."

Her pulse was loud in her ears. She kept her hands flat on her knees.

"I'm only a Padawan, Chancellor. I don't, "

"No," he said gently. "You're not only anything. I've watched you, you see. Forgive me. It's a habit of the office." He reached to the corner of his desk and touched a control, and a holo bloomed in the air between them, grey and blue and soundless.

A corridor. Narrow, institutional, lined with conduit. A timestamp in the corner. She knew the corridor before she understood she knew it, the way you recognize a room in a nightmare: the service passage on the upper level of the Republic Center for Military Operations, the one that ran behind the observation glass above the holding rooms. She had walked it once. Only once.

A hooded figure stood at the end of the corridor with a healer's satchel on one shoulder. The hood was deep. The face beneath it was turned toward a narrow maintenance viewport. The figure's right hand rose, slowly, the fingers drawing together as if closing around something small.

Beside the corridor, a second frame opened. A holding room seen from a high corner. A woman in a chair, a young Toe-groota standing across from her. Letta Turmond's mouth was open. She had been about to speak. The timestamp in the second frame matched the first to the second.

Letta's hands went to her throat.

Palpatine touched the control again, and both images froze before she fell.

"An auxiliary camera," he said into the silence. "Installed by a maintenance contractor some years ago to monitor the coolant lines in that passage. It logged to a separate system that no one had looked at since. Admiral Tarkin's investigators never found it. They were asking a very particular question, and they found the answer they were looking for." He folded his hands. "I asked a different question. I'm afraid I'm a curious old man."

Barris did not move. She could not have said whether she was breathing. Some trained part of her was reaching for the calm the way a falling person reaches for a ledge, and finding it, and finding that it held nothing. She thought, very clearly, of the hilt at her belt, and then of the two red-robed guards beyond the door, and then of nothing at all.

"I sealed these recordings under the authority of this office," Palpatine said, "the same afternoon I found them. They exist in one place. No one has seen them but me. No one will."

"Why," she said. It did not come out as a question. Her voice sounded as if it belonged to someone in the next room.

"Because I knew, the moment I saw them, that it would be a catastrophe." He leaned forward a little, his face grave and soft in the red light. "Think of it, my dear. A Jedi Padawan, a healer, responsible for the bombing of the Jedi Temple. For the murder of a prisoner in Republic custody. For, " he paused, and let the pause say what it wanted to ", whatever else that night held. I'm told there are later recordings, from the detention level. I have not watched them. I find I don't need to."

She closed her eyes.

"Count Dooku's propagandists would have it on every channel in the Outer Rim by morning," he went on. "The Senate would tear the Order apart, and half the Senate would cheer while it did. The war would be lost in a week, not on any battlefield, but in the hearts of the people who pay for it. I could not allow that. Whatever else I am, I am the steward of this Republic."

"And Ah-soka," Barris whispered.

"Ah-soka Tano is alive." He said it with great tenderness. "Admiral Tarkin wanted her dead. The tribunal gave him what he wanted. I took it away from him, at no small cost to myself, and I would do it again. That was the most I could give her, and I gave it." He reached across and touched the control once more, and a third image appeared beside the first two.

Her own face.

Not hooded. The tribunal gallery, the day of sentencing, the moment just after he had pronounced the word death. She had not known anyone was looking at her. Her mouth was half open. Her eyes were wide and wet and wrecked. It was the face of someone watching a building fall on a person she loved, and knowing she had set the charge.

"I kept this as well," Palpatine said. "Forgive me. Of everyone in that chamber, you were the only one whose face looked the way I felt. I wanted to remember that someone else understood what was being done."

She could not look away from it. She could not look at him.

"You could free her," she said at last. "You have the recordings. You could, "

"And put you in her place?" He sounded genuinely pained. "And put the Order in the dock beside you? No. I don't believe you want that, not truly. I don't believe she would want that, if she knew. She thinks of you as her friend, doesn't she? She protected you at her own trial. She would not name you even when it might have saved her." He let that settle. "That is a remarkable loyalty. I would not dishonor it."

Barris's hands had curled into fists on her knees. She uncurled them one finger at a time.

"What do you want from me?"

"Want?" He looked almost hurt. Then he smiled, and it was a kind smile, an old man's smile, the smile of a grandfather at the end of a long table. "My dear girl. I don't want anything from you that you haven't already been trying to give. You did what you did because you believe the Jedi have lost their way. Because they answer to no one, and they have walked into this war without once asking whether they should. Isn't that so?"

She said nothing. He did not need her to.

"I am the elected head of the Republic the Jedi serve," he said. "And do you know how I learn what the Council fears? I read it on the Holo-net, like any clerk in the lower levels. They come to this office and tell me what they have decided, never what they are afraid of. They treat me as one treats a storm. Something to be managed, and waited out." He spread his hands. "I'm not asking you to betray anyone. I would never ask that. I'm asking for friendship. Now and then, when something troubles them, when they are afraid, tell me. So that I can help. So that someone outside that tower knows what is happening inside it."

The holo frames hung in the air between them. The corridor. The holding room. Her face.

She thought: The Senate should oversee the Jedi. She had said it to Ah-soka once, in a different form, on a long night at the clinic, and Ah-soka had laughed and called her a politician. She had believed it then. She believed it now. An Order that answered to no one had let a seventeen-year-old girl be taken in binders out of its own front doors, and had not even flinched. If someone had been watching them, if anyone at all had been allowed to ask them a question they could not refuse to answer, It was not betrayal. It was the very thing she had wanted. It was accountability, at last, wearing a kind face.

"Yes," she said.

"Thank you." He touched the control, and the three images folded away into nothing, and he sat back. "Thank you, Barris. I don't think you know how much it means to an old man to have a friend in the Temple."

A friend in the Temple. Ah-soka had called her that, once, to Ventress, in the lower levels. Barris had not been there to hear it. She had only learned it from the tribunal transcript, and had read the line eleven times.

She rose. Her legs held. That surprised her.

"These recordings are safest with me, I think," Palpatine said, rising too, courteous to the last. "Archives are such fragile things. A change of administration, a curious clerk with too little to do. One never knows." He walked her to the door himself. "And do give Master Unduli my warmest regards."

The doors parted. The antechamber was bright and ordinary. Dace Orrin looked up from his desk and lifted his mended wrist to her in a small, grateful wave.

She did not remember the walk to the platform. She remembered only that, in the moment before the doors had closed between her and that red room, she had realized she was cold, cold to the bone, in a room that had been too warm a moment before, and that she could not tell whether the chill had come from the room, or from her.

Scene break.

She slept that night.

She did not mean to. She sat down on the edge of her bunk in her robes intending to meditate, the way she always did, the way that never worked, and the next thing she knew grey light was coming through the narrow window and the Temple bells were ringing the second hour of the morning. Nine hours. She lay still for a long time and tried to remember the last time she had slept nine hours and could not.

She had missed the dawn candles.

She went to the alcove anyway. Someone had lit the morning's flames in a long uneven row, the way they did every day, for the hangar dead and the war dead and the people no one else remembered. Her four places at the end were empty. She lit them late, with hands that were, she noticed this as if from a distance, perfectly steady. The flames rose straight in the still air. She waited to feel something. When nothing came, she told herself that was the Force, and went to find her Master.

Luminara was in one of the small courtyard gardens on the western side of the Temple, where the water ran over flat black stones and someone had planted moss from Mirial decades ago and coaxed it to grow. She was kneeling in meditation when Barris came in, and she did not open her eyes, but she said, "You're late."

"I'm sorry, Master. I overslept."

Now Luminara opened her eyes.

"You overslept," she said, and there was something in her voice that was not quite pleasure. She studied Barris's face for a long moment. "You look rested. You haven't looked rested since before the hangar."

"I suppose I needed it."

"I suppose you did." Luminara gestured to the stones beside her, and Barris knelt. For a while there was only the water. Then Luminara said, without turning her head, "Where were you yesterday afternoon? The clinic said you were called away."

"The Chancellor's office. One of his aides was hurt. A sprained wrist." True. Every word true. "The Chancellor asked me to stay for tea. He was curious about the field hospitals."

"Was he." Luminara's gaze stayed on the water. "He is a curious man. He has been very curious, lately, about all of us." She was quiet a moment, as if deciding something. "I shouldn't tell you this. But you will be a Knight soon enough, and you may as well know the shape of things. The Council is troubled."

Barris kept her hands loose in her lap.

"By the war?"

"By Skywalker." Luminara sighed. "Since the tribunal, he goes to the Chancellor more than he comes to us. Master Windu wrote to the Chancellor some weeks ago, asking him to stop granting Skywalker's petitions regarding his former Padawan. There has been no answer. Not a refusal, simply nothing. And now there are whispers that Skywalker holds a permit to visit the prison on Narkis, signed by the Chancellor's own hand, over the warden's objections." Her mouth tightened. "Some on the Council think the Chancellor is cultivating him. Some think it's worse than that, that a Jedi Knight who owes his loyalty to the Chancellor's office before the Order is a Jedi Knight the Order no longer commands. Master Windu fears the emergency powers. Master Yoda fears something he will not name. And all of them fear what Skywalker might do if anyone tells him no again."

Barris listened. She listened the way she had been taught to listen to a patient's breathing, not to the words, but to the pattern under them. She heard every piece of it. She filed every piece of it away in some quiet, orderly drawer she had not known she owned until yesterday.

Tell me what they fear.

"That sounds very hard," she said.

"It is." Luminara turned, finally, and looked at her. "Barris. You were close to Ah-soka. Closer than anyone in the Order but Skywalker himself. Since the trial you have been, I don't know the word. Burning down. I've watched it and not known how to reach you. And this morning you come to me rested and steady and calm, and I should be glad, and I find that I'm not. Your stillness feels different." She frowned, choosing the words with care. "Not like water that has settled. Like a door that has closed."

The water ran over the stones.

"I think I've only accepted it, Master," Barris said. "What happened to her. That I can't change it."

"Is that all?" Luminara's voice was very soft. "Is there anything you want to tell me?"

There it was: the question her Master had been holding for two months. Barris had felt it every day, an unasked weight between them, and every day she had braced against it and nearly broken, and once, in the corridor outside the Council chamber, she had come within a single word of telling the truth.

"No, Master," she said.

It came out easily. That was the thing she would remember afterward. Not that she lied, she had lied to her Master for two months, but that, for the first time, the lie had cost her nothing at all.

Luminara held her gaze a moment longer. Then she nodded and closed her eyes and turned back to the water. But Barris saw, before she did, the small line that appeared between her brows, and did not go away.

Scene break.

The healers' duty board hung in the corridor outside the clinic stores, a long panel of soft blue light that listed the requests the Temple received from the rest of the galaxy: field hospitals, refugee ships, a plague ward on some Mid Rim world, a Senator's child with a fever no doctor could break. Most entries had names beside them within the hour. A few did not.

Barris found the ones that did not near the bottom, in a separate block marked JUDICIARY.

Republic Penal Authority requests Jedi healer attendance, circuit rotation, at facilities housing Force-sensitive detainees. Complications reported with new restraint devices: nerve inflammation at the cervical contact points, persistent headaches, sleep disturbance, two cases of seizure. Healer to assess and treat. Circuit: Core-uh-sant Central Detention, Kessan Point Facility, Gallia Orbital Holding, Republic Penal Facility Narkis-Nine. Est. duration six weeks. Departure in twenty days.

No one had signed it. It had been on the board, according to the date in the corner, for nineteen days.

She stood in front of it for a long time.

Collars, she thought. She had heard about them, the way the whole Temple had heard: a Judiciary device, new, experimental, fitted to prisoners who could touch the Force. Fitted, the rumor said, to Ah-soka in flight on the way to the ice. She had imagined it more nights than she could count, the silence it must make, the deafness. She had never let herself imagine what it did to the body. Nerve inflammation at the cervical contact points. Her healer's mind supplied the rest without asking: the burning along the neck, the dull pressure at the base of the skull, the headaches that never quite stopped.

She did not think it through. She would tell herself later that she had, that she had weighed it carefully. But the truth was that she lifted her hand to the panel and pressed her thumb against the signature field and watched her name appear beside the Narkis line, and it felt less like a decision than like falling.

"You're joking."

Healer-Master Ondra Vey had come up behind her with a crate of bacta canisters balanced on one hip. She was a short, broad human woman in her seventies, grey hair cropped close, a field medic's forearms. She had trained half the healers in the Temple and liked almost none of them.

"No, Master Vey."

"That's six weeks through the cold back end of nowhere, treating convicts for a device the Judiciary invented without asking anyone who knows what a nerve is." Vey set the crate down. "Nobody's signed it in three weeks for a reason. You're needed here."

"I'm needed there more," Barris said. "No one else will go."

Vey looked at her the way she looked at a wound that wasn't healing the way it should.

"Is this about the Tano girl?"

The corridor seemed to narrow.

"She's at Narkis," Vey said. "Everyone knows she's at Narkis. You two were thick as thieves. If you're going out there to hold her hand, I won't stop you, but I want to hear you say it."

"I'm going because people are hurt," Barris said, "and because the Order put them there, or let them be put there, and no one in this building wants to look at it." Her voice was steadier than she had expected. Some of it was even true. "If she's one of them, I'll treat her like any other patient."

Vey grunted. "Well. You'll need clearance. New protocol since the trial, any Jedi going into a Judiciary facility needs a countersign from the Chancellery. Some nonsense about chain of custody." She picked up her crate again. "Don't hold your breath. Last time it took a month and a half and three angry letters."

She went off down the corridor. Barris stood in front of the board with her name glowing on it and tried to name the thing she was doing.

Penance was the first word that came. She rejected it. Penance was for people who meant to confess, and she had decided, yesterday, in a red room, in a single syllable, that she would not. Courage was the second word. She rejected that too. There was nothing brave about walking into a prison wearing the robes of the Order that had sent your friend there, knowing what you knew.

What, then? To see her. To know she was alive with her own eyes and not through a holo frame. To put her hands on whatever the collar had done to Ah-soka's neck and make it stop hurting, because that, at least, she could do. Because in two months it was the only thing she had been able to think of that would not make anything worse.

Or perhaps that was another lie, and she simply could not stay away from the place where the worst thing she had ever done was still happening, every day, in the cold.

She went back into the clinic. She treated a Padawan with a dislocated finger, a Knight just back from the Mid Rim with a fever and a cough, a maintenance worker who had cut his hand on a durasteel edge in the rebuilt hangar. She worked through the afternoon with steady hands, and did not think about the board, or the red room, or the drawer in her mind where she had put away everything Luminara said.

The datapad in her satchel chimed a little after the sixth hour.

She almost did not look. When she did, it was a clearance notice, stamped with the seal of the Office of the Supreme Chancellor. Her circuit to the Judiciary facilities had been approved. Departure confirmed in twenty days. Transport arranged on a Chancellery courier, at the Chancellery's expense.

She had signed the board six hours ago. She had told no one but Ondra Vey.

Beneath the formal text, in a smaller, plainer script, someone had added a single line:

She will be so glad of a friendly face., P.

Barris sat very still on the edge of the empty cot. Down the corridor the memorial candles were burning low, four of them at the end of the row, steady in air that did not move. The clinic was warm. It was always warm.

She could not stop shaking.

End of chapter.

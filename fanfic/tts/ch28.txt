Chapter 28. Mirror.

The last time Ah-soka had walked this corridor, she had been on the other side of the energy screens, and the girl waiting at the end of it had come with a healer's kit, warm hands, and a lie. Now Ah-soka was the visitor. Commander Fox walked half a step ahead of her, exactly the way he had walked half a step ahead of her when she wore binders, and he had not said a word since the lift.

Her wrists were bare. She kept noticing them.

Detention level, Military Operations Center. The same pale-grey panels, the same faint ozone bite from the screens, the same hum that settled into the back of the teeth after a while and never left. The overheads were dimmed for the night cycle. Somewhere farther down a prisoner was snoring, a long ugly sawing sound, and Ah-soka found she was grateful for it. It was the only thing on the level that sounded like a person.

Fox stopped at the eighth cell on the left. Hers had been the eleventh. She had counted the doors once, on the way to her own tribunal, because counting was something to do with her mind.

"Ten minutes," Fox said, and tapped his wrist unit. "I'm logging the start. I'll be at the junction." He paused. "Ten. Not eleven."

"I heard you the first time, Commander."

His helmet gave her back her own face, small and orange and very tired. Then he walked away, boots even on the deck, and she turned to the screen.

Barris was sitting on the bench with her back against the wall and her hands open on her knees. Someone had taken her hood and her robes and given her detention grey, and without the hood she looked younger than she had any right to look. Her dark hair was pulled back. The diamond markings on her face were the same as always.

Her throat was not.

The bruise went all the way around, a wide band of purple shading to green at the edges, darker under the jaw where the pressure had been worst. Ah-soka had watched it being made. She had stood in the rain and the dust and watched Anakin lift Barris off the floor with nothing but his hand closed in the air, and for a moment, looking at the bruise, she was back there, with her heart going like a blaster on full auto.

Barris stood. It took her a while.

"You came," she said.

It was barely a voice. It came out scraped and breathy, like something pulled over gravel, and she had to stop after the two words and swallow, and the swallow visibly hurt.

"You asked," Ah-soka said.

They looked at each other through the faint blue shimmer. There was a bench on Ah-soka's side, too. She remembered Barris sitting on it once, kneeling beside her really, cradling her chafed wrists and closing them over with the Force. She remembered how warm it had felt. She remembered saying thank you.

"I could do something about your throat," Ah-soka said. "Not much. I'm not you. But I eased a blaster wound not long ago, and it didn't kill anybody."

"No." Barris touched the bruise, very lightly, the way you touch a sleeping animal. "Leave it. It's the only honest thing anyone's put on me in months."

"That isn't funny."

"It wasn't meant to be."

Ah-soka sat down on the visitor's bench. After a moment Barris sat too, on the edge of hers this time, leaning forward, and the screen between them was the width of a held breath.

"Six Jedi," Ah-soka said. She had not planned to start here. It came out anyway. "Tutso Mara. The workers in the hangar. The clones in the hangar. Jackar. Letta." Her voice wanted to climb and she would not let it. "Twenty-two sixty-one. Forty-four oh-eight. Ninety-one seventy."

Barris's lips moved with the last three numbers. She did not need to hear them. She had been saying them every morning for half a year.

"I asked you to light candles for them," Ah-soka said. "Do you remember? Right here. You were holding my wrists."

"I lit them."

"I know you did. That's the part I can't get past." Ah-soka pressed the heels of her hands into her knees. "You lit candles for the men you killed with my sabers, and then you came to the Deep and fixed my shin, and you went home. I wore that collar for four months, Barris. I couldn't feel anything. Not the Force, not Anakin, not you. I thought it had left me. I thought maybe I'd never had it the way I believed I did. And you sat across from me and let me thank you."

"Yes," Barris whispered.

"Don't just say yes."

"What would you like me to say?" Barris's eyes were dry and very steady. "That I had reasons? I gave the reasons yesterday, in front of the whole Senate, and I still believe half of them. This war is turning us into something. The Order is a weapon that's forgotten it was ever anything else. I was right about that." She stopped to breathe. "I was wrong about everything I did about it. Both of those are true. I've stopped expecting one to cancel the other."

Ah-soka looked away, down the corridor, to where Fox's silhouette stood at the junction under the one bright light. Her mon-trahls ached the way they did before weather. She had imagined this conversation for days and in none of the versions had Barris been calm.

"You said there was one more thing," Ah-soka said. "That you couldn't say in there."

"Yes."

"I've got about six minutes."

Barris folded her hands. When she spoke she spoke slowly, picking each word up and setting it down like something that might go off.

"He knew," she said. "The Chancellor. From the beginning."

The hum of the screen seemed to get louder.

"There was a camera in the upper service passage," Barris went on. "An old maintenance unit. Auxiliary. Nobody had looked at its feed in years. He had. He invited me to his office to heal an aide's wrist, and afterward he gave me tea, and he played it for me. Me, at the end of the passage. My hand coming up." She lifted her hand an inch off her knee, and let it drop. "The exact second Letta stopped breathing."

"When," Ah-soka said. Her own voice sounded far off.

"After your sentencing. But he told me he'd had it sealed since the beginning. Those were his words. Since the very beginning, my dear. He'd watched it before your trial ever opened." Barris swallowed, winced. "He sat on that dais and let Tarkin play the other footage. The silent one, with you in the room. And mine was in his drawer the whole time."

"No."

"He had a still of my face from the gallery, too. The moment he said death. He said I looked like someone who needed a friend." Something moved at the corner of Barris's mouth that was not a smile. "So I became his friend. He wanted to know what the Council was afraid of. Mostly, it turns out, they're afraid of Anakin. Master Luminara told me things in confidence, and I carried them up to that red room and drank his tea and told him."

Ah-soka's hands had gone cold. She pushed them flat against the bench.

"He protected me," Barris said. "That's how he put it. He was protecting the Republic from another scandal, and protecting you, because if the truth came out the Senate would want blood and you might get caught in the spray. He's very good. You feel cared for. You feel chosen." She drew a breath that rasped all the way down. "And when it looked like you'd be cleared, he called me on the Resolute and talked about second trials, and steady hands, and how angry Anakin had become. He never said kill her. He didn't need to. He sent me to the warehouse to make sure you never testified."

She was quiet a moment. "The Rodian who sold me the nano-droids saw my hands. I never went back to him. I lay awake for weeks deciding what to do about him, and then I heard he'd been found in his stall with his hands folded and everything wiped clean, and I thought it was luck."

Ah-soka thought of Ventress turning one white hand over on a table in bad light, describing exactly that. Someone is killing everyone who knew anything, she had said herself, that night. She had never once asked who.

"You said he," Ah-soka said slowly. "At the warehouse. You said he'd sent you, and then you shut your mouth."

"Yes."

"And yesterday, in front of everyone, with Padmay right there, with the Chancellor right there, you said nothing."

"Who would they have believed?" Barris asked. "The Temple bomber, or the man who'd just pardoned you? It would have been my last lie, as far as anyone in that chamber was concerned. A traitor trying to drag down the Republic on her way out." She looked down at her open hands. "And I was afraid. Even then. Even now. He watched me the whole time I spoke. I didn't look back. I couldn't."

Ah-soka stood up. She didn't know why. She walked two steps toward the junction and two steps back and stopped in front of the screen.

It made a terrible kind of sense. The he in the warehouse. The Chancellery courier that had carried Barris to Narkis, white and red against the ice. The way the mercy had come down from the dais exactly when it would land hardest, death, and then the pause, and then life, and how Anakin had looked at that man afterward, as though he'd hung every star.

It also made a terrible kind of sense the other way. Barris had lied to her face for months, warmly, with steady eyes. Barris had stood up yesterday and condemned the Order, the war, and the Senate in one breath. What better parting shot, for someone who believed the whole Republic was rotten, than to put one last seed of poison into the only person who'd shown her mercy? Point it at the top. Let it grow.

Ah-soka reached for the Force and laid it against Barris the way she would test the ice before stepping on it.

Exhaustion. Grief. Fear like a held note. Something underneath that might have been truth, or might have been someone who had learned to lie so well she no longer felt the seam.

She couldn't tell. Months ago she would have known. Months ago she had trusted what she felt, and what she'd felt was a friend.

"Why tell me?" she said. "If nobody would believe you."

"Because you'll be out of here by morning." Barris raised her eyes. "And because he asked Anakin to stay here tonight. Didn't he? To collect you personally."

Ah-soka said nothing.

"Of course he did," Barris whispered. "That's what he does. He puts people exactly where he wants them, and lets them be grateful for it."

"Don't," Ah-soka said. "Don't talk about Anakin."

"Then I'll say one thing and stop. Don't tell him. Not yet. He won't believe you, and if he does, " she touched her throat again ", you've seen what he does with things he can't bear."

Down at the junction, Fox shifted his weight. Ah-soka heard it more than saw it.

"I'm not forgiving you," she said. "I want you to know that. I don't know if I'm ever going to."

"I know."

"I don't know if I believe you."

"You shouldn't," Barris said. "I wouldn't."

"Time," Fox called. His voice carried flat down the corridor.

Ah-soka didn't move. Barris was looking at her the way she had looked across the warehouse with a red blade at Ah-soka's throat and the opening clean, and had not taken it.

"Will you light them?" Barris asked. "The candles. I can't from here. Someone should."

There was a long moment in which Ah-soka could not have said a single word without her voice breaking, so she didn't try. When she could, she said, "Ask me tomorrow."

Something in Barris's face eased, very slightly, like a fist letting go of one finger.

"Tomorrow," she agreed.

Ah-soka walked to the junction without looking back, and Fox logged the stop, and she noticed the time on his wrist unit only because he angled it so she would. Twelve minutes. He'd given her two he hadn't mentioned.

Scene break.

The medical bay was one level up and smelled of bacta and floor sealant. A droid with a long, patient face had her lie back on the table and tipped her chin up with one cold finger, and then it began painting something onto the raw ring around her neck that stung and then went numb.

"The burn under your left jaw has partially reopened," the droid said. "You have been clenching it."

"Sorry."

"It is not my jaw."

She stared up at the ceiling panels while it worked. The light in this room was brighter than in the cells, and it made the inside of her eyelids glow red when she closed them, so she kept them open.

Since the very beginning, my dear.

She tried to remember the Chancellor's face from her own tribunal. She had looked at him exactly twice: once when he took his seat, and once at the end, when he said death. She remembered that he'd seemed sad. She remembered thinking that at least someone up there was sorry. And then the pause, so long, and the word life dropping into it, and the way her whole body had broken around that word like a dam giving way. She had been grateful. She had sat in the transport on the way to Narkis with a collar coming down over her head and still been grateful, a little, in the part of her that didn't want to die.

If Barris was telling the truth, that pause had been chosen. The sadness had been chosen. The mercy had been a leash, and the hand holding it had known all along that she was innocent.

If Barris was lying, then Ah-soka was lying on a table thinking these things about the man who'd just set her free, because a murderer had asked her to.

She reached for the Force again, not toward anything, just to feel it there. It came, the way it had come every time since Juko cut the collar off on Florrum, quick and whole and almost too much. It gave her the droid's small electric attention. It gave her the building, layer on layer of clerks and guards and sleeping prisoners. It gave her, one floor above, the familiar banked heat of Anakin, waiting.

It did not give her an answer.

"Treatment complete," the droid said. "Zero-three-oh-seven. Do not scratch."

She sat up. The numbness had spread down into her collarbone. She thanked the droid, which did not reply, and took the lift to the waiting room on level two.

Anakin was standing at the narrow window with his back to the door and a cup of caf going cold on the ledge beside him. The traffic lanes outside made long slow streaks of light across the glass. He turned when she came in, and his whole face changed, the tiredness falling out of it all at once.

"There you are," he said. "They told me medical would take twenty minutes. That was forty minutes ago. I was about to go down there and start disassembling droids."

"It was mostly paperwork," she said. "And a lecture about my jaw."

"Your jaw's fine. Your jaw's always been fine. It's the things that come out of it." But he was looking at the fresh salve on her neck, and his mechanical hand flexed once inside the dark glove, and he didn't make the next joke. "How was it? Down there."

She had been ready for the question. She still wasn't ready.

"She wanted to say things she couldn't say in front of the Senate," Ah-soka said.

"Like what?"

The words were right there. She says the Chancellor knew. She says he sat on that dais knowing I was innocent. She says he sent her to make sure I never got the chance to prove it. She looked at Anakin's face, worn thin by months of searching and one night of almost killing someone, and lit from the inside by the afternoon in the Senate, by a full pardon, by the Chancellor's hand on his shoulder and his name said warmly in front of everyone. He had spent half a year being grateful to that man. It was holding him together.

You've seen what he does with things he can't bear.

"Mostly that she was sorry," Ah-soka said. It was not a lie. It was not the truth either. It sat in her mouth like a stone.

Anakin's jaw set. "Good," he said, hard. Then, more quietly, "Good. She should be."

"Can we go?"

"Yes. Stars, yes." He picked up the cold caf, looked at it, put it down again. "Obi-Wan got you a guest room at the Temple. Before you say anything, it's one night, it's a bed, nobody's going to ask you to bow to anyone. The Chancellor offered rooms at the Chancellery, but I thought, " He shrugged, a little sheepish. "I thought you'd had enough of being somebody's guest."

"You thought right."

They went down together. At the main doors a Guard trooper scanned Anakin's code and then hers, and the door log chirped twice, and the night air hit her face, wet, metallic, smelling of a hundred thousand speeders, and she stood on the platform outside the Military Operations Center and breathed it in like she'd been underwater.

She was free. It was zero-three-thirty-one, and she was free, and she had never in her life felt less safe.

Scene break.

Obi-Wan came to the guest room door a little after dawn. Ah-soka had slept perhaps two hours, badly. She opened the door before he could knock, because she had felt him coming down the corridor, and she had felt that something was very wrong with him.

He was dressed and composed and his beard was neat. His eyes were not.

"Ah-soka," he said. "I'm sorry to wake you."

"You didn't."

"No. I suppose not." He held himself very still. "Barris Offee was found dead in her cell this morning, at the zero-six-hundred check."

She heard the words. For a moment they did nothing at all. Then her hand found the doorframe on its own.

"How," she said.

"Walk with me," said Obi-Wan. "The Council has asked for you. You were the last person to see her."

The Council chamber at this hour was full of cold early light. Most of the seats were filled, a few by holograms. Yoda sat with his eyes closed and both hands on his stick. Plo Koon looked at the floor, which seemed to be where he looked whenever she entered this room now. Mace Windu did not look at the floor. Anakin was already there, standing at the edge of the circle, still in yesterday's clothes; when she came in his eyes found her and held on.

Commander Fox's image stood in the center of the chamber, blue and flickering, and gave his report in the same flat voice he used for everything.

The prisoner had been found on her bench at the zero-six-hundred check, sitting against the wall. The medical examiner had found her windpipe crushed. No marks of hands, no ligature, no sign of a struggle. The field across her cell door had never dropped; its own power-draw record, which ran on a separate circuit, showed no break all night. The night guard on the junction had seen nothing and heard nothing. "Consistent," Fox said, "with strangulation through the Force."

"The logs," Windu said. "All of them?"

"No, sir." Fox paused, the tiniest hitch. "There's a gap in the detention-level records. Cameras, door sensors, the junction guard's channel. Zero-two-forty-one to zero-two-fifty-two. Eleven minutes. Every system on the level, at once. The techs are calling it a power-regulation fault. They have not found the fault."

Eleven minutes. Ten. Not eleven. Ah-soka's stomach turned over slowly.

"The former Padawan Tano's visit," Kee Ah-dee Mundi said.

"Ended at zero-zero-fifty-eight, sir. Twelve minutes, supervised. She was in medical from zero-two-twenty-five to zero-three-oh-seven. The droid's treatment log covers her." Fox's helmet turned, very slightly. "General Skywalker was logged into the building at nineteen-ten. Logged out at zero-three-thirty-one, with her. Between those times he was in the level-two waiting area."

"Alone," Windu said.

"Alone, sir."

Nobody said anything. Nobody needed to. Ah-soka watched it happen, the way you watch a crack travel through ice: Kee Ah-dee Mundi's eyes going to Anakin and away. Shahk Tee's hologram turning her head. Saesee Tiin, unreadable, still somehow leaning. Plo Koon's mask lifting from the floor at last, to Anakin, and then, briefly, to her. Obi-Wan, beside her, not looking at Anakin at all, and that was somehow the worst of them.

Windu said it anyway.

"She came out of that warehouse with bruises around her throat," he said. "She told Master Kenobi she'd had a fall."

"I know what she said," Anakin said. His voice was quiet and very dangerous.

"Then you understand why this Council has to ask."

"Ask, then."

"Mace," Obi-Wan said.

Windu's eyes didn't move from Anakin. "Where were you between zero-two-forty-one and zero-two-fifty-two, Skywalker?"

"In a chair," Anakin said. "In a room. Waiting for her. Like I was asked to."

"By whom?"

"The Chancellor."

Something passed around the chamber, too faint to name. Yoda opened his eyes.

"Enough, for now," Yoda said. "Grief is in this room, and fear. Neither one sees well." He looked at Ah-soka. "The prisoner. Said anything to you, did she?"

Every face turned to her. Anakin's, too.

He won't believe you. And if he does, "She said she was sorry," Ah-soka said. "She asked me to light candles for the people she killed."

It was true. She hated how easily it came out.

Yoda held her eyes for a long moment, and she had the uncomfortable feeling that he heard the shape of the thing she had left out, if not the thing itself. Then he nodded, slow, and closed his eyes again.

When the Council released her, Anakin had already gone. She went out into the corridor and found it nearly empty, the long windows full of morning, and halfway down, on a stone bench under one of them, Luminara Unduli sat alone with a lightsaber hilt in her lap.

Barris's hilt. Ah-soka had handed it over herself, in the warehouse.

She didn't mean to stop. She stopped.

Luminara looked up. Her face was composed the way the surface of deep water is composed. Only her hands gave her away; they were holding the hilt as if it might be taken from her.

"She asked to see you," Luminara said. "Not me."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. She was right to." Luminara turned the hilt over once. "Did she say anything about me?"

Ah-soka thought of the long whispered confession, of Master Luminara told me things in confidence. None of that was hers to hand over in a corridor.

"She said you knew," Ah-soka said. "Before anyone. That you were waiting for her to tell you."

Luminara closed her eyes.

"I left my door lit for her," she said. "The night she went down there. I told her I would be in my quarters, if that changed." Her thumb moved along the hilt. "I left it lit again last night. I don't know why. Habit, I suppose."

Ah-soka stood there a moment longer. There was nothing to say. She went on down the corridor, and behind her Luminara did not move.

Scene break.

She found Anakin where she'd known she would, in the rebuilt hangar, in the bay where the bomb had gone off. The new floor panels were a slightly different grey from the old ones. You only noticed if you knew. He was sitting on an empty munitions crate with his elbows on his knees and his gloved hands hanging between them, staring at the seam.

She sat down on the crate beside him. It creaked. For a while neither of them said anything, and the hangar went on around them, techs and droids and the high echoing clatter of a gunship being serviced three bays down.

"Did you?" she said.

She'd thought about how to ask it all the way down from the Council chamber. In the end there wasn't a gentler way, and he'd have hated a gentler way.

He didn't flinch. He didn't get angry, which frightened her more than anger would have. He turned his head and looked at her, and his eyes were red-rimmed and very, very tired.

"No," he said.

She waited.

"I sat in that room for eight hours," he said. "And I thought about her. I'm not going to tell you I didn't. I thought about the warehouse. I thought about how it felt, holding her up there." His right hand closed slowly, metal under the leather, and opened again. "It felt easy, Snips. That's the part I keep, it was the easiest thing I've ever done. And I sat in that chair and I thought, she's two floors down. And I didn't go. I drank terrible caf and I watched the traffic and I waited for you." His voice cracked straight down the middle. "I didn't touch her. I swear it. I swear it on you."

She reached out with the Force, the way she'd reached for Barris the night before, and laid it against him.

There was so much there. Grief like a wound left open to the air. Shame, old and new. Fear, of himself, of the Council, of her looking at him the way the Council had. Rage, banked low and always, the way it had been since she'd known him. And underneath all of it, simple and solid as the floor under her boots, nothing hidden. Nothing that slid away from her touch. She had spent three years learning the shape of him. She knew what Anakin felt like when he was lying; she had felt it in a dozen small fibs about mission reports and late returns. This wasn't that.

"I believe you," she said.

He made a sound that wasn't quite a laugh and put his face in his hands.

"They don't," he said into his palms. "Did you see them? Obi-Wan wouldn't even look at me."

"I saw."

"Eleven minutes, and I was in the building, and I'd already, " He stopped. "I'd already done it once. In front of you. Of course they think it was me. I'd think it was me."

She put her hand on his back, between his shoulder blades, and felt him shaking. She left it there. She was thinking, with a kind of cold clarity that seemed to come from somewhere outside her, that he was right. Anyone looking at the records would think it was him. Eleven minutes, on exactly the night he'd been asked to sit alone in that building. Bruises already around her throat that everyone had seen, and a Jedi Master who had suspected him of putting them there. If someone had wanted to kill Barris Offee and leave a single name standing in the wreckage, they could not have built it better.

He puts people exactly where he wants them.

She didn't want to think it. She thought it anyway, and the thought had teeth.

"What did she really say?" Anakin asked. He'd lifted his head. "Last night. You've got that face."

"What face?"

"The one you used to make when you'd broken something on the ship and were hoping I wouldn't find it."

Tell him. She opened her mouth. Barris said the Chancellor knew. Barris said, And she saw it as clearly as if it had already happened: Anakin on his feet, white around the mouth, either refusing her outright or, worse, not refusing, going up the long corridors to that red office with the dark rising off him like heat off a reactor. On the very morning half the Council was wondering if he was a murderer.

"She said she wished she'd been braver," Ah-soka said. "About a lot of things."

He looked at her a beat too long. Then he nodded, and let it go, because he was too tired not to.

His comlink chimed. He fumbled it out of his belt, and a small blue figure rose above his palm: Dace Orrin, the Chancellor's aide, his young face arranged in careful sympathy.

"General Skywalker. The Chancellor asked that you hear this from his office before it goes to the Holo-net." The aide's voice was gentle and practiced. "In light of the circumstances, the Chancellor has personally closed the inquiry into the death of the prisoner Offee, and ordered the detention-level records sealed. He feels very strongly that the Republic must protect a hero of the Republic from baseless suspicion at such a painful time. He sends his warmest regards, and asks you not to trouble yourself further."

The image flickered out.

Anakin sat there through three slow breaths with the dark comlink in his hand. Then he let out a breath that seemed to come all the way up from the floor of him, and Ah-soka felt it go through him in the Force, relief, enormous and helpless, like a man pulled out of deep water.

"He closed it," Anakin said. His voice was unsteady. "Before the Council could even, " He laughed, once, raggedly, and pressed the comlink to his forehead. "Snips. He didn't even ask me whether I did it."

Ah-soka kept her hand on his back. She made it stay still.

He didn't need to, she thought.

Somebody had walked into a sealed detention level in the middle of the night and killed Barris without opening a door, in eleven unrecorded minutes, a few hours after a girl had promised to answer her tomorrow. Somebody had made the cameras forget. And now somebody had made sure that no one, ever, would be allowed to ask who.

Beside her, Anakin was smiling at the place where the bomb had gone off, his eyes wet, grateful with everything he had left.

Ah-soka had stood in front of a firing line of Guard troopers, and a Trandoshan with an ice pick, and Cad Bane. She had never in her life been as afraid as she was now, sitting on a crate in the bright Temple hangar, with her hand on her master's back and a dead friend's last secret in her mouth, unsaid.

End of chapter.

Chapter 27. The Second Tribunal.

The chip was the size of Padmay's thumbnail, and there was a hole in it three minutes and forty-one seconds long.

She had known about the hole since the warehouse, since the moment her own finger found the switch on the side of Fives's helmet and pressed it while Barris Offee's boots left the floor. She had known about it through the gunship ride, through the long wet hour at the Military Operations Center while Fox logged the chip under Obi-Wan's eyes, through a night in which she did not sleep and Anakin, lying an arm's length away, did not either. But knowing a thing and watching it projected six meters high above the floor of the Senate tribunal chamber were different kinds of knowing. The timestamp bar ran along the bottom of the holo in clean white numerals, and at the point where it should have counted smoothly onward it simply jumped, the way a heart skips, and every person in the room could see it.

"The defense notes the interruption," Tarkin said, before she could. He stood at the prosecution table with his hands folded behind his back, as composed as he had been five months ago, when this same room had condemned a seventeen-year-old girl. "Three minutes and forty-one seconds. In a recording the defense describes as tamper-evident."

"Tamper-evident," Padmay said, "not tamper-proof. The seal shows no breach. The court's own technician has confirmed it." She kept her voice level and her right hand, with its bacta patch, flat on the table where it would not shake. "The confession is complete before the interruption. The prisoner's surrender is complete after it. Nothing in those minutes bears on the charges before this tribunal."

"The Admiral will forgive me." On the high dais, Palpatine leaned forward, his face lit gently from below by the holo's glow. "Senator Amidala is quite right. A recorder dropped in a ruined warehouse, in the middle of the night, among lightsabers, I think we may be grateful we have any of it at all. The gap is of no consequence. Proceed."

Tarkin inclined his head and sat.

Padmay did not let herself look at Anakin. He was in the first row behind the defense table, close enough that she could hear him breathe out when the Chancellor spoke, and she knew without turning what his face was doing. She looked instead at the senators' box: Riyo Chuchi, very upright, blue hands clasped; Mon Mothma, unreadable; Bail Organa, who met her eyes for half a second with an expression she couldn't name and then looked down at his datapad. She looked at the Jedi gallery, where Obi-Wan sat with Plo Koon on one side and Luminara Unduli on the other, and where Luminara had not moved since the session opened. She looked at the evidence table, where two sealed lightsaber hilts, numbered 417 and 418, lay in the same plastoid tray as before.

She looked last at Ah-soka.

Ah-soka sat beside her in grey detention clothes, unbound for the session at Padmay's insistence. The collar was gone, but the place where it had been was still a raw, healing ring around her throat, and there was a small, shiny burn under the line of her left jaw. She had folded her hands in her lap. She had not moved them since she sat down.

Padmay had asked, the night before, whether Ah-soka wanted to go to Barris first, Barris had asked for it, shouting it through the rain. Fox had refused on Tarkin's standing order: no contact between the accused and the witness before testimony. Ah-soka had only nodded, as though she had expected nothing else. After, the order said. After was a word Padmay had come to distrust.

"The tribunal calls Barris Offee," said the clerk.

Scene break.

They brought her in through the same side door Ah-soka had used. Two Core-uh-sant Guard troopers walked her to the witness stand, and Fox walked behind them, his helmet turning to watch the gallery as much as the prisoner. Barris wore detention grey, too. Someone had given her a high collar, and it did not hide the bruises. They climbed from her jaw down into the fabric, dark as thumbprints, and when she was asked to state her name, the voice that came out of the amplifier was scraped thin, half breath.

"Barris Offee. Padawan of Master Luminara Unduli." A pause. "I suppose that last part is for the Council to decide now."

In the Jedi gallery, Luminara's hands closed in her lap.

The families of the hangar dead had come. They sat together in the lower gallery in deep blue, as they had every day of the first trial, and Padmay saw a Zabrak woman among them press her fist against her mouth when Barris turned to face the room. They had watched Ah-soka from those seats for two days and hated her. Padmay wondered what they did with that now, where it went.

Tarkin rose. "The prosecution has reviewed the recording entered by the defense and does not contest its authenticity." Each word was laid down separately, like a coin he resented paying. "The witness has asked to make a statement in her own words. The prosecution does not object."

"Then let her speak," said Palpatine.

Barris put both hands on the rail of the stand. Her fingers were steady. Padmay, who had seen them shake in the warehouse, noticed that more than anything else.

"I have already told the Commander of the Guard what I did," Barris said. "I've told it to a recorder in the dark. I will tell it to you, because you are the ones who condemned her, and you should have to hear it.

"I bought the nano-droids. I chose the hangar. I found Letta Turmond, who hated the war and had good reason to, and I gave her a way to make it cost something, and she gave it to her husband without telling him what it was. Jackar Bowmani carried my bomb into the Temple because he loved his wife. Six Jedi died. Clone troopers died, and workers whose names nobody in this room has ever said aloud. I did that.

"When Letta was taken, I stood in the upper service passage of the Military Operations Center and closed her throat through the Force before she could say who I was. She died looking at Ah-soka Tano. I meant her to.

"I took Ah-soka's lightsabers from the evidence locker and killed three troopers of the Core-uh-sant Guard outside her cell." She said their numbers, two-two-six-one, four-four-oh-eight, nine-one-seven-oh, one after another, in the thin ruined voice, and the troopers on either side of her did not move at all. "I left her a keycard and let her think a friend had left it. Then I told her where to go and I waited for her there, wearing a mask, and I made certain she would be found."

She breathed. The amplifier caught the rasp of it.

"And when she was found, I came here. I sat in that gallery." She looked up at it, at the Jedi seats where Luminara sat motionless. "Every day. I watched Admiral Tarkin play the footage of the woman I killed and call it her crime. I watched this tribunal decide. Later I went to Narkis and healed the wounds she took in a prison I had put her in, and she thanked me." For the first time her voice slipped. She caught it. "That is what I am. I don't want anyone in this room to make it smaller.

"But I will not let you make it simpler, either."

Padmay felt the room change. It was not a sound; it was the absence of one, the small shuffles and murmurs of a chamber full of people all ceasing together.

"I was trained as a healer," Barris said. "The first thing a healer learns is to count. How many are breathing. How many can wait. How many we have already lost. I was taught to count the living, and when the war came, the Order gave me the dead instead, and asked me to count those. Christophsis. Geonosis, twice. Umbara. I have stood in field hospitals where the clones were laid in rows, and I have watched Jedi Masters walk those rows with their hands behind their backs and their faces calm, and then go back to the Temple and light a candle and accept the next command.

"We were keepers of the peace. That was the oath. Somewhere, I could not tell you the day, we became officers in someone else's war, and we never once stood in the Council chamber and asked whether we should. We asked only how to win it. We told ourselves the clones were men and then we spent them like ammunition. We told ourselves we served the Republic and we let the Republic decide what serving meant.

"I wanted the Temple to feel what the battlefields feel. I thought if the war came home to them, if they had to sweep their own dead off the hangar floor, they would wake. That was my reasoning, and I give it to you whole, because I still cannot find the place where it fails." She turned her head, slowly, until she was looking at the Jedi gallery. "I can only find the place where I did. It was not the reasoning. It was the bodies. It was Jackar, who didn't know. It was Letta, who did and still deserved a trial. It was three men at a cell door who had done nothing but stand where they were told.

"And it was her." She did not look at the defense table. "Because the Order I wanted to wake did wake, for an afternoon, long enough to expel her. They needed it to be someone. She was nearest. They gave her to you without a hearing of their own, and you", now she did look at the senators' box, and Riyo Chuchi's chin rose a fraction, "you needed it to be someone too. A Jedi bomber was an answer the whole Republic could understand. Nobody here wanted the harder answer. I counted on that. I was right to.

"Don't let anyone tell you I was seduced by the dark side. Nothing seduced me. I walked there, one reasonable step at a time, and every step had a good argument under it. That is what I would like the Order to understand, if they understand anything from this. It was not a shadow that came for me. It was a war, and a Temple that kept saying yes to it.

"I am guilty of every charge laid against Ah-soka Tano. She is guilty of none of them. That's all."

She let go of the rail.

Padmay noticed, then, what Barris had not done. In all that time she had looked at the Jedi gallery and the senators' box and the families in blue and, once, very briefly, at the ceiling. She had not looked once at the dais. Not once. As if it were a light too bright to face.

Palpatine, on the dais, had listened with his chin on his folded hands and an expression of grave sorrow. "Thank you, Padawan Offee," he said. "That cannot have been easy."

Barris said nothing.

"The witness will remain in the custody of the Guard," Palpatine went on. "The matter of her sentence is grave, and this body has learned, I think, the cost of deciding a life in haste. Sentencing is deferred to a future session, at a date to be fixed with the Judiciary and the Jedi Council." He let that sit. "We will not make the same mistake twice."

Barris was led out. At the side door, she stopped, the trooper let her, and for one breath she looked at Ah-soka, and Ah-soka looked back. Neither of them did anything with her face. Then the door closed.

Under the table, Padmay felt Ah-soka's knee press, very slightly, against hers. It was the only sign she gave.

Scene break.

Tarkin did not wait for the recess.

"The prosecution accepts that the original convictions cannot stand on the evidence now before the tribunal," he said, rising, and there was a soft wave of sound through the chamber, relief and outrage mixed. He let it pass. "The prosecution is not finished. There remain the events of the past six weeks. On the night of the Narkis raid, six Judiciary personnel were killed, including Sergeant Aron Holt, who died on a stair in Ring Four while escorting the accused. The accused left Narkis that night aboard a vessel commanded by a known agent of Count Dooku. She did not return to Republic custody. She evaded a lawful recovery operation for the better part of a month, in the company of Asajj Ventress, a Separatist assassin who, I note, is not in this room. Whatever the defendant was five months ago, Chancellor, these are new acts. They are hers."

It was clever. Padmay had expected nothing less. He had lost the war he'd been fighting, so he had simply walked onto another field and planted his flag there, and half the senators in the box were already looking at Ah-soka with the old wariness, because it was easier than looking at what they had just heard.

"Senator Amidala?" said Palpatine.

Padmay rose. "The defense has two statements to enter regarding the Narkis raid. The first is a sworn holo deposition from Commandant Ilsa Vorne, Warden of Narkis-Nine. The Admiral will know her. He recommended her for the post."

Tarkin's mouth thinned by a hair.

The holo bloomed over the floor: Vorne in her uniform, impeccable except that the right sleeve hung empty and the shoulder beneath it was bound flat against her chest. She sat very straight. Her iron-grey hair was pinned exactly as Padmay remembered it from the image on Ah-soka's file. She gave her name, rank, and post in a voice that sounded as though it had never once been raised.

"On the night in question," Vorne said, "power to the Rings failed at twenty-two forty. I was in my office. I observed, from the office monitor and then in person, the Dur-ose bounty hunter known as Cad Bane enter Ring Four with three accomplices. Sergeant Holt was moving prisoner NK9-2214 to the secure room per standing procedure. Bane shot him on the Ring Four stair. The prisoner was in binders and wearing an active dampening collar. She engaged Bane unarmed. She was stunned. I saw her carried past my door unconscious. Bane then shot me and took the collar control from my belt. The prisoner did not kill Sergeant Holt. She did not kill any member of my staff. Every one of my dead is accounted for by Bane's crew."

She paused, as if consulting an internal list.

"For the record of this tribunal, I will add one further fact. Eighteen days before the raid, during a coolant rupture in Cut Five, I released the prisoner's collar on my own authority. For four minutes and nine seconds she had full use of the Force, and an open yard, and a perimeter fence she could have cleared without effort. She used the time to hold an ice shelf while twenty-three people got out from under it. Then she put it down and stood still while I re-engaged the collar. I have been Warden of Narkis for nine years. I have not seen a prisoner do that." The faintest pause. "I make no judgment. I state it because it is true, and the tribunal should have it."

She looked down, once, at something out of frame. "And one correction to my own file. The prisoner's posting to Terrace Eleven, Cut Three, in her second month came to my desk as a transfer order carrying an off-world authorization code. I signed it. I have not since been able to trace that code, and the camera on that terrace froze the same afternoon. I should have asked sooner."

Padmay did not look up from her datapad. She wrote the word code and underlined it twice.

The holo went dark.

"The second statement," Padmay said, into the quiet, "is from Guard Corporal Dannick, the only member of the Ring Four detail to survive the raid. It is shorter. He states that the prisoner pushed him into a supply alcove and told him to stay down before Bane reached the landing. He states that he believes this saved his life. He adds that an inmate of Ring Four, a Trandoshan named Krasso, took a bolt tackling one of Bane's crew off her back; Krasso survived, wounded, and has given a statement that Twenty-two fourteen never raised a hand against staff. He asked that it be recorded that he paid a debt." She set the datapad on the clerk's tray. "The defense submits that a prisoner who is stunned, bound, collared, and carried off by Separatist mercenaries has not escaped. She has been abducted. The defense further submits that a woman wrongly condemned to die in the ice, hunted under an order of lethal force issued in the field, who ran from that hunt rather than into the arms of Count Dooku, who refused Count Dooku to his face, and who then walked back into the custody of this Republic of her own will, with the true culprit's confession in her hands, has committed no crime this tribunal should be proud to name."

She sat down. Her right hand was shaking now. She put it under the table.

Tarkin rose again, but slower. "The defendant's associations remain, "

"Wilhuff."

Palpatine had not raised his voice. He did not need to. He had risen from the high chair, and the whole chamber turned to him the way plants turn in a window. He came forward to the edge of the dais, and his robes caught the light, and he looked down at the defense table with an expression of such tired, fatherly sorrow that Padmay, who had sat across from him in committee for years, felt it work on her even as she watched it.

"I think we have heard enough," he said. "I think this Republic has asked enough of this young woman."

Padmay's stomach tightened. She had a motion prepared. It was on the datapad at her elbow: a motion to vacate the convictions in their entirety, on the record, as unfounded. A finding that Ah-soka Tano had not done these things. She had written it at third hour that morning, while Anakin pretended to sleep.

"Five months ago," Palpatine said, "I stood in this room and exercised the powers of my office to spare a life. I told myself at the time it was mercy. I see now it was not enough. A great wrong was done here, in the Republic's name, and I presided over it. That error is mine to answer for, and I will answer for it." He lifted one hand. "By the emergency authority vested in this office, I grant Ah-soka Tano a full and unconditional pardon, for the offenses of which she was convicted before this tribunal, and for any and all acts arising from her abduction from Narkis and her return. No further charge will be brought. She leaves this chamber free."

A pardon. Padmay sat very still. A pardon was forgiveness. A pardon said you did a thing, and I, in my grace, release you from it. It did not say you did nothing. It could not be appealed, and it could not be refused, and it belonged entirely to the man who gave it. Her motion lay on the table beside her and was, in that moment, worth exactly as much as the datapad it was written on.

She half rose anyway. Bail Organa, in the senators' box, caught her eye and gave the smallest shake of his head. Not now. Not against this.

Palpatine had not finished.

"And I would be remiss," he said, "if I did not say before this chamber what I have said privately. One man never stopped believing in her. When the Order let her go, when this Senate condemned her, when the Holo-net named her a traitor, one man went into the dark after her, and brought her home." His gaze moved past Padmay and settled, warm as a lamp, on the first row. "Knight Skywalker. The Republic is fortunate in your loyalty. I am fortunate in it. I hope this day repays a little of what it has cost you."

Somebody began to clap. Padmay never saw who. It started in the upper tiers and rolled down through the delegation pods, and then the senators' box was on its feet, Chuchi first, Mothma a moment after, Bail last; and the lower galleries took it up, and the families in blue, some of them weeping; and the sound filled the great round chamber and went on and on, a whole Senate applauding itself for undoing what it had done.

Padmay turned, finally, and looked at her husband.

Anakin was standing. She did not think he knew he had stood up. His face was turned up toward the dais, and it was lit, there was no other word, lit the way it had been the first time she ever saw him, nine years old in a junk dealer's shop, before he knew the size of the galaxy or anything it would take from him. All the exhaustion of the last months was gone out of it. His mechanical hand had found the rail in front of him and was gripping it, and the metal under his glove made a small, tortured sound, and he didn't notice that either. He looked at Palpatine as though the Chancellor had reached down and handed him back the sun.

He had let Ah-soka go in a canyon on Florrum. Padmay knew that. Palpatine had just thanked him, before the entire Republic, for bringing her in.

And she had switched off the recorder so that no one would ever see what else he had done in that warehouse. She had done it for him. She had done it, she understood now with a cold, slow clarity, for exactly the same reason Palpatine had just waved the gap away. The two of them had protected the same thing. She did not know what that made her.

She looked at Ah-soka.

Ah-soka had not stood. Around her the chamber roared and stamped; the clerk was trying, uselessly, to restore order; up in the Jedi gallery Plo Koon had bowed his head, and Obi-Wan was looking not at the dais but at Anakin, with an expression Padmay recognized because she had worn it in a mirror that morning. Ah-soka sat at the defense table with her hands folded in her lap and her eyes on the evidence tray where her lightsabers lay sealed. She did not smile. She did not weep. She did not move at all, not her hands, not her lekoo, not the healing ring at her throat. She sat as perfectly still as a prisoner in a cell who has learned that stillness is the only thing in the room that belongs to her.

Padmay reached over and put her hand on Ah-soka's folded ones. They were cold.

"You're free," Padmay said, under the noise.

Ah-soka's eyes moved to her. Something passed behind them, too fast to catch.

"Am I?" she said.

Scene break.

It took an hour for the chamber to empty, and Padmay spent most of it being congratulated by people who had voted to convict.

She said the right things. She shook the hands. She let a junior senator from Kuat tell her it had been a privilege to witness, and she thanked him, and did not ask where he had been five months ago. Anakin had gone down with Ah-soka and Fox to the detention level for the release processing; there would be forms, a medical review, a formal return of personal effects that did not include her sabers. Evidence in an open matter, the clerk had said, apologetic. The Offee sentencing. Ah-soka had only nodded.

Padmay went to find her datapad, which she had left on the defense table in the confusion, and found the floor empty and the side passage behind the dais standing open, and she heard voices.

She should have turned around. She did not. She stood very still in the shadow of the arch with the useless motion in her hand.

", a disappointing afternoon for you, I'm afraid," Palpatine was saying. His voice was easy, almost amused. "You'll forgive me for cutting you off."

"I understood it was coming." Tarkin, dry as ever. "I'd have preferred to have been told when. Major Rusk's recovery team was stood down an hour ago, and the collar case has gone back to inventory. A closed file is a tidy thing."

"If you had been told, my friend, the Senate would have seen it on your face. They needed to see a prosecutor who believed in what he was doing. And you did, didn't you?" A pause, a rustle of heavy fabric. "You served the Republic's need for an answer when it needed one. Today it needed a different one. There's no shame in that. A firm hand is not less valuable because it occasionally has to be withdrawn."

"The collar program, "

"Will survive this. The Senate will feel it has been very generous today. Generous assemblies are seldom vigilant ones." Something that might have been a soft laugh. "When this war ends, Wilhuff, the Outer Rim will want governing. Not administering, governing. I have been giving some thought to whose hand that should be. I find I keep returning to one name."

A silence.

"I'm honored, Chancellor."

"Don't be honored. Be patient. And do send Commandant Vorne my thanks for her deposition. Such a precise woman. I'd like to keep an eye on her career."

Padmay took one step back out of the arch, and then another, and the hem of her gown whispered on the stone.

The voices stopped.

She made herself turn and walk, not quickly, toward the defense table, and pick up her datapad from where it lay, and tuck it under her arm. When she looked up, Palpatine was standing in the archway behind the dais. Tarkin was not with him. She hadn't heard him go.

"Senator Amidala." The Chancellor smiled down at her, warm, a little weary, exactly the face he had worn on the dais. "I'd hoped to catch you. That was splendid work. Truly. Naboo should be very proud."

"Thank you, Chancellor." Her voice came out steady. She was grateful to it.

"I'm afraid I rather stepped on your motion." His eyes went, very briefly, to the datapad under her arm, and she had the sudden impossible certainty that he knew exactly what was written on it. "Forgive an old man his sense of occasion. The result is the same, I think. She is free, and she is safe, and that is what we all wanted."

"It isn't quite the same," Padmay said.

He tilted his head, pleasantly, as though she'd made an interesting point in committee. "No? Well. We can talk about the distinction some other time." He began to turn, then paused. "Oh, the release processing will run late, I'm told. The Judiciary is very thorough. I've asked young Skywalker to stay and collect her personally, however long it takes. It seemed fitting, after today." His smile deepened, fond. "He's earned that much."

He went back through the archway. The door slid shut behind him.

Padmay stood alone on the tribunal floor, under the great dim ring of empty pods, and found that her hand had gone to her throat.

Her comlink buzzed. Anakin's code. She thumbed it on.

"They're saying it'll be hours," he said. His voice was still bright at the edges, still carrying the afternoon in it. "Some medical thing about the collar burns. I'm going to wait. Don't wait up." A pause, and the brightness faltered for a moment, and she heard the man underneath it, the one who had not slept. "Padmay. She asked them for something. Before they'll let her out."

"What?"

"Ten minutes on the cell level," Anakin said. "Fox says it's the same block. Same row as hers was." He breathed out. "She wants to see Barris. Tonight."

Padmay looked at the closed archway where the Chancellor had stood.

There's one more thing that's true, Barris had called through the rain. I couldn't say it in there.

And she had not. Not one word of it, in a room full of people, with her eyes on everything except the dais.

"Ani," Padmay said, and heard her own voice come out strange. "Stay with her. Whatever happens tonight. Don't leave the building until she does."

"I wasn't planning to," he said, puzzled. "Why?"

She did not have an answer that she could say aloud. She had only the memory of a warm, fond voice saying he's earned that much, and of a woman in detention grey who would not look at the dais, and somewhere far below her feet, in a cell she had once argued a girl out of, a confession that wasn't finished yet.

End of chapter.

# Chapter eleven: Intangible assets and intellectual property

On 30 September, in Group Year Three (GY3) of our invented group, Tarnmoor, Dan Hartley clears his desk at Calder Valve Engineering Ltd (CVE). His division is closing. The next morning, 1 October GY3, Calder sells that division's customer contracts and its manufacturing know-how to the group's Vallarian subsidiary, Tarnmoor Vallaria SA (TVS), for **£6.0m**. Vallaria, like everything else in this story except Ireland, is invented.

Think about what was sold. Not a building. Not a machine: the machines went separately. What crossed the border was a set of promises from customers and a body of knowledge, much of it written down in the weeks before from interviews with engineers like Dan. You cannot drop it on your foot. Yet it was worth £6.0m, and the tax system has to decide three things about it. Is the money income or a capital gain? What was it worth, when the buyer and the seller answer to the same board? And when the buyer is abroad, what stops the know-how leaving the UK tax net at a bargain price?

This chapter answers those questions. It sits in Part Two, on measuring profit, because the intangibles code is the clearest case of tax following the accounts. **Intangible fixed assets (IFAs) are core (grade 1)** for the LCG paper; the **Patent Box is awareness (grade 3)**. *The Living Law* (TKS), chapter 21, introduced the regime at the level of a company buying and writing off an asset. Here we go deeper: goodwill, reinvestment, groups, degrouping, related parties, the FA 2026 move to arm's length pricing, and the Patent Box. Everything is taught on FY2026 law (Finance Act 2026).

**Statutes in this chapter:** CTA 2009 Part 8 (ss 711–906); CTA 2010 Part 8A (Patent Box); TIOPA 2010 Part 4 (transfer pricing) and s 407 (CIR tax-EBITDA); TCGA 1992 s 179 (for contrast); FA 2026 Sch 6.

## Why intangibles got their own code

Before April 2002 a company's intangible assets lived in an awkward place. Goodwill and most other intangibles were capital assets: a company that bought goodwill got no deduction as it wore away, and on a sale it had a chargeable gain. Patents and know-how had their own small capital allowance codes. Trade marks and brands generally got nothing. The patchwork bore little relation to how the accounts treated the same assets, and it fitted badly with an economy whose value was moving from factories to ideas.

The answer was a single code, introduced by **FA 2002 Sch 29** and rewritten as **CTA 2009 Part 8**. Its central idea is simple: **for a company, an intangible fixed asset is taxed as income, not capital.** Amortisation and impairment in the accounts are deductions (debits); receipts and profits on sale are taxable credits; losses on sale are debits. Tax follows the accounts, with a few deliberate departures, which are where most of the exam marks sit.

The code is **symmetrical**, and that is its purpose. A company that gets a deduction as an asset is written down must give it back if it later sells for more. Think of a meter running in both directions: every pound of relief is metered out, and if the asset turns out to have kept its value, the meter runs back.

**The boundary.** Part 8 applies to assets created on or after **1 April 2002**, to assets acquired from an unrelated party on or after that date, and (from **1 July 2020**) to any asset acquired on or after 1 July 2020 whoever the seller is (s 882, as amended by FA 2020 s 31). Older assets stayed in the capital gains world. Their detailed rules (Part 8 Chs 16, 16A, 16B) are **outside the LCG syllabus**: spot that an asset is old and say it sits outside the code.

For a large group the code matters in four ways: it relieves bought intangibles; it taxes the sale of home-grown ones, often at far more than book value; it lets assets move round a group without tax but polices them when a company leaves; and it sets the price when an intangible crosses a border to a related company.

## What counts as an intangible fixed asset

The definition starts with the accounts. An **intangible asset** has its GAAP meaning (s 712) and includes **intellectual property**: patents, trade marks, registered designs, copyright and design right, and also **any idea, information or technique not protected by such a right**, which is how know-how gets in. Goodwill is expressly included (s 715).

The word **fixed** matters: the asset must be acquired or created **for use on a continuing basis** in the company's activities (s 713). Know-how bought to be resold quickly is not a fixed asset.

**Excluded assets** (Part 8 Ch 10, ss 800–816) include rights over land and tangible movable property, financial assets, and **shares and rights in companies and trusts**. Shares are the big one: when a group buys a company it buys shares, and the goodwill inside that company never becomes the group's IFA. A company may also elect to keep capital spending on computer software within capital allowances.

An asset inside the code is a **chargeable intangible asset**. It is used in a **trade**, held for a **property business**, or held for another purpose. Trading credits and debits go straight into trading profits; everything else is **non-trading** and pools into a non-trading gain or loss on intangibles (below).

**In our invented case**, the customer contracts and know-how Calder sold were trading assets and home-grown: built up over decades, with the cost of building them always charged in the accounts as incurred. So they had **no balance sheet cost and no tax cost**. That decides the size of the tax bill on the sale.

The same is true of most intellectual property a group creates. Project Ashlar's research costs were expensed and relieved through the RDEC (chapter 10); the resulting patents carry only a small capitalised cost such as filing fees. That is fair (the research was relieved once), but it means that **when home-grown IP is sold, almost all of the price is taxable**. Groups that think of their patents as capital assets protected by the substantial shareholding exemption (SSE) are often surprised: the SSE protects a sale of **shares**, never a sale of the assets themselves.

## Writing down by the accounts, or at 4%

**The default (s 729).** The tax debit follows the accounts: amortisation and impairment charged in the profit and loss account are deductible; a later reversal of an impairment is a credit. Where the tax cost differs from the accounting value, the accounting write-off is scaled by the ratio of tax written down value (WDV, s 742) to accounting value (AV) immediately before the charge.

**The problem.** Some intangibles are never amortised. Under IFRS (IFRS 3 and IAS 38), goodwill and intangibles with an indefinite useful life are not amortised, only tested for impairment. Under FRS 102 every intangible, goodwill included, is treated as having a finite life and amortised. Two identical acquisitions can produce very different deductions depending on the framework.

**The fixed-rate election (ss 730–731).** A company may elect to write an asset down at **4% of cost a year** whatever the accounts do. The debit is the **lower of 4% of cost and the remaining tax WDV** (pro rata for short periods). At 4% the cost is relieved over 25 years.

| Condition | Rule |
|---|---|
| Form | In writing to HMRC |
| Deadline | Within **2 years after the end of the AP** in which the asset was created or acquired |
| Scope | All capitalised expenditure on that asset; asset by asset |
| Revocation | **Irrevocable** |

> **Worked example 11.1 (labelled; not a story fact): the 4% election**
>
> Suppose Tarnmoor Engineering Ltd (TEL) buys a registered trade mark for £5,000,000 and its IFRS-based accounts give it an indefinite life (no amortisation).
>
> | | Without election | With 4% election |
> |---|---|---|
> | Annual debit | £0 (unless impaired) | £200,000 |
> | CT saving at 25% | £0 | £50,000 a year |
> | Years to relieve cost | indefinite | 25 |
>
> For a long-lived asset the accounts never amortise, the election is usually worth making. For an asset written off over five years in the accounts, it would slow relief down. Choose asset by asset, inside the two-year window.

**One twist for goodwill:** goodwill and customer-related assets that qualify for relief at all are written down at a fixed **6.5%** (s 879B), not 4% and not by the accounts (see below).

## Realisation, when an intangible leaves

A **realisation** (s 734) is a transaction that results in the asset ceasing to be recognised on the balance sheet, or in a reduction in its accounting value; an asset with no balance sheet value is treated as if it had one (s 734(3)). A sale is the obvious case; a part disposal is a part realisation.

| Asset's history | Credit (or debit if negative) | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Written down for tax | Proceeds − tax WDV | s 735 |
| Not written down for tax | Proceeds − cost recognised for tax | s 736 |
| Part realisation | Proceeds − matching proportion of cost / WDV | Ch 4 |

**Proceeds** are the amount recognised in the accounts, less incidental costs of realisation; non-money consideration is treated as money equal to its open market value (s 739, including s 739(1A) from FA 2018).

Because an asset never on the balance sheet is treated as having a balance sheet value, **home-grown assets are caught in full**: their cost was expensed long ago, so their tax cost is nil, and the whole of the proceeds is a credit.

> **Worked example 11.2 (invented story): Calder's sale to TVS, 1 October GY3**
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Proceeds (customer contracts and know-how) | 6,000,000 |
> | Less: cost recognised for tax (home-grown, never capitalised) | 0 |
> | **Realisation credit (trading)** | **6,000,000** |
> | CT at 25% (Calder: main rate, very large) | 1,500,000 |
>
> The credit is taxed in trading profits in Calder's **9-month AP 1 April–31 December GY3**. There is no capital gain, no indexation, no capital loss to offset and no SSE: it is income. The related plant (£1.4m) follows the capital allowance rules of chapter 8.

**The price.** £6.0m is what Calder and TVS agreed; both are owned by Tarnmoor plc. The law does not simply accept it: it asks what independent parties would have paid (see the related party section). In our invented case HMRC later argues for £9.5m, and the dispute settles in GY6 at £8.0m (an adjustment of £2.0m: CT £500,000 plus interest), which is chapter 27's story. **A realisation credit is only as good as the price behind it.**

## Non-trading debits, credits and losses

Not every intangible is used in a trade. A holding company may own a brand and license it to subsidiaries; an investment company may hold a patent portfolio. For those assets, royalties, write-downs and realisation credits and debits are **pooled** into a single **non-trading gain or loss on intangible fixed assets** for the period (Part 8 Ch 6).

- A **net gain** is taxed as part of total profits.
- A **net loss** (s 753) may be (1) set against **total profits of the same AP** on a claim, (2) **surrendered as group relief** (chapter 15), or (3) **carried forward** as a non-trading debit of the next AP, joining that period's pool.

> **Worked example 11.3 (labelled): the non-trading pool**
>
> Suppose a holding company owns brands licensed to the group's trading companies.
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Royalties received | 1,000,000 |
> | Write-down of brands | (300,000) |
> | Loss on sale of one brand | (200,000) |
> | **Non-trading gain on IFAs** (taxed with total profits) | **500,000** |

**Two exam points.** First, a debit that the goodwill rules deny as a write-down may still arise on **realisation**, and it is then a **non-trading debit** even if the asset was used in the trade: a blocked deduction cannot be turned into a trading loss. Second, the change-of-ownership rules in **CTA 2010 Part 14** can block the carry-forward of non-trading intangibles losses, as they block other carried-forward reliefs (chapter 14).

For a trading group like Tarnmoor most intangibles are trading assets. In the exam, a holding company with a licensing arm is a favourite way of moving an intangible out of the trade and into the pool: **read the facts for who uses the asset, not who owns it.**

## Goodwill, a door that opened, slammed and reopened

Picture a door. In **2002** it opened: companies buying a business could write off purchased goodwill, following their accounts. For acquisitions from **8 July 2015** it slammed shut: relief for goodwill and customer-related intangibles was withdrawn (F(No.2)A 2015; the date is written into s 879D). From **1 April 2019** it reopened, but only for visitors carrying intellectual property (FA 2019, CTA 2009 Part 8 Ch 15A).

**Why slam it?** HM Treasury's 2015 policy paper said relief was available only where a company bought a business directly, not where it bought the shares of a company owning the business, which distorted deals towards asset purchases; removing it "brings the UK regime in line with other major economies, reduces distortion and levels the playing field for merger and acquisition transactions". **Why reopen it?** HMRC's tax information and impact note on the 2019 reform said the reinstatement was meant to support UK investment in intangible assets and improve the UK's attractiveness as a place to do business. The 2019 compromise is the law today.

**Relevant assets (s 879A):** goodwill; information relating to customers or potential customers; customer relationships (contractual or not); unregistered trade marks and other signs used in a business; and licences of any of these.

**The rate (s 879B):** a relevant asset acquired or created on or after 1 April 2019 is written down at a fixed **6.5% a year** (a deemed fixed-rate election at 6.5% instead of 4%).

**Three restrictions (no write-down debits; any realisation debit is non-trading):**

| # | Restriction | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pre-2019 cases: asset caught by the 2015 regime (acquired 8 July 2015–31 March 2019 and chargeable in the 29 October 2018–31 March 2019 window), or acquired later from a related party in such a case | ss 879C–879H |
| 2 | **Not acquired as part of a business**, or acquired with a business that includes **no qualifying IP assets** | s 879I |
| 3 | Acquired from a **related individual or firm** (typically an incorporation), unless the third-party acquisition condition is met | s 879K |

**Qualifying IP assets (s 879J):** patents, registered designs, copyright and design right, plant breeders' rights, and their foreign equivalents and licences (not excluded or pre-FA 2002 assets).

**The 6× cap (ss 879M–879O).** Where the business includes qualifying IP but the relevant assets cost more than **6 × the IP expenditure**, the debits are scaled down in proportion: in effect relief on relevant-asset cost is limited to six times the qualifying IP spend. (The s 879O formula is an image on legislation.gov.uk; the rule is taken from s 879M's wording.)

> **Worked example 11.4 (labelled): the 6× cap**
>
> Suppose a group company buys a business for £20,000,000: patents £2,000,000; goodwill and customer relationships £15,000,000; plant and stock £3,000,000.
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Qualifying IP expenditure (A) | 2,000,000 |
> | Cap: 6 × A | 12,000,000 |
> | Relevant assets (B) | 15,000,000 |
> | Proportion relievable: 12,000,000 / 15,000,000 | 0.8 |
> | 6.5% × £15,000,000 × 0.8 = **annual debit** | **780,000** |
> | CT value at 25% | 195,000 |
>
> The patents themselves are relieved separately (accounts basis or 4% election).

**In our invented case:** in **GY2** Tarnmoor Ireland Ltd (TIL), then UK resident because its board met in Leeds, bought an Irish distributor's business including its **customer list** for **£3.0m**. The distributor had no qualifying IP, so the list is a relevant asset acquired with a business with no qualifying IP: **no debits** (s 879I). Its tax value stays at its cost of **£3.0m**. That matters when TIL migrates in GY4 and the list is deemed realised at market value (chapter 22). Had the business included enough qualifying IP, the list would have attracted 6.5% × £3.0m = £195,000 of debits a year.

## The myth of relief for bought goodwill

**Misconception: "Goodwill bought with a business always gets tax relief."** People believe it because it was true from 2002 to 2015, because accountants amortise goodwill under FRS 102, and because "acquisition" sounds like a purchase of assets.

It is wrong in at least four ways:

1. **Buying shares gives no relief.** The goodwill is the subsidiary's, or appears only on consolidation; Part 8 never sees it (shares are excluded assets). Most large acquisitions are share deals. Nor can a group fix that by **hiving the business up** afterwards: the intra-group transfer is tax-neutral (s 775), so the transferee takes over the subsidiary's own tax cost (for home-grown goodwill usually nil), not the price paid for the shares.
2. **An asset deal with no qualifying IP gives no debits** (s 879I), as TIL found.
3. **The 6× cap** limits relief even where IP is acquired.
4. **Related-individual acquisitions** (incorporations) are blocked (s 879K).

And where relief is due it is at **6.5%** (about 15½ years for the whole cost), not the accounts rate.

> **Going further: asset deal or share deal?**
>
> A **buyer** often prefers an asset deal: relief on the IP (accounts or 4%) and, within the cap, on goodwill at 6.5%; capital allowances on plant at its price. A **seller** that is a company usually prefers a share sale: a trading subsidiary's shares can often be sold within the SSE, while an asset sale by the subsidiary taxes home-grown intangibles in full as income. The tension is at the heart of chapter 20 (buying and selling companies); price usually settles it. Stamp taxes differ too (chapter 21): stamp duty at 0.5% on shares against SDLT on any land in an asset deal.

> **Exam lens: goodwill and IFAs in a computation**
>
> - **Grade:** IFAs and IP (excluding Part 8 Chs 16, 16A, 16B, 17) **core (1)** in the 2026 grid (up from 2 in the draft 2025 grid); **falls to 2 in the 2028 grid** (not your sitting).
> - **Past appearance:** **N23 Q4 (20 marks)**: a loss-making retailer's CT computation included **goodwill and registered designs acquired in 2019**. The registered designs were qualifying IP, which opened the door for the goodwill; then the cap and the 6.5% rate. The examiners reported the question was very well answered overall. IFAs have not been a standalone question M23–M26.
> - **Style:** "Calculate, with explanations, the tax-adjusted trading profit"; 0.5–1 mark per point; explain each adjustment (add back the accounts amortisation; deduct the tax debit; say why).
> - **Traps:** the three restrictions; the 6× cap; 6.5% not 4%; acquisition date (from 1 April 2019); shares never give relief; restricted goodwill's realisation debit is non-trading.

## Reinvestment relief

Should a company that sells an intangible at a profit and buys another be taxed on the first sale? The gains code says no through roll-over relief; Part 8 has **reinvestment relief** (Ch 7, ss 754–763).

**Conditions:**
- the old asset was a chargeable intangible asset throughout the company's ownership;
- **proceeds exceed the old asset's cost recognised for tax** (s 755);
- expenditure on **other chargeable intangible assets** in the window **12 months before to 3 years after** the realisation (HMRC may extend) (s 756);
- the new expenditure is **capitalised** in the accounts;
- a **claim** (HMRC's manual, CIRD20150: normal 4-year claim time limit; a provisional declaration is possible).

**Computation (s 758; CIRD20210–20220):**

| Case | Relief |
|---|---|
| Expenditure on new assets ≥ proceeds | Proceeds − cost of old asset |
| Expenditure < proceeds | Expenditure − cost of old asset (nil if negative) |

The realisation credit is reduced by the relief, and so is the **tax cost of the new asset**. **Write-downs already given are always clawed back**: the relief defers only profit above original cost. The shape mirrors the gains rule, where the amount not reinvested comes into charge.

> **Worked example 11.5 (labelled): partial reinvestment**
>
> Suppose a company bought a brand for £1,000,000, wrote it down for tax to £600,000, and sells it for £3,000,000. Within the window it spends £2,500,000 on a new patent (capitalised).
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Proceeds | 3,000,000 |
> | Less tax WDV | (600,000) |
> | Realisation credit before relief | 2,400,000 |
> | Relief: expenditure £2,500,000 − cost £1,000,000 (expenditure < proceeds) | (1,500,000) |
> | **Taxable credit** | **900,000** |
> | = write-downs clawed back £400,000 + proceeds not reinvested £500,000 | |
> | New patent: cost £2,500,000 − relief £1,500,000 = **tax cost** | **1,000,000** |
>
> Had the company spent £3,200,000 (at least the proceeds), relief would be £3,000,000 − £1,000,000 = £2,000,000; taxable credit £400,000 (the clawback only); new asset tax cost £1,200,000.

**Groups.** Expenditure by another company in the same intangibles group counts, on a joint claim (s 777). If a group company **acquires control of a company** that owns chargeable intangibles, the underlying assets can count as reinvestment (ss 778–779), even though the group bought shares.

**In our invented case,** Tom Hesketh, the group head of tax, considers whether Calder's £6.0m credit could be deferred. With a nil tax cost, the potential relief is large, but it needs capitalised spending on new chargeable intangibles by Calder or a group company inside the window, and a claim cuts that new asset's tax cost by the same amount. He finds nothing that makes a claim worth having. **No claim is made; Calder pays the tax.**

## Tax-neutral transfers inside a group

Groups move intangibles: a brand to a brand-owning company; patents into a new subsidiary before a sale. If each move were a taxable realisation at market value, groups would be stuck. So Part 8 makes intra-group transfers **tax-neutral**.

**The group (ss 764–773):** built like the gains group: a principal company, its 75% subsidiaries and their 75% subsidiaries, provided each is an **effective 51% subsidiary** of the principal company. Non-resident companies can be members.

**The rule (s 775).** A transfer is tax-neutral if:
1. both companies are **members of the same group** at the time of the transfer;
2. the asset is a **chargeable intangible asset of the transferor immediately before** the transfer; and
3. it is a **chargeable intangible asset of the transferee immediately after**.

**Effect (s 776):** no credit or debit for the transferor; the transferee is treated as having held the asset all along and done everything the transferor did (same cost, same WDV, same history). **TIOPA 2010 Part 4 (transfer pricing) does not apply** to the transfer (s 775).

**Exclusions** include a transferee that is a dual resident investing company, and a transferor with a **branch exemption election (s 18A)** where the asset has been used in its exempt foreign PE.

**Why the TVS sale is not neutral.** TVS is a member of the Tarnmoor intangibles group, but after the sale the know-how is **not a chargeable intangible asset of TVS**, which is not within UK CT on it. Condition 3 fails: the 1 October GY3 sale is a **realisation at the transfer price**, taxed in Calder. Tax neutrality is only for assets that stay inside the UK net.

**A transfer that passes.** On **1 February GY6** TEL hives down its actuators business into **Tarnmoor Actuators Ltd (TAL)**. The assets include TEL's actuator **patents**: **tax WDV £1.2m; market value £4.0m**. Both companies are group members within UK CT, so the patents pass tax-neutrally: TAL takes over WDV £1.2m. (Chapter 19 covers the hive-down of the trade under CTA 2010 Part 22 Ch 1; chapter 17 the factory under TCGA s 171.)

## The degrouping charge and its switch-off

Tax neutrality invites a game: move an asset into a subsidiary at no tax cost, then sell the subsidiary's shares. The **degrouping charge** (s 780) stops it.

**The rule.** Where a company (the transferee) **leaves the group within 6 years** of acquiring an asset in a tax-neutral transfer, and still holds the asset (or an associated company leaving with it does, s 783), it is treated as having **realised and immediately reacquired the asset at its market value at the time of the original transfer**. The resulting credit or debit is brought into account **immediately before it leaves**.

**Contrast with TCGA 1992 s 179:**

| | Gains (s 179) | Intangibles (s 780) |
|---|---|---|
| Time limit | 6 years | 6 years |
| Value | Market value at the intra-group acquisition | Market value at the time of the transfer |
| Who bears it | Since FA 2011 (leavers via share disposals): **added to the seller's consideration** for the shares (s 179(3D)), so the SSE can exempt it | **The leaving company** |
| SSE share sale | Exempt through the SSE on the adjusted proceeds | **Switched off** (s 782A) |
| Buyer's tax cost after an SSE sale | Rebased to market value at the transfer | **No uplift**: the company keeps the transferred WDV |

**Section 782A** (FA 2019 s 26; degroupings on or after **7 November 2018**). Where a company leaves because of a **disposal of shares by another group company** and that disposal would qualify for the SSE (ignoring TCGA Sch 7AC para 6, and provided it is not part of arrangements for an onward disposal), **s 780 does not apply**. It also covers the s 783 associated-company charge (CIRD40570–40575). HMRC's manual describes the purpose as stopping degrouping adjustments impeding genuine commercial disposals of trading businesses. Think of it as the twin of the gains rule reached by a different road: the gains rule moves the charge into an exempt sale; the intangibles rule switches it off.

> **Worked example 11.6 (invented story; the counterfactual is labelled): TAL's patents**
>
> On **31 December GY6** TEL sells TAL to Brennock Industries Inc (invented US buyer) for £48.0m plus an earn-out, 11 months after the hive-down. Chapter 20 explains why the sale qualifies for the SSE (TCGA Sch 7AC para 15A). **So s 782A switches off the patents' degrouping charge.**
>
> | Counterfactual (no switch-off; ignoring write-downs since 1 February GY6) | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Market value of patents at the transfer (1 February GY6) | 4,000,000 |
> | Less tax WDV | (1,200,000) |
> | Degrouping credit in TAL | 2,800,000 |
> | CT at 25% | 700,000 |
> | **Actual charge (s 782A)** | **nil** |
>
> The price of the switch-off: with no deemed reacquisition, the patents keep their tax WDV of **£1.2m** in TAL. Brennock gets no uplift, which a well-advised buyer reflects in the price.

**Where the switch-off does not apply,** the group's tools are: an election to **reallocate** the degrouping credit to another group company (s 792); **reinvestment relief** against it (s 791); and HMRC's power to **recover** unpaid tax from other group companies or a controlling director (s 795). There are also exceptions for certain mergers (s 789) and other cases (ss 782, 785, 787). In the exam, the first question is always whether the share sale qualifies for the SSE.

> **Exam lens: groups and degrouping**
>
> - **Grade:** core (1), within IFAs.
> - **How tested:** inside a sale-of-subsidiary question alongside TCGA s 179 and the SSE (compare M24 Q3, 15 marks: degrouping added to proceeds and SSE). A complete answer covers both regimes.
> - **Traps:** (1) the intangibles charge is on the **leaving company**, not added to the seller's proceeds; (2) market value **at the time of the transfer**; (3) s 782A needs an **SSE-qualifying share disposal**; (4) a transfer to a non-UK group company is **not** tax-neutral.

## Related parties and the arm's length turn of 2026

The group rules cover transfers inside the UK net; the **related party rules (ss 835–847)** cover everything else between parties not at arm's length. Without them a company could sell an intangible cheaply to a friendly party, or buy one dearly, and shift profit with a stroke of a pen.

**Related parties (s 835)** include a company that controls or is controlled by another, companies under common control, a company in which another has a **major interest**, and, for close companies, participators and their associates. Between related parties a transfer of a chargeable intangible asset is treated as made at **market value** (s 845), except for tax-neutral group transfers.

**Two valuation standards.** Market value asks what the asset would fetch in an open market. The **arm's length price** under **TIOPA 2010 Part 4** asks what independent enterprises would have agreed in comparable circumstances, considering each party's functions, assets, risks and realistic options. For a cross-border transfer both could apply, with different answers.

**FA 2026 Sch 6 paras 25 and 28** chose one:

| Point | Rule |
|---|---|
| Scope | A **cross-border** transfer of a chargeable intangible asset between related parties that is **subject to transfer pricing** (TIOPA Part 4) |
| Effect | Market value rule **disapplied**; transfer treated as made at the **arm's length price** (new s 845(4ZA)–(4ZG), (6), (7); new s 846) |
| Other related-party transfers | Market value still applies |
| Commencement | Transfers and grants made **on or after 1 January 2026**, unless made under a contract that became unconditional before that date |

> **Worked example 11.7 (labelled real-calendar example)**
>
> A UK company sells a brand to its French sister company on **15 February 2026**. The transfer is cross-border and subject to transfer pricing, so it is priced at **arm's length**, not market value. Had it been made under a contract that became **unconditional in December 2025**, the old market value rule would govern.

**In our invented case** every story period falls under FA 2026 law, so Calder's sale to TVS is priced at arm's length. The £6.0m came from a valuation report. Whether it was the arm's length price is a transfer pricing question about who really controlled the development and exploitation of the know-how; Dan's functional analysis interview returns in chapter 27.

> **Exam lens: related parties and FA 2026**
>
> - New for FA 2026 sittings: expect a cross-border restructuring (IP moved to an overseas subsidiary) where the answer must say **arm's length price under TIOPA Part 4, not s 845 market value**, and that **s 775 does not apply** because the asset leaves the UK charge.
> - Do not drift into a full transfer pricing analysis unless asked (the examiners penalise answering the question not asked); one paragraph flagging TP and documentation earns the marks.

## The Patent Box, a 10% rate for inventions

The last tool is a reward. The **Patent Box (CTA 2010 Part 8A)** lets a company elect to have profits from patented inventions taxed at an effective **10%** (the "special IP rate"). It is **awareness (grade 3)**: principles, not full computation.

**Why it exists.** It is an incentive: cutting the tax on profits from patented inventions makes the UK more attractive as a place to own and exploit them, not just to do the research. It began on **1 April 2013**, phased in so that the full benefit applied from **1 April 2017** (CIRD201010). It was reshaped by the OECD's BEPS work on harmful tax practices (Action 5), which requires IP regimes to reward R&D done by the company itself: the **nexus approach**, for elections made **after 30 June 2016**; Ch 2A computes relevant IP profits for APs beginning on or after **1 July 2021** (and for new entrants).

**Conditions (outline; Part 8A Ch 2; GOV.UK guidance, last updated 2020):**
- liable to CT; makes a **Patent Box election**;
- holds **qualifying IP rights** (patents granted by the UK IPO, the European Patent Office or specified EEA states, and certain similar rights) or an **exclusive licence** of them;
- the **development condition**: it, or a group company, carried out **qualifying development** on the invention;
- if a group company did the development, the **active ownership** condition: the holder must actively manage its portfolio.

**The mechanism: a deduction, not a rate (s 357A).**

Deduction = RP × (MR − IPR) / MR

where RP = relevant IP profits, MR = main rate (25%), IPR = 10%. At 25% the deduction is **15/25 = 3/5 of RP**. (For a company taxed at the small profits rate, the formula uses that rate so the 10% result still holds.)

**Relevant IP profits in outline (Ch 2A, streaming; CIRD275000):**
1. stream total gross income between **relevant IP income** (sales of patented items, licence fees and royalties, infringement income, notional royalties) and other income, allocating expenses;
2. deduct a **routine return** of **10%** of specified costs (ordinary manufacturing profit is not a reward for invention);
3. deduct a **marketing assets return** (or, for small claims, a simplified small claims figure);
4. multiply by the **R&D (nexus) fraction**:

R&D fraction = (D + S1) × 1.3 / (D + S1 + S2 + A), capped at 1

where D = the company's own qualifying R&D expenditure; S1 = R&D subcontracted to **unconnected** persons; S2 = R&D subcontracted to **connected** persons; A = cost of **acquiring** qualifying IP (including licence fees for the right to exploit it).

> **Worked example 11.8 (labelled): the nexus fraction**
>
> Suppose a later patent family cost: own R&D (D) £3,000,000; unconnected subcontractor (S1) £500,000; connected group subcontractor (S2) £1,000,000; acquired licence (A) £500,000.
>
> R&D fraction = (3,000,000 + 500,000) × 1.3 / 5,000,000 = **0.91**.
>
> If the stream's profit after the routine and marketing returns were £2,000,000, relevant IP profits would be £1,820,000 and the deduction £1,820,000 × 15/25 = £1,092,000.

> **Worked example 11.9 (invented story; simplified): Calder's Patent Box, GY4**
>
> Calder filed Project Ashlar patents in **GY2**; they were **granted in GY4**. Calder elects into the Patent Box from **GY4** and licenses the patents to TVS for a royalty of **6% of TVS's Ashlar valve sales**: **£1.2m a year** (implying Ashlar sales of £20m). Calder did the development itself, with work subcontracted to Brackenwell Sensors Ltd before Tarnmoor acquired it (then unconnected: S1), so the nexus fraction is (D + S1) × 1.3 / (D + S1) → capped at **1**.
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Relevant IP profits (simplified; after streaming, routine and marketing returns) | 900,000 |
> | Patent Box deduction: 900,000 × (25% − 10%) / 25% | (540,000) |
> | Taxable | 360,000 |
> | CT at 25% | **90,000** (= 10% of £900,000) |
> | CT without the Patent Box (25% × £900,000) | 225,000 |
> | Saving | 135,000 |
>
> *Simplified: the real computation streams Calder's income and applies every step. The royalty bears Vallarian withholding at the invented treaty rate of 5% (£60,000); credit relief and its limit after the Patent Box deduction are chapter 24's.*

**Election and exit:** the election must be made no later than the last day for amending the return for the first AP it is to cover, that is within **12 months after the filing date** (CIRD260100). A company may revoke; it **cannot re-elect for 5 years** after the end of the AP in which the revocation takes effect (CIRD260110).

> **Exam lens: Patent Box**
>
> - **Grade:** awareness (3). **Not examined M23–M26.**
> - **What you need:** spot that an election would help a company with profits from patents; state the 10% effective rate and the deduction formula; name the main conditions (qualifying patents, development, active ownership, election) and the nexus restriction. You will not be asked to stream profits.
> - **Interactions:** RDEC and Patent Box can both apply to the same invention (one relieves the research cost, the other the profits); the Patent Box deduction is **excluded from CIR tax-EBITDA** (TIOPA s 407(3)(l)); dealings that are **patent box provision** are excluded from the new UK-to-UK TP exemption (TIOPA s 164A; chapter 27).

## The tax function's work, and the other regimes

**Acquisitions.** On an asset deal the **purchase price allocation** drives everything: how much is qualifying IP, how much goodwill can be relieved within the 6× cap, what capital allowances and SDLT follow. The tax team works with the valuers before the accounts are finalised and diarises every **4% election** inside its two-year window.

**Internal moves.** Before any hive-down, confirm s 775 applies and **record the market value at the date of transfer** (the value a degrouping charge would use within six years). Before any share sale, check the SSE, because it decides whether s 782A applies.

**The calendar:**

| Item | Deadline |
|---|---|
| 4% fixed-rate election | 2 years after the end of the AP of creation or acquisition |
| Patent Box election | 12 months after the filing date of the return for the first AP |
| Patent Box re-entry after revocation | Not for 5 years |
| Reinvestment relief claim | Normal claim time limit (CIRD20150); provisional declaration possible |
| Degrouping exposure | 6 years from each tax-neutral transfer |

**Cross-border transfers.** Since 1 January 2026 a transfer to an overseas group company is priced at arm's length: a functional analysis, a valuation and contemporaneous TP documentation (Master File and Local File for a group like Tarnmoor). A restructuring that moves know-how abroad is exactly the transaction HMRC reviews (chapter 27).

**Royalties.** A UK company paying a royalty for IP to a non-resident must normally deduct income tax at the basic rate (ITA 2007 s 906), unless it reasonably believes a lower treaty rate applies (s 911) (chapter 23). Calder's royalty from TVS bears Vallarian withholding of 5% (£60,000 a year); the credit and its limit are chapter 24's.

**CIR (chapter 28).** Tax-EBITDA ignores the **Patent Box deduction** (TIOPA s 407(3)(l)). HMRC's guidance (CFM95805) says it also ignores **relevant intangibles debits and credits** (s 407, defined in s 408): mainly amortisation, including 4% write-downs, **losses and gains on disposals of IFAs**, and reversals of excluded amounts. On that guidance, a large realisation credit like Calder's £6.0m raises taxable profits but **not** interest capacity. Chapter 28 works through the effect on Tarnmoor's figures.

> **Going further: judgement and ethics**
>
> Pricing an intra-group IP transfer is a judgement with a wide range. The tax function's duty is to document a defensible arm's length price at the time, not to pick the lowest number in a valuer's range. Where the advantage of a filing position exceeds the UTT threshold, consider notification (chapter 4). Penalties for an understated TP price turn on reasonable care; Tarnmoor's records later spare it a penalty (chapter 27).

## What to take away

- IFAs are taxed as **income following the accounts** (CTA 2009 Part 8), for assets created or acquired from **1 April 2002** and all acquisitions from **1 July 2020**.
- Write-downs follow the accounts unless the company elects for **4% a year**: within **2 years** after the end of the AP of acquisition; **irrevocable**; best for assets the accounts never amortise.
- **Realisation:** proceeds − tax WDV (or − cost if never written down). A home-grown asset has no tax cost: **Calder's £6.0m sale to TVS gave a £6.0m trading credit and £1.5m of CT**.
- **Goodwill and customer-related assets** acquired from 1 April 2019: **6.5%** a year, only if acquired with a business that includes **qualifying IP**, and only up to **6×** the IP spend; none for related-individual acquisitions. **TIL's £3.0m customer list gets no debits.** Buying shares never gives relief.
- **Reinvestment relief:** window 12 months before to 3 years after; capitalised spend; defers only profit above original cost; write-downs always clawed back.
- **Groups:** a 75% / effective 51% group; transfers tax-neutral only if the asset is **chargeable in both companies' hands**; a transfer to an overseas company outside the UK net is a realisation.
- **Degrouping:** leaving within **6 years** holding the asset → realisation at market value at the transfer, charged on the **leaving company**; **switched off** by an SSE-qualifying share sale (s 782A), which saved **TAL a £2.8m credit (£700,000 CT)**, at the cost of no uplift.
- **Related parties:** market value; since **1 January 2026**, cross-border transfers subject to TP at the **arm's length price** (FA 2026 Sch 6 paras 25, 28).
- **Patent Box:** effective **10%** via a deduction of RP × 15/25; for Calder, RP **£900,000** → deduction **£540,000** → CT **£90,000**.
- All of this is FY2026 law.

The intangibles code showed tax following the accounts for things you cannot touch. Chapter 12, on loan relationships and derivatives, follows the accounts for the most intangible thing of all: money owed and money promised.

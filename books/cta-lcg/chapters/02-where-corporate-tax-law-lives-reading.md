# Chapter 2: Where corporate tax law lives

On 18 March 2026, the Finance Act 2026 received Royal Assent. Sixteen years earlier to the day, on 18 March 2010, the Taxation (International and Other Provisions) Act 2010 had received its own. TIOPA 2010 was the last product of a fourteen-year project to rewrite the UK's direct tax statutes in a clearer, better ordered form. Its aim was to restate the law, not to change it.

FA 2026 went a step further than any rewrite Act. In its transfer pricing and permanent establishment rules, it points to texts written by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), and it tells us to read them as they stand from time to time, not as they stood on the day Parliament voted.

So where does corporate tax law actually live? In Acts and regulations, certainly. But also in treaties, in accounting standards that Parliament never wrote, in judgments, and now in international texts that change without a vote at Westminster. Around all of that sits a large body of HMRC guidance that is not law at all, yet shapes every enquiry.

This is the second chapter of Part One, and it is the book's "how we know" chapter. *The Living Law* (TKS), chapter 2, explained statute, interpretation, the tribunals and the status of guidance. Here we go further, into the sources a larger company's tax function uses every week. No single row of the syllabus grid is owned by this chapter, but the 2026 grid makes "Double tax treaties – application of OECD model and supplied extracts from treaties" and "Impact of accounting standards on taxable profits" core (grade 1), and Note 2 of the grid expects knowledge of the Law, Professional Responsibilities and Ethics, and Principles of Accounting manuals. We finish in the exam room, with what the screen gives you and what it does not.

## The statute book a group lives in

Start with the Acts. As *The Living Law*, chapter 2, explained, statute is the law; everything else either interprets it or applies it. For a larger company the statute book is a handful of big Acts, and most of them come from the **Tax Law Rewrite Project**. The project began with a blueprint paper, *Tax Law Rewrite: The Way Forward*, in July 1996, and produced seven Acts: CAA 2001, ITEPA 2003, ITTOIA 2005, ITA 2007, CTA 2009, CTA 2010 and TIOPA 2010. The explanatory notes say the rewritten Acts do not generally change the meaning of the law. They restate it, in a new order.

**The corporate statute map (FY2026)**

| Statute | Spoken name (audio) | What a larger company finds there |
|---|---|---|
| Corporation Tax Act 2009 (CTA 2009) | "the two thousand and nine Act" | The charge (s 5) and residence (s 14); UK PE profits (ss 19–21, as rewritten by FA 2026); the foreign branch exemption (Part 2 Ch 3A, ss 18A–18S); trading income (Part 3, including s 46); loan relationships (Part 5); derivative contracts (Part 7); intangible fixed assets (Part 8); R&D (Part 13) |
| Corporation Tax Act 2010 (CTA 2010) | "the twenty ten Act" | Losses (Part 4); group and consortium relief (Part 5); carried-forward losses and the restriction (Parts 5A, 7ZA); change in ownership (Part 14); Part 22; distributions (Part 23); definitions (Part 24), including control (s 1124), the PE definition (ss 1140A–1144) and GAAP (s 1127) |
| Taxation (International and Other Provisions) Act 2010 (TIOPA 2010) | "the International Act" | Treaties and DTR (Part 2; Orders in Council under s 2); transfer pricing (Part 4); UTPP (new Part 4A, ss 217A–217T, FA 2026); APAs (Part 5); hybrids (Part 6A); CFCs (Part 9A); CIR (Part 10, Sch 7A) |

Each year's Finance Act mostly amends these Acts, so the amended Acts are what you read. But some rules never move into the main Acts: the senior accounting officer, tax strategy and uncertain tax treatment rules still live in schedules to the Finance Acts that introduced them. A researcher who searches only the Corporation Tax Acts will never find them.

**Statutory instruments.** Beneath the Acts sit regulations made by ministers under powers the Acts give them. They carry the same force of law and often hold the detail that costs money. Five run through this book:

| SI | Short name | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| SI 1998/3175 Corporation Tax (Instalment Payments) Regulations 1998 | the instalment payment regulations | When large and very large companies pay (chapter 3) |
| SI 2004/3256 (the Disregard Regulations) | the Disregard Regulations | Keep certain exchange and hedging movements out of taxable profit for the time being (chapter 12) |
| SI 2004/3271 (change of accounting practice regulations) | the COAP regulations | Loan relationship and derivative transitions when a company changes its accounting framework (chapters 5, 12) |
| SI 2016/237 (country-by-country reporting) | the CbC regulations | €750m consolidated revenue threshold (reg 3) (chapters 1, 27) |
| SI 2023/818 Transfer Pricing Records Regulations 2023 | the TP records regulations | Master File and Local File for CT accounting periods beginning on or after 1 April 2023; UK-to-UK transactions excluded from the Local File (reg 6) (chapter 27) |

**Treaties** arrive by yet another route. TIOPA 2010 s 2 provides that arrangements specified in an Order in Council, declared to have been made with a view to affording relief from double taxation, have effect (in accordance with s 6); s 5(2) requires a draft of the Order to be approved by resolution of the House of Commons first. So a treaty is negotiated by governments, but it reaches a company's tax return only because Parliament has said it may.

## When Parliament points at Paris

For most of the history of transfer pricing in this country, the OECD Transfer Pricing Guidelines entered UK law through a narrow, fixed door. TIOPA 2010 s 164 told the reader to construe Part 4 consistently with "the transfer pricing guidelines". But the phrase meant one particular version, frozen in time, and a newer version counted only when the Treasury **designated it by order**. The July 2017 Guidelines were designated by SI 2018/266 for accounting periods beginning on or after 1 April 2018; the 2022 Guidelines by SI 2022/1147 for corporation tax accounting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2023 (both as summarised in the sources listed in the notes).

FA 2026 opened the door wide. For chargeable periods beginning on or after **1 January 2026**, UK law refers to the OECD's materials in three places, and in the two most important it refers to them **as amended or replaced from time to time**:

| Rule | Provision (FA 2026) | OECD material referred to | Dynamic? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transfer pricing | TIOPA 2010 s 164(3)–(4C), substituted by FA 2026 Sch 6 para 18 | Article 9 of the OECD Model Tax Convention approved by the OECD Council on **18 November 2025**; the **Transfer Pricing Guidelines 2022** (published 20 January 2022) | Yes: as amended or replaced from time to time, subject to UK reservations; Treasury may opt out of an update by regulations |
| Attribution of profits to a UK PE | CTA 2009 s 20(1A)–(1E), substituted by FA 2026 Sch 7 para 4 | Article 7(2) of the 18 November 2025 Model and its Commentary; the **2010 Report on the Attribution of Profits to Permanent Establishments** (22 July 2010); the TPG 2022; the March 2018 Additional Guidance | Yes: as amended from time to time, subject to UK reservations; Treasury regulation power |
| The PE definition | CTA 2010 s 1140A (new purpose clause), FA 2026 Sch 7 para 11 | Article 5 of the 18 November 2025 Model and its Commentary | Version named; whether the reference is dynamic was not confirmed (see notes) |

HMRC's International Manual describes the transfer pricing result plainly: the Model and the Guidelines are now interpretative aids **whether or not a treaty is in place**, and the references are to their most recent versions, with a Treasury power to opt out of specified updates (INTM414120).

Think of the statute as the rulebook and the OECD materials as the official commentary that the rulebook now tells the referee to read. The referee still applies the rulebook. But when a rule could bear two meanings, the referee must prefer the one that fits the commentary, and the commentary is updated in Paris.

Why would Parliament do this? Because transfer pricing and PE disputes are fought between tax authorities, under treaties, through mutual agreement procedures. A UK rule that drifted from the international reading would invite double taxation. The cost is a loss of control: Parliament no longer votes on each change of reading, so the Treasury's power to switch off an update is the safeguard. Chapter 23 owns the "authorised OECD approach" to PE attribution; chapter 27 owns transfer pricing.

### The misconception: "the OECD Guidelines are just guidance"

The misconception runs like this. The OECD cannot legislate for the UK; its documents call themselves guidelines and commentaries; so they are just guidance, like HMRC's manuals, to be quoted when helpful and ignored when not.

People believe it for good reasons. The OECD is not a parliament. Its Model is a template that each pair of countries adapts. And for most of the corporation tax code it remains true that nothing outside the statute and the cases binds you.

For transfer pricing it has not been true for a long time, because s 164 has long required consistency with the designated Guidelines. What FA 2026 adds is **reach and currency**: the PE rules now carry the same kind of instruction, and the transfer pricing reference follows the texts as they change. Ignoring them is not choosing a different view of the guidance; it is misreading the statute. An argument about the arm's length price of an intra-group loan, or about how much capital a branch should be treated as having, must now be built from two things together: the words of the Act and the OECD text the Act points to.

Two limits keep the picture honest. First, the OECD texts guide interpretation. They do not override clear statutory words, and the UK's reservations and any Treasury opt-out still apply. Second, the texts are long and they change. **This book could not open the OECD's own website** (oecd.org refused access during the research), so where it describes the Guidelines it relies on the statute and on HMRC's descriptions, and says so.

There is also an exam wrinkle. The examination screen gives you the OECD Model as a PDF of the **2017 articles**, while FA 2026 points to the version approved in 2025. In the exam, use what you are given, and use a supplied treaty extract in preference to either.

> **Going further: building an argument from statute plus OECD text.** A defensible technical position on a TP or PE point now has four layers, in this order: (1) the UK statute in force for the chargeable period (check commencement); (2) the OECD text the statute names, in the version it names, and any later version if the reference is dynamic, checking UK reservations and any Treasury opt-out regulations; (3) HMRC's manual, as the reading the inspector will put forward (INTM414000 onwards for the reformed TP rules); (4) case law, remembering that pre-2026 judgments were decided on older wording. A memo that skips layer 2 on a TP point is now incomplete as a matter of statutory construction, not just of persuasion.

> **Exam lens: sources and the OECD materials.**
> - **Grade:** DTTs, "application of OECD model and supplied extracts" **1 (core)**; transfer pricing and APAs **1 (core)** (2026 grid; check the 2027 grid when published). Note 2: knowledge of the Law, PR&E and Principles of Accounting manuals' terms is expected.
> - **Past appearances:** N23 Q1 (20 marks: residence with a **supplied treaty tie-breaker extract**; examiners: some "ignored the supplied treaty article and used the OECD Model text instead"); N23 Q6 (20 marks: TP methods from the OECD Guidelines; theory good, functional analysis weak).
> - **Style:** source-awareness is never asked for as such; it earns marks indirectly, by citing the right statute and applying the right version.
> - **Traps:** using the OECD Model PDF where an extract is supplied; quoting HMRC guidance as if it were law; applying FA 2026 rules to a period that began before 1 January 2026.

## Treaties, and the judges who read them

A double tax treaty is an agreement between two states. In the main it allocates taxing rights and limits what each state may take; it is not a free-standing source of tax charges. Its words were negotiated by officials, usually following the OECD Model, and they are read differently from a UK statute. A treaty is meant to mean the same thing in both countries, so its words take an international meaning rather than a purely domestic one.

The leading modern authority is *Fowler v HMRC* [2020] UKSC 22, decided by the Supreme Court on **20 May 2020**. Mr Fowler was a diver resident in South Africa who worked in the UK sector of the continental shelf in 2011/12 and 2012/13. UK domestic law (ITTOIA 2005 s 15) deems an employed seabed diver to be carrying on a trade. Mr Fowler argued that the deeming carried through into the UK–South Africa treaty, so that his income fell under the business profits article, which South Africa alone could tax. The Supreme Court unanimously disagreed. Lord Briggs, giving the judgment, held that the domestic deeming rule did not change which treaty article applied; treaty words take their ordinary international meaning, and the income fell under the employment article, so the UK could tax it.

On the OECD Commentaries, Lord Briggs said they carry persuasive force "as the cogency of their reasoning deserves", even where a Commentary post-dates the treaty. That describes the general position for treaties. FA 2026 goes further for transfer pricing and PEs, because there the statute itself directs you to the OECD texts.

Treaties also change in bulk now. The **Multilateral Instrument** (MLI), in force for the UK from **1 October 2018**, modifies many treaties at once, and HMRC publishes synthesised texts showing how each covered treaty reads after the modification. The UK–Ireland synthesised text is an example: it applies, among others, MLI Art 7, the **principal purpose test**, which denies a treaty benefit where "it is reasonable to conclude ... that obtaining that benefit was one of the principal purposes of any arrangement or transaction", unless granting it would accord with the object and purpose of the treaty. So a tax function reading a treaty asks three questions: which text applies after the MLI; what its words ordinarily mean in context; and whether a purpose test could take the benefit away. Chapter 24 owns treaties in depth.

## The accounts as a source of tax numbers

The largest source of numbers in a corporation tax computation was not written by Parliament at all. It was written by accounting standard setters.

CTA 2009 s 46(1): the profits of a trade "must be calculated in accordance with generally accepted accounting practice, subject to any adjustment required or authorised by law". The loan relationship and derivative rules go further: the amounts brought into account are, in general, those recognised in determining profit or loss under GAAP (CTA 2009 ss 307–308; ss 595, 597). CTA 2010 s 1127 defines GAAP to cover both UK GAAP and international accounting standards. In practice that means the Financial Reporting Council's standards, chiefly FRS 102 and the reduced disclosure framework in FRS 101, or full IFRS for listed group accounts (HMRC BIM31020; CFM20030).

Why build tax on accounts? Because they exist already, they are audited, and they follow a consistent logic from year to year. A second, tax-only set of books for every company in a group would be costly and easy to manipulate. So the law starts from the accounts and then adjusts them where policy demands: capital allowances replace depreciation; entertaining is disallowed.

The courts have generally held to this line. In *HMRC v NCL Investments Ltd* [2022] UKSC 9, HMRC argued that IFRS 2 debits for employee share options should be disallowed; it lost on every ground, and the debits followed the accounts. The line has limits. In *GDF Suez Teesside Ltd v HMRC* [2018] EWCA Civ 2075, the old FA 1996 requirement that amounts "fairly represent" the profits required a credit that GAAP-compliant accounts did not show.

The consequence is easy to miss: when a standard changes, taxable profit can change without any Finance Act. ICAEW commentary (23 March 2026) notes that the FRS 102 periodic review, effective for periods beginning on or after 1 January 2026, brings most leases onto the balance sheet and that the net transition amount generally falls into the first period on the new basis, which can push companies into instalments for the first time [secondary]. A tax function therefore reads accounting standard updates as closely as Finance Bills. Chapter 5 owns the detail (s 46, change of basis under ss 180–187, leases); chapter 6 owns the tax charge in the accounts.

> **Exam lens: accounts as a source.** **Grade:** "Impact of accounting standards on taxable profits" **1 (core)**; the Prospectus expects "an understanding of current accounting issues relevant to tax such as deferred tax, profit recognition and share schemes". **Past appearances:** tax-accounting tails in M24 Q2(b), M25 Q2(b), N25 Q1(b) and a 10-mark deferred tax question in M26 Q6. **Trap:** forgetting that a non-tax accounting change (a framework move or a revised standard) can create a taxable adjustment.

## HMRC's view, and asking first

HMRC publishes thousands of pages of manuals. For a larger company the main ones are:

| Manual | Code | Typical use in this book |
|---|---|---|
| International Manual | INTM | Treaties (INTM153000 series), DTR (INTM164000 series), CFCs, branch exemption, transfer pricing (reformed guidance from INTM414000) |
| Corporate Finance Manual | CFM | Loan relationships, derivatives, CIR background (CFM95110–95130) |
| Company Taxation Manual | CTM | Returns, instalments (CTM92520), losses, change of ownership |
| Corporate Intangibles Research and Development Manual | CIRD | Intangibles, R&D, CIR guidance |
| Capital Gains Manual | CG | Company gains, groups, SSE |
| Business Income Manual | BIM | Trading profits, GAAP (BIM31020, BIM31095) |
| Business Leasing Manual | BLM | Leasing |
| Senior Accounting Officers Guidance | SAOG | SAO duties and penalties |

Around them sit policy papers, consultation outcomes and tax information and impact notes (TIINs), which explain why a measure was introduced.

Their status is simple to state. **A manual is HMRC's view of the law. It is not law.** A tribunal is not bound by it, and where it conflicts with the statute, the statute wins. Manuals also date: some INTM transfer pricing pages still carry a note that the rules were reformed by Finance Bill 2025-26 and point to draft guidance at INTM414000 for periods from 1 January 2026.

Yet for a larger company HMRC's view has a sharper edge. Under the uncertain tax treatment rules (FA 2022 Sch 17), a qualifying company whose treatment relies on an interpretation **not in accordance with HMRC's known position** (published guidance or dealings with HMRC), with a tax advantage over **£5m**, must notify HMRC (paras 10, 11, 18). Chapter 4 tells that story. So the manuals are not law, but departing from them can create a legal duty to say so.

The practical lesson: read the manual as the argument your inspector will make, test it against the statute and the cases, and record why you agree or disagree.

**Asking first.** Sometimes the best source of law is an answer from HMRC in advance. There are three main routes, plus APAs for transfer pricing.

| Route | Legal basis | When | What you get |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Statutory clearance** | In the Act concerned: e.g. demergers CTA 2010 s 1091; share exchanges and reconstructions TCGA 1992 s 138; transactions in securities CTA 2010 Part 15 (30 days for HMRC to respond; 30 more after any request for information) | Transactions where the law turns on purpose | Binding on HMRC on the facts disclosed |
| **Non-Statutory Clearance Service** | HMRC guidance | **Genuine uncertainty** about how the law applies to a specific transaction | Written confirmation of HMRC's view; HMRC will not act as a "confirmation service", advise on tax planning or clear arrangements designed to avoid tax; a statutory route takes precedence |
| **Advance tax certainty for major projects** | **FA 2026 ss 266–274** | A qualifying investment project expected to involve at least **£1bn** of UK expenditure over its life | Clearance binding on HMRC for up to **5 years**, subject to unchanged facts and law; service opened **1 July 2026**; published scope excludes transfer pricing, valuations, purpose tests and the GAAR [launch date and exclusions: secondary sources] |
| **Advance pricing agreement** | TIOPA 2010 Part 5 (ss 218 onwards) | Transfer pricing, PE attribution | The Tax Acts apply in accordance with the agreement for covered periods (chapter 27) |

> **Going further: when to ask.** A clearance costs time, discloses your thinking and fixes the facts you must then follow. Not asking leaves the risk on the balance sheet and, for a large business, may trigger UTT if the position departs from HMRC's known view. Good practice in a tax function: keep a clearance log; build the statutory clearance timetable into the deal timetable (a demerger or share exchange cannot sensibly complete before clearance); use the non-statutory route only where you can show genuine uncertainty; and use an APA where a recurring transfer pricing question carries real money (chapter 27, Tarnmoor's bilateral APA in GY6).

## Case law, and the echo of Europe

The tribunal ladder is as *The Living Law*, chapter 2, described it: First-tier Tribunal (Tax Chamber), Upper Tribunal (Tax and Chancery Chamber), Court of Appeal, Supreme Court. A First-tier decision binds no later tribunal, though it can persuade; the Upper Tribunal and the courts above it make precedent.

Since **April 2022** the National Archives has published judgments free of charge through **Find Case Law** (caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk), searchable by neutral citation, party and judge. Most of the corporate cases in this book, from *BlackRock HoldCo 5, LLC v HMRC* [2024] EWCA Civ 330 in the prologue to the unallowable purpose cases in chapter 12, can be read there in full, and so can First-tier decisions such as *DSG Retail Ltd v HMRC* [2009] UKFTT 31 (TC). Read the judgment, not a summary. Two habits help: note the court (a Court of Appeal judgment outranks a First-tier decision however recent), and check what happened next (a pending appeal, such as *Syngenta Holdings Ltd v HMRC*, where the Upper Tribunal granted permission in [2025] UKUT 338 (TCC), can change the answer).

**The echo of Europe.** While the UK was a member state, the Court of Justice tested UK tax rules against freedom of establishment. In *Cadbury Schweppes plc v IRC* (Case C-196/04), judgment 12 September 2006, it held that CFC-type rules could be justified only against "wholly artificial arrangements". *Marks and Spencer*, the cross-border group relief litigation that *The Living Law*, chapter 24, described, shaped the rules on foreign losses. The present CFC regime (TIOPA 2010 Part 9A, from 2012) was built partly in answer to that case law, and its finance company exemption then spent years in a State aid dispute: Commission decision 2 April 2019; annulled by the Court of Justice on 19 September 2024 [dates from secondary sources]; SI 2024/1307 and FA 2026 s 51 dealt with the repayments.

Brexit has not erased that history. Since the end of 2023 the principle of the supremacy of EU law is not part of UK domestic law, and what was "retained EU law" is now "assimilated law" (Retained EU Law (Revocation and Reform) Act 2023). But the rules shaped by those judgments remain on the statute book, and the judgments still explain why they look as they do. Chapters 22, 25 and 26 return to them.

## Reading the law on the right day

A section of TIOPA 2010 does not have one meaning. It has a meaning on a date, and the online text you read may not be the text that applies to your client's period.

The free official source is **legislation.gov.uk**. Each page carries a **"Changes to legislation"** box stating the date up to which the text is up to date with changes known to be in force, and listing effects the editorial team has not yet applied ("outstanding" or "changes not yet applied"). When that box lists outstanding effects, the text on screen may be out of date. The site also keeps **point-in-time** versions: you can read CTA 2010 s 1141 as it stood on 31 December 2025 (old dependent agent test: an agent who "has and habitually exercises there authority to do business on behalf of the company") beside the current version (a person who "habitually concludes contracts, or habitually plays the principal role leading to the conclusion of contracts..."). Pending effects are not shown on point-in-time views.

The site also holds each Act **as enacted** (add `/enacted` to the URL, e.g. FA 2026 is `ukpga/2026/11`). The revised text shows the law as amended; the enacted text shows what the amending Act did and from when. Commencement rules often sit at the end of a Schedule: for the UK-to-UK exemption, FA 2026 Sch 6 para 32(1).

**Commencement in practice (real-calendar illustration, not the Tarnmoor story).** Most of FA 2026's international changes (PE definition and attribution, transfer pricing, UTPP) apply to chargeable periods **beginning on or after 1 January 2026**:

| Company's accounting period | FA 2026 PE / TP / UTPP rules? |
|---|---|
| 1 January 2026 – 31 December 2026 | Yes, for the whole period |
| 1 April 2025 – 31 March 2026 | No: old rules (and DPT) for the whole period; new rules from the AP beginning 1 April 2026 |
| 1 October 2025 – 30 September 2026 | No: old rules for the whole period, although most of it falls after 1 January 2026 |

So the same section, read on the same day, can give different answers to three companies. Ask three questions every time: which version applies to this period; has the site applied every change; and what does the Act as enacted say about commencement?

> **Going further: coming next.** The draft Finance Bill 2026-27 (published 13 July 2026) includes reform of the foreign PE exemption, including a further amendment to CTA 2010 s 1140A for accounting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2027 in the draft legislation. This is **proposed, not law**, and not examinable on FA 2026.

## Tom Hesketh's question

*Our invented group, Tarnmoor, the listed UK engineering group introduced in chapter 1. Every company and person in it is invented.*

In the first weeks of GY1, Tarnmoor's group head of tax, Tom Hesketh, receives a question from the CFO, Nadia Kerr.

The group's treasury company, Tarnmoor Finance Ltd (TFL), has lent £430m to four UK group companies, all at 6%. It has also lent £120m to Tarnmoor Vallaria SA (TVS) in the invented country of Vallaria. And Tarnmoor plc has lent £20m, interest free, to Tarnmoor Ireland Ltd (TIL), which is incorporated in Ireland but, on our invented facts, centrally managed and controlled from Leeds and so UK resident.

Tarnmoor's consolidated revenue (GY1 £1,180m, about €1,357m at the story's assumed €1.15 per £) is far above €750m, so its UK companies already keep a Master File and Local File under SI 2023/818, which already leaves UK-to-UK transactions out of the Local File (reg 6). Nadia's question goes further: does the pricing of the UK loans still have to be tested at all? Tom does not answer from memory, because the law on this point was rewritten by FA 2026. He sets his team a research memo, and it follows the order of this chapter.

**Why the question arises.** The transfer pricing rules (then ICTA 1988 Sch 28AA) originally applied only to transactions between a UK and an overseas person. FA 2004 (ss 30–37) extended transfer pricing and thin capitalisation to UK-to-UK transactions for chargeable periods beginning on or after **1 April 2004** (INTM413250). The change followed the Court of Justice's judgment in *Lankhorst-Hohorst* (2002), which held that national thin capitalisation rules could in principle breach freedom of establishment; the usual explanation is that the UK chose to apply its rules at home too, **an explanation, not something the statute says**. Two decades later the Corporate Tax Roadmap (October 2024, para 2.48) said that "the potential removal of UK-to-UK transfer pricing ... would reduce the UK compliance burden". FA 2026 did it.

> TFL's lending: £550m in total (matching its £550m 5.5% notes, interest £30.25m), of which £430m (78%) is UK-to-UK.
>
> **Illustration: why the old rule protected nothing (labelled hypothetical, not a story fact).** Suppose an arm's length rate for TEL's loan had been 5.5% rather than 6%.
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Excess interest: £200m × (6.0% − 5.5%) | 1,000,000 |
> | TEL: deduction disallowed under s 147(3); extra CT at 25% | 250,000 |
> | TFL: compensating adjustment claim (s 174), interest income reduced; CT saved at 25% | (250,000) |
> | Net effect on the group's UK tax | nil |
>
> **Conclusion (in our invented case).** No TP adjustment on the four UK loans; the Vallarian loan stays fully within Part 4; the TIL loan qualifies only while TIL remains UK resident. The exemption switches off one rule, not the code: unallowable purpose (CTA 2009 s 441, chapter 12) and the corporate interest restriction (chapter 28) still apply to every one of these loans. The memo feeds chapters 12 and 27.

> **Going further: the tax function's tasks.** (1) Record the s 164A analysis for each UK-to-UK provision and re-test it each period (a change of residence, a company becoming a bank or a QAHC, or a patent box election can take a provision out). (2) Decide whether any election out is wanted: advisers have noted that groups may sometimes prefer arm's length pricing where other rules depend on it [secondary, RSM]. (3) Keep the cross-border pricing (TVS) and its Master File and Local File in order. (4) Remember that the CIR and Pillar Two computations, not transfer pricing, now carry most of the weight on intra-UK financing.

> **Exam lens: s 164A.** **Grade:** within transfer pricing **1 (core)**. **Past appearances:** none yet (new in FA 2026; first examinable in 2027 sittings on FA 2026). **Likely angle:** a UK group with UK and overseas subsidiaries where candidates must say which intra-group loans need TP (cross-border, and UK-to-UK only if a condition fails) before computing a CIR. **Traps:** applying s 164A to a cross-border loan or to a CFC (s 371SD(5A)); forgetting that the SME exemption (s 166) is a different rule; assuming the exemption also switches off s 441 or CIR.

## Research in the exam room

On the day of the exam, the sources shrink. The paper is closed book, sat on **RM Assessment Master** (replacing Exam4 from the October 2026 sitting). Behind the "open book" icon you will find: the **tax tables** (also on your desk in hard copy); the **OECD Model Convention** as a PDF of the 2017 articles, available only on screen; and links to **Croner-i or Tolley** student legislation, which you must buy in advance (work subscriptions will not log in).

You cannot make notes in either legislation product. You may highlight, and you may tag: a tag may carry a section number or a topic name (the CIOT's example: "Corporate rescue exception = CTA 2009 (s 361D)"), but not a pro forma or formula, and invigilators may spot-check.

Practise with the product you will use on the day: each publisher lays out the Acts and its search differently.

The examiners have also told candidates how to use treaty material. After N23 they reported that some candidates ignored the supplied treaty article and used the OECD Model instead. When a question supplies an extract, the extract is the treaty for that question. Use the Model only when nothing else is given, and quote the article number so the marker can follow you.

The exam does not test your ability to find an obscure manual page, and it rewards statute over guidance. It does test whether you know which rule applies to which period, so state the law year you are applying. For a 2027 sitting that will almost certainly be FA 2026 (FY2026), and the 2027 tax tables, when the CIOT publishes them (not yet published at 9 October 2026), will show it.

> **Exam lens: research under exam conditions.**
> - **Resources:** tax tables (paper and PDF), OECD Model (2017 articles, PDF only), Croner-i or Tolley online legislation; nothing else; no watches; two sheets of note paper (not marked).
> - **The spreadsheet rule:** "Calculations left in the spreadsheet and not copied to the answer box will not be marked" (Candidate Instructions, October 2026); examiners cannot see formulae.
> - **Rubric:** "All workings should be shown and made to the nearest month and pound unless the question specifies otherwise."
> - **Style:** no letter, report or email format in LCG AT; technical prose and computations; 10, 15 or 20-mark questions; 0.5–1 mark per point.
> - **Traps:** citing a manual as authority; using the Model over a supplied extract; citing pre-FA 2026 PE sections (old ss 22–32 CTA 2009 are omitted for periods from 1 January 2026). Chapter 32 owns exam craft in full.

## What to take away

Corporate tax law lives in several places at once, and a tax function needs to know which kind of source it is holding.

- **Statute first.** CTA 2009 computes profits; CTA 2010 gives reliefs and structure; TIOPA 2010 holds DTR, TP, CFCs, hybrids and CIR; TCGA 1992, CAA 2001, TMA 1970 and FA 1998 Sch 18 complete the core, with free-standing schedules for SAO, tax strategy and UTT. SIs carry equal force; treaties take effect by Order in Council under TIOPA 2010 s 2.
- **OECD materials in statute (FY2026).** TIOPA 2010 s 164 (TP), CTA 2009 s 20(1A)–(1E) (PE attribution) and CTA 2010 s 1140A (PE definition) refer to the Model and Commentary approved on 18 November 2025 and the TPG 2022; for TP and attribution, as amended from time to time; chargeable periods beginning on or after 1 January 2026. There the Guidelines are not "just guidance". Elsewhere, *Fowler*: Commentaries persuade "as the cogency of their reasoning deserves".
- **Accounts** supply most of the numbers (CTA 2009 s 46; ss 307–308), so a change of accounting standard can change taxable profit without a Finance Act.
- **HMRC's manuals** are its view, not law; but departing from its known position can trigger a UTT notification above £5m.
- **Certainty in advance:** statutory clearance where one exists; the non-statutory service only for genuine uncertainty; the advance tax certainty service for projects of at least £1bn (binding up to 5 years); an APA for TP.
- **Read the law on the right day:** "Changes to legislation" box; point-in-time versions; commencement words applied precisely.
- **In our invented case,** Tarnmoor's four UK loans (£430m at 6%) fall within the UK-to-UK exemption in s 164A; the Vallarian loan stays inside transfer pricing.

The next chapter turns from where the law lives to how a large group lives with it: returns, payments and enquiries.

# Chapter twenty: Buying and selling companies

On 31 August 2023 the Upper Tribunal decided a case that turned on one missing month. The company was **M Group Holdings Ltd**. It had run a trade itself, as a single company with no subsidiaries.

On 29 June 2015 it formed a subsidiary, Medinet Clinical Services Ltd. On 30 September 2015 it moved its trade and assets into that subsidiary. On 27 May 2016 it sold the subsidiary to an outside buyer, just under eleven months after the subsidiary was born. The reported gain was about £53m.

The company claimed the substantial shareholding exemption (SSE), which needs a holding of at least twelve months. It relied on the hive-down rule in TCGA 1992 Sch 7AC para 15A, which lets a seller count time when the business's assets were used in the trade of another member of its group. The First-tier Tribunal said no. The Upper Tribunal (Michael Green J and Judge Phyllis Ramshaw) agreed: before the subsidiary existed there was no group, because a group requires more than one company. The missing month could not be borrowed (*M Group Holdings Ltd v HMRC* [2023] UKUT 213 (TCC)).

Some years into our invented story, Tarnmoor Engineering Ltd (TEL) does something that looks almost identical. Tarnmoor, its companies, its people and its numbers are invented for teaching, as they are throughout this book. On **1 February GY6** TEL moves its actuators business into a new subsidiary, Tarnmoor Actuators Ltd (TAL). On **31 December GY6**, eleven months later, it sells TAL to an American buyer. Same gap. Different answer. By the end of this chapter you will see why.

This is the second last chapter of Part Three. It owns no single row of the LCG grid. Instead it applies a dozen **core (grade 1)** rows at once, the way the examiner does: the SSE, companies leaving groups, change in company ownership, value shifting, depreciatory transactions, *Marren v Ingles*, CT self-assessment, the senior accounting officer and transfer pricing. Stamp duty and SDLT (**awareness, grade 3**) run alongside. It builds on *The Living Law* (TKS), chapters 23 (company gains) and 24 (groups).

Statutes: Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act 1992 (TCGA 1992), Corporation Tax Act 2009 (CTA 2009), Corporation Tax Act 2010 (CTA 2010), Finance Act 1986 (FA 1986, stamp duty) and Finance Act 2003 Part 4 (SDLT). We apply the law for FY2026. FA 2026 made no change to the SSE, s 171, s 179, Part 14 or SDLT Sch 7; its changes to s 137 and s 139 (chapters 18 and 19) and to transfer pricing (chapter 27) are signposted where they matter.

## Two ways to buy a business

A business can change hands in two ways. The buyer can buy the **company** that owns the business, or it can buy the business's **assets** out of the company. The commercial result can look the same. The tax result rarely is.

Think of it as buying a house. Buy the house with everything in it, and you get the previous owner's furniture, the unpaid bills in the drawer and the quarrel with the neighbours. Buy only the furniture you choose, and you leave the drawer and the neighbours behind. A share purchase is the first; an asset purchase is the second.

**The buyer.** On a share purchase nothing inside the company changes for tax: its assets keep their base costs, tax written down values and pools. The buyer gets a base cost for the shares and nothing else, and *The Living Law*, chapter 23, explained why that cost will probably never matter: a later sale of a trading subsidiary is likely to fall within the SSE.

On an asset purchase each asset gets a fresh tax history at the price paid: a **step-up**.

| Asset | Buyer's tax result on an asset purchase | Authority (chapter) |
|---|---|---|
| Plant and machinery | Qualifying expenditure at the price (second-hand: no full expensing; AIA/WDAs); s 198 election for fixtures | CAA 2001 (8, 9) |
| Intangibles (IP, customer relationships) | Part 8 cost; debits as amortised, or 4% fixed-rate election | CTA 2009 Part 8 (11) |
| Goodwill | 6.5% only with qualifying IP, capped at 6 × IP expenditure | CTA 2009 ss 879A ff (11) |
| Land and buildings | New base cost for gains; SBA continues on the original qualifying expenditure with an allowance statement | TCGA 1992; CAA 2001 Part 2A (9, 16) |

The asset buyer also **leaves the history behind**: unpaid tax, open enquiries, doubtful claims and losses stay with the seller. The share buyer takes all of it.

Two more differences decide many deals. **Stamp taxes**: shares cost the buyer stamp duty at 0.5%; land bought directly costs SDLT at up to 5% on non-residential property (chapter 21). **VAT**: an asset deal is usually a transfer of a going concern, and a company joining a group may join its VAT group (signpost only: VAT is not on the LCG paper).

> **Misconception: "a share sale is always better for the seller."** Often it is, but only if the SSE applies, and only if the seller does not give the advantage back in the price. The Tarnmoor Actuators sale below shows both halves.

## The seller's side of the table

A company selling a trading subsidiary usually wants to sell **shares**. If the SSE conditions are met (chapter 18 owns them), the gain is not a chargeable gain and a loss is not allowable. Nothing is claimed. In outline: at least 10% of the shares (and of profits and assets) held for a continuous 12 months in the 6 years before the sale (Sch 7AC paras 7–8), with the investee a trading company or holding company of a trading group (para 19).

An **asset sale** is different for the selling company:

| Asset | Seller's tax result on an asset sale |
|---|---|
| Land and buildings | Chargeable gain (indexation frozen at December 2017); roll-over relief possible |
| Plant | Balancing charge if proceeds exceed TWDV; **special balancing charge** on full-expensed plant (CAA 2001 ss 59A–59B, chapter 8) |
| Home-grown intangibles | Realisation credit taxed as **income**, nearly the whole price (chapter 11); the SSE never protects an asset sale |
| Cash left in the selling company | Must be paid up as a dividend: exempt for a corporate parent (CTA 2009 Part 9A); a second layer of tax for an individual owner |

So why would a seller accept an asset deal? Because the buyer pays for what it gets. A buyer who loses a step-up worth, say, £1m of future tax wants some of it back in the price; a buyer who fears hidden liabilities wants indemnities, a retention or a lower price. **Price settles the difference**, and it is often the largest number in the negotiation.

The CIOT's own description of the paper asks candidates to plan for "corporate transformations", and the 2028 handbook's key principles name "share versus asset sale" as a topic in its own right (CIOT LCG syllabus page; CTA Handbook 2028).

## What comes with the shares

The share buyer inherits the company's whole tax history. Five parts of it matter most at this level, and each changes what the buyer should pay.

1. **Losses** (chapter 14). A change in ownership (CTA 2010 s 719: more than half the ordinary share capital) plus a major change in the nature or conduct of the trade within any 5-year period beginning up to 3 years before it cancels carried-forward trading losses (Part 14 Ch 2, ss 673–674). Even without a change, **Ch 2C** bars Part 5A surrender of pre-acquisition losses for 5 years. Pre-entry capital losses are ring-fenced by TCGA 1992 Sch 7A (chapter 17).
2. **Base costs and pools** stay with the company, so latent gains come too.
3. **Intangibles.** Shares are excluded assets (CTA 2009 Part 8 Ch 10): the goodwill and customer relationships inside the target never become the buyer's IFAs. In the consolidated accounts they appear at fair value with no tax base: a deferred tax liability. **In our invented case**, Calder's customer relationships were recognised at **£4.0m**, DTL **£1.0m** (chapter 6). The CT return sees none of it.
4. **Secondary liabilities.** Under TCGA 1992 s 190 HMRC can recover unpaid CT on a group company's gain from the principal company or a group member that owned the asset in the previous 12 months (51% group; notice within 3 years). Under CTA 2010 Part 14 Ch 6, pre-change CT unpaid after a change in ownership can be collected from linked persons. An SDLT group relief claimed by the target can be clawed back from the target when it leaves its old group (FA 2003 Sch 7 para 3).
5. **Status.** Nothing resets on completion: instalment history, R&D claims, transfer pricing positions, returns and enquiries all continue.

## Due diligence

**Due diligence** is the buyer's investigation of the target before it signs. The tax part finds every exposure the buyer would inherit, values it and decides how the contract should deal with it; it also finds the tax assets, so the buyer knows what it is really buying.

| Area | What the tax team checks |
|---|---|
| Compliance | Returns filed on time; QIPs paid when due (large/very large status); penalties and interest; late-filing history (a third successive late return costs £1,000/£2,000) |
| Disputes | Open enquiries; discovery windows (4/6/20 years); clearances relied on |
| Losses | By type and period (pre- or post-April 2017); Part 14 analysis against the buyer's integration plan; pre-entry capital losses |
| Capital allowances | Pools; fixtures supported by s 198/199 elections; SBA allowance statements; special balancing charge exposure |
| Intangibles | Tax WDVs; pre-2002 assets; any degrouping exposure on assets received intra-group within 6 years |
| Groups | Assets received at no gain, no loss within 6 years (s 179); SDLT group relief within 3 years; s 190 exposure |
| International | Transfer pricing; withholding on interest and royalties; CFCs; PEs |
| Finance | CIR position; loan relationships with connected parties; debt bought at a discount |
| R&D | Claims and notifications; linked-enterprise status after completion |
| Governance | SAO; tax strategy; UTT notifications; DOTAS or other schemes used or offered; CCO procedures |
| Other taxes | Employment taxes, VAT, stamp taxes |

Findings are sorted: some into the **price**, some into an **indemnity**, some into a **condition** to be fixed before completion, some simply noted.

> **Worked example 20.1: Brackenwell's diligence (invented)**
>
> Tarnmoor plc (TPLC) bought Brackenwell Sensors Ltd (BSL) on **1 July GY2**. The diligence found:
>
> | Finding | Response |
> |---|---|
> | Return for the year to 31 December GY1 unfiled; it carries an ERIS payable credit claim of **£943,950** (chapter 10) | Price and tax deed (below) |
> | Two earlier returns late | Filing plan: TPLC's team files well before 31 December GY2 to avoid a third successive failure (chapter 3) |
> | £8.6m of losses; Tarnmoor's integration plan makes BSL a captive supplier to Calder and TEL | Valued at **nil**: Part 14 Ch 2 (chapter 14) |
> | Royalty of £50,000 a year to a Vallarian university (Vallaria is invented) with tax deducted at the treaty rate of 5% | Treaty and reasonable-belief paperwork checked (ITA 2007 ss 906, 911; chapter 23) |
>
> **The missed point.** On HMRC's view (CIRD92000) a company taken over by a large group is large for R&D purposes for the **whole** AP of the takeover. Ending BSL's AP at completion could have kept January–June GY2 under ERIS. Nobody thought of it in time (chapter 10). R&D status belongs on the list before signing.

## Warranties, indemnities and the tax deed

The contract is the **share purchase agreement** (SPA). Its tax protection comes in two forms.

| | Warranty | Indemnity (tax deed or tax covenant) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | A statement of fact about the company ("all returns filed on time"; "no enquiry open") | A promise to pay, pound for pound, any tax of the company arising from pre-completion events |
| Buyer's remedy | Damages for breach: must prove loss in the value of the shares | Payment of the tax amount: no need to prove loss in value |
| Disclosure | Matters fairly disclosed cannot found a claim | Not cut down by disclosure |
| Main function | Forces the seller to disclose | Allocates the cost of pre-completion tax |
| Seller's usual limits | Time limits, caps, de minimis | Time limit, cap, exclusions for tax provided in the accounts or arising from the buyer's post-completion acts, conduct of disputes with HMRC |

**Tax treatment of the payments.** Tax deeds usually say that any payment is treated as a reduction in the price, and the gains rules broadly follow:

- **Seller:** TCGA 1992 s 49(1)(c): a contingent liability under a warranty is ignored at first; if enforced, the gain is recomputed on a claim as if the consideration had been reduced (CG14805, CG14815). **ESC D33** (para 13) applies the same treatment in practice to payments under an **indemnity** (CG14825). For an SSE-exempt seller the adjustment changes nothing.
- **Buyer:** a payment received is in principle a capital sum derived from an asset (s 22), but in practice it reduces the base cost of the shares (CG13010; ESC D33).
- **Gross-up:** if the buyer is taxed on a payment anyway, the seller pays more to cover the tax.

> **Worked example 20.2: Brackenwell's pending credit (invented)**
>
> The £943,950 ERIS credit related to BSL's year as an independent company but would arrive after completion, in a company TPLC owned. **In our invented case** the price of **£22.2m** assumed the credit would be paid in full, and the tax deed made the sellers liable to repay TPLC if HMRC reduced it after an enquiry. The sellers were paid for the credit once; the buyer bore no risk that it might shrink.

The logic runs through every deal in this chapter: tax that belongs to the seller's years is the seller's, and the contract makes sure the buyer can recover it whoever HMRC sends the bill to.

## The costs of buying

A larger acquisition produces a pile of invoices, each taking a different route. **M23 Q6** gave 10 marks to nothing else.

**Advisers' fees** (chapter 13). In *Centrica Overseas Holdings Ltd v HMRC* [2024] UKSC 25 the Supreme Court held that fees for selling a subsidiary, although expenses of management, were capital and so excluded by CTA 2009 s 1219(3)(a). **HMRC's view** (CTM08190, CTM08260) is that appraising possible investments is revenue, but costs become acquisition costs, at the earliest, at the decision to acquire a particular target.

**Finance costs** are loan relationship debits relieved as the accounts recognise them (CTA 2009 ss 306A, 307; chapter 12), never management expenses (s 1219(3)(b)). The M23 examiners noted that some candidates missed exactly this.

**Stamp duty** is part of the cost of the shares (0.5%, rounded up to the nearest £5). The loan capital exemption (FA 1986 s 79(4)) does **not** apply to loan capital carrying a right of conversion into shares (s 79(5)); HMRC's manual confirms that transfers of convertible notes are chargeable (STSM021230, STSM041060).

> **Worked example 20.3: Tarnmoor's acquisition costs (invented; £)**
>
> | Item | Deal | Treatment | Authority | £ |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Strategic review before the 3 February GY1 board decision | Calder | Management expense (TPLC) | CTA 2009 s 1219; CTM08260 | 300,000 |
> | Due diligence, SPA legal, corporate finance after the decision | Calder | Capital: no deduction | s 1219(3)(a); *Centrica* | 900,000 |
> | RCF arrangement fee 1 | Calder | Loan relationship debit as amortised (£250,000 GY1; £50,000 GY2) | CTA 2009 ss 306A, 307 | 300,000 |
> | Stamp duty: £32.0m × 0.5% | Calder | Cost of shares | FA 1999 Sch 13 (stamp duty on a transfer on sale) | 160,000 |
> | Stamp duty: £22.2m × 0.5% | Brackenwell shares | Cost of shares | as above | 111,000 |
> | Stamp duty: £1.8m × 0.5% on the £3.0m convertible notes bought from the venture investors | Brackenwell notes | Chargeable: conversion right defeats the loan capital exemption | FA 1986 s 79(5) | 9,000 |
> | **Stamp duty paid by TPLC on the two acquisitions** | | | | **280,000** |
>
> The £9,000 on the notes is a fact this chapter adds to the story.

**Payments to people.**

- **Compensation for loss of office** paid by the target to a departing director is deductible only if incurred wholly and exclusively for the target's trade (CTA 2009 s 54). **HMRC's view** (BIM38380–BIM38390; BIM42955) is that the purpose test is unlikely to be met where the payment is connected with the sale of the business or a change in the controlling shareholding; the SPA, the compensation agreement and negotiation correspondence are evidence of purpose. Amounts within a statutory or contractual entitlement are easier to defend than ex gratia sums.
- **Retention bonuses** paid by the target to keep key staff after completion are an ordinary employment cost of its trade, subject to the 9-month rule for unpaid remuneration (CTA 2009 s 1288: if not paid within 9 months after the end of the period of account, deductible only when paid). If the **acquiring holding company** pays instead, it is paying for services to another company and may get no deduction. Who pays matters as much as what is paid. (Jess Hartley's retention bonus in chapter 31 is paid by Ridgeway Cycles.)

> **Exam lens: acquisition costs and share v asset**
>
> - **Grade:** companies with investment business **1**; loan relationships **1**; employment costs **1**; stamp duty on shares **3**. "Share versus asset sale" is named in the 2028 key principles; in the 2026 grid it sits within the SSE, gains and CA rows.
> - **Past appearances:** **M23 Q6** (10 marks: due diligence, legal fees, a loan arrangement fee, compensation for loss of office, retention bonuses; generally good; some missed the loan relationship treatment of the fee); M24 Q1 (capital costs of a share sale in an investment company).
> - **Style:** a list of costs, each with a treatment and an authority: 0.5–1 mark each. State the decision date if the question gives one.
> - **Traps:** deducting post-decision deal costs; treating finance fees as management expenses; deducting stamp duty; assuming loan notes are always exempt from stamp duty; deducting change-of-control compensation without discussing purpose.

## The day a company joins

When a company joins a large group almost every compliance setting changes. **N24 Q5** (20 marks) turned that into a question: a US multinational bought a UK SME and candidates had to explain its new obligations. The marking guide gave **4** marks to filing and instalments, **3** to CbC reporting, **6.5** to transfer pricing (now applying because the company is no longer an SME; master and local file), **3.5** to CIR (probably no restriction, but file to protect the position; reporting company; abbreviated return) and **3** to stamp duty on the share purchase (0.5%, 30 days, non-residents must pay).

| Setting | What changes on joining | Authority (chapter) |
|---|---|---|
| Associated companies: marginal relief | Associated **at any time** in the AP: limits divided at once, for the whole AP | CTA 2010 s 18E; CTM03580 (3) |
| Associated companies: QIPs | Counted **on the day before the AP begins** (HMRC's reading of SI 1998/3175 reg 3): thresholds change from the next AP | CTM92530; COM95001 (3) |
| Group relief | Only from joining, for the overlapping period; the old group's relief ends earlier, when arrangements for the sale exist | CTA 2010 ss 139–142, 154 (15) |
| Deductions allowance | Newcomer must be covered by the group's nomination | CTA 2010 ss 269ZR ff (14) |
| AIA | One £1m shared by the group (parent undertaking test at the end of each chargeable period) | CAA 2001 s 51C (8) |
| SAO | UK-incorporated member counts for a financial year only if it was a member at the end of its **preceding** financial year | FA 2009 Sch 46; SAOG11260 (4) |
| Tax strategy; UTT | Covered by the group's strategy; UTT notifications on its returns | FA 2016 Sch 19; FA 2022 Sch 17 (4) |
| Transfer pricing | **SME exemption lost**: size measured across linked enterprises; arm's length pricing and records; local file and inclusion in master file and CbC report (€750m groups) | TIOPA 2010 ss 166, 172; SI 2023/818; SI 2016/237 (27) |
| UK-to-UK transactions | Generally exempt for chargeable periods commencing on or after 1 January 2026 | TIOPA 2010 s 164A (27) |
| CIR | Part of the worldwide group; one reporting company | TIOPA 2010 Part 10 (28) |
| Pillar Two | Constituent entity of a group with revenue above €750m | F(No.2)A 2023 Parts 3–4 (30) |
| R&D | ERIS lost; merged RDEC; HMRC: large for the whole AP of takeover | CTA 2009 Part 13; CIRD92000 (10) |
| Stamp duty | Buyer pays 0.5% within 30 days | FA 1999 Sch 13; FA 1986 s 87 (SDRT) (21) |

The examiners reported that most candidates found transfer pricing, but some wrote at length on it and ignored easier points, and some raised hybrids with no trigger in the question.

**Calder.** TPLC bought Calder Valve Engineering Ltd (CVE) from the Oldroyd family, our invented former owners, on **1 April GY1** for **£32.0m** cash; stamp duty **£160,000**. The Oldroyds are individuals, so their side is capital gains tax, outside this paper.

> **Going further: integrating a target**
> - Diary the **first very large instalment** and the **first SAO year** on the day the SPA is signed.
> - Decide the target's **accounting date** deliberately: keeping Calder on 31 March kept it out of the group payment arrangement for three years; ending BSL's AP at completion would have saved ERIS on six months' spending.
> - Add the newcomer to the **deductions allowance nomination**, the **CIR** group, the **master file/local file** and **CbC** data, and the **Pillar Two** data set.
> - Check **associated company** consequences for marginal relief in every other group company: the count rises at once.

## Selling a subsidiary

A group selling a subsidiary has its own checklist. **M24 Q3** (15 marks, 7/5/3) combined a sale after an intra-group transfer with the degrouping charge, the SSE, losses after a change of ownership and enquiries; **N24 Q3** (15 marks) combined pre-sale transactions with depreciatory transactions, value shifting, the SSE and pre-sale dividends.

| Step | Question | Rule (chapter) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Does the SSE cover the share sale? | Sch 7AC: gains exempt, losses not allowable (18) |
| 2 | Does the company leave with an asset received at no gain, no loss within 6 years? | TCGA 1992 s 179: deemed sale and reacquisition at market value **immediately after the intra-group acquisition**; on a share sale by a group member the gain is **added to the seller's consideration** (s 179(3A)–(3D)), so it is exempt with the share sale; the asset is rebased (17) |
| 2a | Intangibles received within 6 years? | CTA 2009 s 780 charge on the leaving company, **switched off** by s 782A where the share sale qualifies for the SSE; no rebasing (11) |
| 3 | Land received with SDLT group relief within 3 years? | FA 2003 Sch 7 para 3: clawback on market value at the original date; charge on the leaving company (21) |
| 4 | Value moved out before the sale? | s 31 value shifting (main purpose; **not** where the arrangements consist solely of an exempt distribution); s 176 depreciatory transactions (no motive test; **losses only**); s 177 dividend stripping (17) |
| 5 | When does it leave? | For gains, on the sale; for group relief, when arrangements exist (CTA 2010 s 154). HMRC treats a plain sale as an arrangement (CTM80206); its consortium guidance dates arrangements from agreement in principle, even subject to contract (CTM80625; SP 3/93) (15) |
| 6 | How is the price paid? | Earn-out right: a separate chose in action, valued in the proceeds (*Marren v Ingles*, HL, 1980; CG14990); share earn-outs: s 138A (18) |

> **Exam lens: selling a subsidiary**
>
> - **Grade:** SSE **1**; companies leaving groups **1**; depreciatory transactions **1**; value shifting ss 29–30 **1** and s 31 **1**; change in company ownership **1**; *Marren v Ingles* **1**.
> - **Past appearances:** **M24 Q3** (15: degrouping into SSE, preference shares, then major change in nature or conduct of trade, then enquiry/discovery; well answered; some answered pre-sale loss relief, which was not asked); **N24 Q3** (15: the examiners said candidates confused depreciatory transactions, which "do not consider motive and only apply to losses", with value shifting, which is "motive based", and missed the exempt distribution carve-out).
> - **Style:** explain and compute: degrouping gain, where it lands, rebased cost, SSE conclusion; 0.5–1 mark per point.
> - **Traps:** market value instead of the transferor's cost on the intra-group transfer; measuring the degrouping gain at leaving instead of at the transfer; computing a loss within the SSE; putting the intangibles charge on the seller's proceeds (it is on the leaving company, and switched off); answering questions not asked.

## The hive-down

The **hive-down** is the group's standard way of selling a business that is not already in its own company: move the trade and assets into a new subsidiary, then sell the subsidiary's shares. Each part of the move is designed to be tax-neutral:

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| Trade | CTA 2010 Part 22 Ch 1 (75% common ownership; tax condition): no cessation; CAs continue at TWDV (s 948); trading losses pass, restricted where relevant liabilities stay behind (chapter 19) |
| Land and other capital assets | TCGA 1992 s 171: no gain, no loss |
| Intangibles | CTA 2009 s 775: tax-neutral at tax WDV |
| SDLT | FA 2003 Sch 7 group relief, **denied at the outset** if arrangements then exist for the new company to leave the group (para 2(2)(b)); otherwise clawed back on a sale within 3 years |
| VAT | Transfer of a going concern (signpost) |

The buyer gets a **clean company** whose only history is post-hive-down; older liabilities stay with the seller. The seller gets a share sale. The difficulty is **time**. **Sch 7AC para 15A** treats the seller as having held the shares during any part of the 12 months before the sale when the subsidiary's assets were used by another **member of the group** for that member's trade while a member. Because the holding period relies on para 15A, the company sold must also be trading **immediately after** the sale (para 19).

That is where M Group failed: before its subsidiary was formed it was a single company, its assets were not used by "a member of a group", and the Upper Tribunal held that a group requires more than one company. A business run by a single company cannot hive down and sell within 12 months; a business run inside an existing group can.

The hive-down does not dissolve every charge: the degrouping charge still arises (usually exempt); SDLT claws back within 3 years; and any losses in the new company meet Part 14 when it is sold.

## Tarnmoor Actuators is sold

**The facts (invented).** On **1 February GY6** TEL hived its actuators business down into TAL under CTA 2010 Part 22 Ch 1, TCGA 1992 s 171 and CTA 2009 s 775. **In our invented case** the board's reason was to run actuators as a separate business with its own management; no buyer was in view at that time, and Brennock Industries Inc (an invented US company) first approached the group in the summer of GY6. TAL received:

| Asset | TEL's tax figure | Market value at 1 February GY6 |
|---|---|---|
| Factory (bought after 2017: no indexation) | Cost £5,000,000 | £7,500,000 |
| Patents | Tax WDV £1,200,000 | £4,000,000 |
| Plant | Moved at TWDV (s 948) | — |

On **31 December GY6** TEL sold TAL's shares to Brennock for **£48.0m** cash plus a cash earn-out of up to **£6.0m** based on TAL's results over the next two years (GY7 and GY8), valued at **£3.0m** at completion. In December TAL paid TEL a dividend of **£2.0m** from its post-hive-down profits.

> **Worked example 20.5: the sale of TAL (invented; £)**
>
> **1. SSE (TCGA 1992 Sch 7AC).** TEL held TAL for 11 months. The actuators assets were used in TEL's own trade, as a member of the Tarnmoor group, throughout the 12 months before the sale: **para 15A** treats TEL as holding the shares for that time. Para 15A also treats TAL as a qualifying (trading) company for that period, before it existed (as HMRC's guidance at CG53080C and commentators describe it); it was trading from 1 February GY6 and immediately after the sale (para 19). **Exempt.**
>
> **2. Degrouping charge on the factory (s 179).** TAL acquired it from TEL on 1 February GY6 and leaves within 6 years.
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Market value at 1 February GY6 (deemed sale and reacquisition immediately after the acquisition) | 7,500,000 |
> | Less TEL's cost carried over at no gain, no loss | (5,000,000) |
> | Degrouping gain (no indexation after December 2017) | 2,500,000 |
>
> TAL leaves because TEL disposes of shares, so the gain does not accrue to TAL; it is **added to TEL's consideration** for the shares (s 179(3A)–(3D)):
>
> | TEL's consideration for the TAL shares | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Cash | 48,000,000 |
> | Earn-out right at completion value (*Marren v Ingles*) | 3,000,000 |
> | Degrouping gain added | 2,500,000 |
> | **Total consideration** | **53,500,000** |
> | Less base cost of the TAL shares | (irrelevant) |
> | **Chargeable gain** | **nil: SSE** |
>
> Taxed on its own, the degrouping gain would have cost £2,500,000 × 25% = **£625,000**. TAL's base cost of the factory becomes **£7,500,000**.
>
> **3. Patents (CTA 2009 ss 780, 782A).** The leaving is caused by an SSE-qualifying share disposal, so **s 782A switches off s 780**. The counterfactual credit would have been £4,000,000 − £1,200,000 = £2,800,000 in TAL (CT **£700,000**, chapter 11). The patents keep tax WDV **£1,200,000**: no rebasing.
>
> **4. SDLT clawback (FA 2003 Sch 7 para 3).** The factory moved with group relief less than 3 years before TAL left the group. Clawback on market value at 1 February GY6:
>
> | Band (non-residential) | £ |
> |---|---|
> | £0–£150,000 at 0% | 0 |
> | £150,001–£250,000 at 2% | 2,000 |
> | £250,001–£7,500,000 at 5% | 362,500 |
> | **SDLT clawed back (payable by TAL)** | **364,500** |
>
> TAL is liable, but by then Brennock owns it: the SPA has **TEL indemnify** Brennock. (Had arrangements for the sale existed at the hive-down, relief would have been denied at the outset instead: same tax, earlier, with a late-return risk. The board minute recording why the hive-down happened is the evidence: chapter 21.)
>
> **5. Pre-sale dividend.** £2,000,000 from post-hive-down profits: an exempt distribution for TEL (CTA 2009 Part 9A). The arrangements consist solely of an exempt distribution, so **s 31 does not apply**; s 176/s 177 can only reduce a loss, and there is none.
>
> **6. Buyer's stamp duty (FA 1986).** 0.5% on the consideration, within 30 days; a non-resident buyer is not excused. Under the **contingency principle** (STSM021120, citing the House of Lords in *Underground Electric Railways*, 1906) a stated maximum is charged in full and never refunded:
>
> | | Consideration £ | Stamp duty £ |
> |---|---|---|
> | Cash | 48,000,000 | 240,000 |
> | Earn-out cap | 6,000,000 | 30,000 |
> | **Brennock's stamp duty** | **54,000,000** | **270,000** |
>
> **7. Leaving.** TAL had no losses, so the group relief arrangements rules took nothing away. Its AP (1 February–31 December GY6; QIP divisor 11: very large threshold £20m ÷ 11 × 11/12 = **£1,666,667**; large £125,000; first-year limit £833,333) ended on the day of sale in any case. Brennock's advisers now run the joining checklist from the other side.

**The earn-out.** Each later payment will be a disposal of the earn-out right, a separate asset, not of shares (*Marren v Ingles*; CG14990). Whether gains on those receipts are taxed, given that the share sale was exempt, is examined in chapter 18.

> **Going further: negotiating the TAL deal**
> - **Price and structure together.** Brennock gets the factory rebased at £7.5m free of tax (s 179(3) rebasing), but the patents stay at £1.2m. Its model prices the lost step-up on the patents, not on the factory.
> - **Indemnities.** The SDLT clawback and any degrouping exposure are seller items; so is TAL's tax for its 11 months under Tarnmoor. A **locked-box** or **completion accounts** mechanism decides who bears TAL's pre-completion instalments.
> - **The dividend.** Paying £2.0m out before completion took cash out tax-free and reduced the consideration on which Brennock paid stamp duty (for this example only: had the cash been left in and the price raised by £2.0m, the duty would have been £10,000 more).
> - **Timing.** A sale within 3 years of the hive-down claws back SDLT; a sale within 6 years triggers degrouping. Neither costs CT here because of the SSE; both must be in the model.

## Shares or assets, priced

Suppose, **for this example only**, that TEL had sold the actuators assets directly to Brennock at the same values.

> **Worked example 20.6: TAL counterfactual (hypothetical; not a story fact; £)**
>
> | | Asset sale by TEL | Share sale of TAL (actual) |
> |---|---|---|
> | **Seller's CT** | | |
> | Factory gain £2,500,000 × 25% | 625,000 | 0 (degrouping gain added to SSE proceeds) |
> | Patents credit £2,800,000 × 25% | 700,000 | 0 (s 782A) |
> | Plant balancing charges | not computed | 0 |
> | **Total CT on these items** | **1,325,000** | **0** |
> | **Buyer's tax costs of the assets** | | |
> | Factory base cost | 7,500,000 | 7,500,000 (rebased) |
> | Patents tax cost | 4,000,000 | 1,200,000 |
> | Lost step-up on patents × 25% (undiscounted) | — | 700,000 |
> | **Stamp taxes** | | |
> | SDLT on the factory (buyer) | 364,500 | — |
> | SDLT clawback (TAL; borne by TEL under the indemnity) | — | 364,500 |
> | Stamp duty on shares (buyer) | — | 270,000 |
>
> The plant tells the same story on a smaller scale: bought as assets it would have entered Brennock's pools at its price (second-hand: no full expensing); inside TAL it continues at TWDV, with balancing charges deferred.

Every pound of the £1,325,000 saving came from the SSE. The buyer gave up almost nothing on the factory (the 2011 degrouping rule rebased it) and everything on the patents. Stamp taxes cut the other way: a hive-down followed by a sale within 3 years made them dearer, not cheaper.

The tax function's job is to run this comparison **before heads of terms are signed**. Once the structure is agreed in principle, the price has been struck on it, and the arrangements that switch off reliefs may already exist.

So the misconception has its answer. The share sale was better for Tarnmoor, but only because the SSE applied; and the price still had to absorb the buyer's lost relief on the patents, though not on the factory.

## How the examiner tests it

This topic rarely arrives with one label. It arrives as a scenario: a group buys or sells a company, and the requirement asks for the tax consequences.

> **Exam lens: buying and selling companies**
>
> - **Grades:** SSE **1**; companies leaving groups **1**; change in company ownership **1**; value shifting **1**; depreciatory transactions **1**; *Marren v Ingles* **1**; CT self-assessment **1**; SAO **1**; tax strategy **1**; transfer pricing **1**; CIR **1**; stamp duty and SDLT **3** (add-on marks routinely missed). At least 70% of the CT element comes from core material.
> - **Past appearances:** **M23 Q6** (10: acquisition costs); **M24 Q3** (15, 7/5/3: sale of a subsidiary, degrouping and SSE, change of ownership, enquiries); **N24 Q3** (15: pre-sale transactions, depreciatory transactions v value shifting, SSE, pre-sale dividends); **N24 Q5** (20, 4/3/6.5/3.5/3: compliance consequences of joining a US group).
> - **Style:** technical explanation with short computations; no letter or report format; 0.5–1 mark per point. "Tax consequences" means every tax in the syllabus, including stamp duty and SDLT; a named tax means only that tax. Follow the deal: what happens on the day, then what changes afterwards, tax by tax. Where the facts leave a point open (for example whether a change in a trade is "major"), state the facts that drive your conclusion. Give a recommendation if asked.
> - **Traps:** market value instead of NGNL cost; degrouping gain measured at leaving; computing an SSE loss; stamp duty confused with SDLT; QIP associates counted at the wrong date (marginal relief: any time in the AP; QIPs: the day before the AP); writing at length on TP and missing easy marks; pre-sale loss relief when not asked; forgetting the stamp taxes; ignoring the contingency principle on an earn-out cap.
> - **Layout:** a numbered list of consequences by tax; a degrouping computation showing where the gain lands; a stamp taxes line for every share or land transfer; £ throughout (not £000) where amounts are small.

## What to take away

- **Shares or assets.** A share buyer gets the company's whole history and no step-up; an asset buyer gets a fresh tax cost for each asset and leaves the history behind. A share seller usually gets the SSE; an asset seller pays tax on gains, balancing charges and intangibles credits. Price settles the difference.
- **Contract.** Due diligence finds the history; warranties make the seller disclose; a tax indemnity makes the seller pay, pound for pound, for tax of its years. Payments under either are usually treated as price reductions (s 49; ESC D33).
- **Costs.** Post-decision acquisition costs are capital (*Centrica*); arrangement fees are loan relationship debits; stamp duty is part of the cost of shares, and convertible notes are not exempt loan capital (FA 1986 s 79(5)); change-of-control compensation is unlikely to be deductible (HMRC's view).
- **Joining.** Count associates twice: marginal relief at any time in the AP; QIPs on the day before the AP. Calder was large in its first group AP (4 × £187,500) and very large one period later. Then group relief, deductions allowance, AIA, SAO, transfer pricing and records, CIR, Pillar Two and R&D status.
- **Selling.** SSE; degrouping (s 179: added to the seller's proceeds and usually exempt; asset rebased); intangibles (s 782A: switched off, no rebasing); SDLT clawback; s 31 and s 176; leaving date and arrangements; earn-outs.
- **Hive-down.** Part 22 Ch 1, s 171, s 775 and SDLT group relief move the business; para 15A lends the seller the trade's history inside the group, but only inside a group (*M Group*).
- **Tarnmoor Actuators (invented):** £48.0m cash + earn-out valued £3.0m + degrouping gain £2.5m = **£53.5m** exempt; patents stay at £1.2m; SDLT clawback **£364,500** under TEL's indemnity; Brennock's stamp duty **£270,000** (£240,000 on cash + £30,000 on the £6.0m cap).

Eleven months was enough for Tarnmoor, because the group had been there all along.

Many of the taxes in this chapter were stamp taxes, quietly attached to bigger questions. Chapter twenty one, on stamp taxes for groups, gives them a chapter of their own.

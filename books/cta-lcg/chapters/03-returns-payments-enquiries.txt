Chapter three: Returns, payments and enquiries.

When should a company pay tax on profits it has not yet finished making? For most of the life of corporation tax the answer was simple. You made the profit, you closed the books, and months later you paid. Then the rules changed for accounting periods beginning on or after the first of April, twenty nineteen. The very largest companies were told to pay inside the year itself. A company with a calendar year end now hands over the first quarter of its tax bill in March, when nine months of the trading that will produce it have not yet happened.

That rule, and the machinery around it, is the subject of this chapter. Corporation tax for a larger company is not one event a year. It is a calendar. Instalments fall due every quarter. Returns fall due a year after the accounts close. The window in which the tax authority may open an enquiry stays open longer for a company in a large group than for a company on its own. Discovery can reach back four, six or twenty years. And every one of those dates carries a price when it is missed, in interest or in penalties.

This is the third chapter of Part One. Self-assessment, the charge to corporation tax and accounting periods, disclosure and discovery, penalties and interest, and the surrender of tax refunds within a group are all core, grade one, on the Advanced Technical grid. The Living Law, chapter five, built the compliance machine for a single invented company, Calder Valve Engineering, paying four equal instalments. Here we put Calder into a group, and the machine starts to behave differently. Throughout, the law is the law for the financial year twenty twenty six.

A few names first. The company return rules sit in Schedule eighteen to the Finance Act nineteen ninety eight, which I will call Schedule eighteen. The Taxes Management Act nineteen seventy is the Management Act. The Corporation Tax, Instalment Payments, Regulations nineteen ninety eight are the instalment payment regulations. The Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine is the two thousand and nine Act, and the Corporation Tax Act twenty ten is the twenty ten Act. His Majesty's Revenue and Customs is H M R C.

The accounting period, again.

Every date in this chapter hangs from one idea, the accounting period. The Living Law, chapter twenty, set out the rules. An accounting period begins when a company comes within the charge to corporation tax, or when its previous accounting period ends. It ends on the first of a list of events. Twelve months pass. The company reaches its accounting date, or the end of a period for which it makes up accounts. It starts or stops trading, or stops being within the charge. It stops being resident in the United Kingdom. A winding up starts, or the company enters administration. Those last events belong to chapter fourteen, and leaving the United Kingdom belongs to chapter twenty two.

Notice what is missing from that list. A change of owner does not end an accounting period. When our invented group, Tarnmoor, buys Calder on the first of April in Group Year One, Calder's accounting period simply begins as usual on that date, because its old year ended the night before. When Tarnmoor buys Brackenwell Sensors on the first of July in Group Year Two, Brackenwell's accounting period runs straight on to the thirty first of December. The acquisition matters enormously for losses, for group relief and for the associated company count. It does not reset the tax calendar.

A word about the invented case for anyone joining here. Tarnmoor is an invented, widely held engineering group listed in London. Every company, person and number in its story is invented for teaching. Its story years are Group Years, never calendar years, and every computation applies the law for the financial year twenty twenty six.

The twelve month ceiling produces the long period problem. A company may make up accounts for fifteen months, for example when it changes its year end or in its first period. Tax cannot follow. The first twelve months form one accounting period and the last three months form a second. Two returns, two computations, two payment dates, from one set of accounts. A short period of account, such as the nine months Calder will later use to move its year end, is simpler. It is one accounting period, and every threshold in it is scaled down to nine twelfths.

Two notification duties sit at the start. Under section fifty five of the Finance Act two thousand and four, a company must tell H M R C within three months after its first accounting period begins that it has come within the charge. And under paragraph two of Schedule eighteen, a company that is chargeable for a period but has not received a notice to deliver a return must notify H M R C within twelve months after the period ends. Failure to notify attracts a penalty under Schedule forty one to the Finance Act two thousand and eight, geared to the tax unpaid and to the company's behaviour. In a large group the risk lies with the newcomers, the hive-down vehicle or the new holding company formed for a deal. The group tax function keeps a list, and every new company goes on it the day it is incorporated.

When the return is due.

The return is built on the accounts, so its deadline is built on the accounts too. Paragraph fourteen of Schedule eighteen sets the filing date as the last of several dates. The first is twelve months after the end of the accounting period the return covers. The second applies where the accounting period sits inside a period of account. If that period of account is eighteen months or less, the date is twelve months after the period of account ends. If it is longer than eighteen months, the date is thirty months after the period of account begins. The third is three months after the notice to deliver the return. Whichever is latest wins.

Put simply, the filing date follows the period of account. That sentence won marks in November twenty twenty five, when a question on the administration of a new subsidiary turned on it.

Here is a labelled example, not part of the story. Suppose a new subsidiary starts trading on the first of October and makes up its first accounts for fifteen months, to the thirty first of December of the following year. It has two accounting periods. The first runs for twelve months, to the thirtieth of September of that following year. The second runs for three months, to the thirty first of December. Both returns are due on the same day, twelve months after the period of account ends, so the thirty first of December a year later again. The first return therefore gets fifteen months, not twelve.

Now the trap. Payment does not follow the period of account. For a company outside the instalment rules, the tax for each accounting period is due nine months and one day after that accounting period ends. So the tax for the first period in our example falls due on the first of July, months before its return is due. The examiners have seen candidates move the payment date along with the filing date. Do not.

There is a reason for the asymmetry. Payment depends on profit, which the company can estimate. Filing depends on finished accounts, which it cannot rush. The law lets the paperwork wait for the accounts, but not the money.

In a group, the return is also where the group regimes are switched on. Claims and surrenders of group relief, elections and many other claims are made in or with the return of the company concerned. Each of Tarnmoor's companies in the United Kingdom files its own return, with its own filing date. The tax function's real deadline is the earliest of them, because a surrender in one company's return must match a claim in another's.

Paying before the bill is known.

Instalments arrived with corporation tax self-assessment, for accounting periods ending on or after the first of July, nineteen ninety nine. They were phased in. H M R C's manual records that sixty per cent of a large company's liability went through instalments in the first year, seventy two per cent in the second, eighty eight per cent in the third, and the whole liability for periods ending on or after the first of July, two thousand and two. The effect was to bring large companies' tax forward, closer to the time the profits were earned.

Think of it as paying the bill while the meal is still being eaten. A large company pays its first two courses' worth before the meal ends and the last two shortly after. A very large company pays the whole bill before it puts down its fork.

The precise rule is in the instalment payment regulations. A company is large if its augmented profits exceed one point five million pounds. Augmented profits are its taxable total profits plus most exempt dividends it receives from companies outside its own group. A large company with a twelve month accounting period pays four equal instalments, on the fourteenth day of the seventh, tenth, thirteenth and sixteenth months after the period begins. Two fall inside the year and two after it.

A company is very large if its augmented profits exceed twenty million pounds. For accounting periods beginning on or after the first of April, twenty nineteen, it pays on the fourteenth day of the third, sixth, ninth and twelfth months. Every payment falls inside the period. For a company with a calendar year, that means the fourteenth of March, June, September and December.

Each instalment is three twelfths of the total liability for a twelve month period. In a shorter period it is three divided by the number of months, so a nine month period has three instalments of a third each.

Two exemptions keep small cases out. A company whose total liability does not exceed ten thousand pounds, scaled down for a short period, is not treated as large. And there is a year of grace. A company is not treated as large for a period if its profits do not exceed ten million pounds and it was not large in the twelve months before. A growing company therefore usually pays its first large year on the normal date, nine months and one day after the year end.

Here is the misconception this chapter must kill. The grace is for large companies only. H M R C's manual is explicit that the period of grace does not apply in deciding whether a company is very large. A company is very large in the first period its profits pass the threshold. There is no warning year.

Counting the family.

The thresholds are not fixed amounts for a company in a group. They are divided by one plus the number of the company's associated companies, and scaled down for a short accounting period. The Living Law, chapter twenty, taught the test. Since the first of April, twenty twenty three, companies are associated if one controls the other or both are under common control, under sections eighteen E to eighteen J of the twenty ten Act. Dormant companies and passive holding companies are ignored. Companies resident abroad count.

So a large group shrinks every member's thresholds. In our invented case, once Calder joins in Group Year One, nine Tarnmoor companies count. Each has eight associates, so the divisor is nine. For a twelve month period that would bring the large threshold down to one hundred and sixty six thousand, six hundred and sixty seven pounds, and the very large threshold to two million, two hundred and twenty two thousand, two hundred and twenty two pounds. Tarnmoor Pumps, the group's dormant company, does not count. For marginal relief, that divisor of nine applies at once. For instalments, as we are about to see, it arrives a year later, in Group Year Two.

Now the subtle part, and it is where careful advisers part company with careless ones. When do you count? For the small profits rate and marginal relief, a company is associated with another if the relationship exists at any time in the accounting period. But H M R C's manual says that, for instalments, associated companies are counted as at the day before the accounting period begins, or on its first day if the company had no accounting period the day before. That reflects the instalment payment regulations as H M R C reads them. It has a practical logic. A company needs to know at the start of its year which instalment regime it is in, because a very large company's first payment falls only three months in.

So the same acquisition has two different timings. Join a group, and your marginal relief limits are divided at once, for the whole period in which you join. Your instalment thresholds are divided only from the next accounting period, the first one that begins after you joined.

Apply it to the invented Tarnmoor companies with a thirty first of December year end. At the end of the year before Group Year One, each of them had seven associates. Calder was not yet in the group. So for Group Year One their instalment divisor is eight, and the very large threshold is two and a half million pounds. For Group Year Two, Calder is counted, Brackenwell is not yet, and the divisor is nine. From Group Year Three, Brackenwell counts and the divisor is ten, giving a very large threshold of two million pounds. Every main trading company in the group is far above those figures, so the conclusion does not change. But in an exam the divisor is worth a mark, and the counting date is where it is won or lost.

One more point of status. I have described the counting date in H M R C's words because the regulation itself was not opened for this chapter. The manual is not law. If a question turns on it, cite the regulations and note H M R C's reading.

Calder joins the group.

Go back to The Living Law, chapter five. In its year to the thirty first of March, twenty twenty seven, Calder was a standalone family company with taxable total profits of two million pounds and no associated companies. Its corporation tax was five hundred thousand pounds, paid as a large company in four instalments of one hundred and twenty five thousand pounds, on the fourteenth of October, twenty twenty six, and the fourteenth of January, April and July, twenty twenty seven. It had been large before, so it had no year of grace.

Some years later, in our invented story, Tarnmoor buys Calder from the Oldroyd family on the first of April in Group Year One, for thirty two million pounds. Calder keeps its thirty first of March year end for now. Its first accounting period in the group runs from the first of April in Group Year One to the thirty first of March in Group Year Two.

Graham Pike, Calder's finance director, asks Tom Hesketh, Tarnmoor's head of tax, the obvious question. Calder now has eight associates. Its profits for the year are forecast at three million pounds. Divide twenty million pounds by nine and you get about two point two two million pounds. Is Calder now very large, paying from June?

Tom's answer turns on the counting date. On the thirty first of March in Group Year One, the day before the period began, the Oldroyds still owned Calder, and they own no other companies. Calder had no associates. For instalment purposes, its divisor for this period is one. Its augmented profits of three million pounds are above one point five million pounds but far below twenty million. So Calder is large, not very large. It was large before, so no year of grace applies. It pays on the fourteenth of October in Group Year One, and the fourteenth of January, April and July in Group Year Two.

Then comes the amount, and here the merged research and development scheme bites. Calder's three million pounds of profit includes a research and development expenditure credit of four hundred thousand pounds, which is a taxable receipt. Corporation tax at twenty five per cent is seven hundred and fifty thousand pounds. Chapter ten explains how the expenditure credit is set first against the company's corporation tax, leaving three hundred and fifty thousand pounds to pay. But H M R C's research manual says the credit is not taken into account in calculating instalments. It is claimed in the return, after the year. So Calder's instalments are a quarter of seven hundred and fifty thousand pounds, one hundred and eighty seven thousand five hundred pounds each.

The four hundred thousand pounds comes back only once the return is filed and the claim made. H M R C's manual says the credit may then be used to discharge later instalments. So Graham files Calder's first group return early, in the summer of Group Year Two, and the credit goes towards the next period's instalments instead of waiting for a repayment.

The next period is where the divisor arrives. On the thirty first of March in Group Year Two, Calder has eight associates. Brackenwell does not join until July, too late to count. The divisor is nine, the very large threshold is two million, two hundred and twenty two thousand, two hundred and twenty two pounds, and Calder's forecast profits are comfortably above it. From the first of April in Group Year Two, Calder is very large. It pays on the fourteenth of June, September and December in Group Year Two and the fourteenth of March in Group Year Three.

Count Calder's payment dates in the calendar of Group Year Two. January, April and July for the old period. June, September and December for the new. Six instalments in twelve months. That is the cash cost of crossing into the very large regime, and the treasury team needs it in the forecast before the first one is due. The full working is in the reading edition.

Truing up.

An instalment is a payment on account of a liability nobody yet knows. So how can it be wrong? The answer is that it is judged afterwards. Once the final liability for the period is settled, the regulations ask what each instalment should have been, three months' share of that final figure, and charge interest on any shortfall from each due date until it is paid.

So the company's duty is to pay on its best estimate and to correct as the estimate moves. If the forecast rises, the next instalment must catch up, so that the total paid matches what should by then have been paid on the new forecast. If the forecast falls, the company can reclaim an overpayment. In May twenty twenty five the examiners set a very large company whose forecasts changed during the year. They reported that the instalments were done badly, that forecast changes were not trued up, and that some candidates did not know the very large company rules.

Here is a labelled example, not part of the story. Suppose a very large company with a calendar year expects a liability of four million pounds. It pays one million pounds in March and one million in June. By September its forecast is six million pounds. By then it should have paid three quarters of six million, four and a half million pounds. So the September payment is two and a half million pounds. In December the final liability comes in at six point four million pounds, and the company pays the remaining one point nine million.

Was it penalised for March and June? Not with a penalty. But measured against the final six point four million pounds, each instalment should have been one point six million. The company was short by six hundred thousand pounds after March, one point two million after June and three hundred thousand after September. Interest at the instalment rate of six point two five per cent on those shortfalls, each for three months, comes to thirty two thousand, eight hundred and twelve pounds and fifty pence. Honest forecasting does not stop interest. It only keeps the company clear of penalties.

Penalties need more than a bad forecast. Regulation thirteen of the instalment payment regulations allows a penalty where a company, knowingly or recklessly, pays too little. The maximum is twice the interest on the shortfall. H M R C's manual adds that a penalty can arise only after the return has been filed and the normal due date has passed.

Calder faces the forecasting problem for real in Group Year Three. To join Tarnmoor's calendar it makes up accounts for nine months, from the first of April to the thirty first of December. On the thirty first of March in Group Year Three it has nine associates, so the divisor is ten, and the very large threshold for nine months is one point five million pounds. Applying H M R C's manual on short periods, Calder pays three instalments of a third each, on the fourteenth of June, September and December.

The June figure is built on a trading forecast. But on the fifteenth of June Calder announces the closure of its process valves division, and on the first of October it sells that business to Tarnmoor's subsidiary in Vallaria, an invented country, receiving six million pounds for the intangible assets alone. Neither was in the June forecast, and the taxable credit on the sale dwarfs the closure costs. So the December instalment carries the catch-up, and interest runs on the earlier shortfalls whatever Graham does. The lesson for a tax function is that a known transaction belongs in the forecast as soon as it is probable, not when it completes.

Paying as a family.

Every company in a group is a separate taxpayer with its own instalments. That separateness costs money. One company overpays and earns interest at three point five per cent. Another underpays and is charged six point two five per cent. The group as a whole loses the difference. Two tools close the gap.

The first is the group payment arrangement, under section fifty nine F of the Management Act. H M R C may agree that one company in a group pays corporation tax on behalf of the others. The group for this purpose is a company and its fifty one per cent subsidiaries. The nominated company must be resident in the United Kingdom, and the members must share the same accounting date and be up to date with their filing and payments. Crucially, the liability stays with each company. The arrangement changes who writes the cheque, not who owes the tax. H M R C's guidance describes the nominated company paying a single amount on each due date, with the payments allocated among the members once their liabilities are known. That is where the interest saving comes from. One member's overpayment covers another's shortfall from the day it was paid.

In our invented case Tom Hesketh sets up a group payment arrangement from Group Year Three for the companies with a thirty first of December year end, with Tarnmoor Finance as the nominated company. Calder cannot join at first. Its year ends on the thirty first of March. That is one reason for its nine month period in Group Year Three. From Group Year Four it has a calendar year, and it joins.

The second tool is the surrender of tax refunds, in Chapter four of Part twenty two of the twenty ten Act, sections nine hundred and sixty three to nine hundred and sixty six. A group company that is due a refund for an accounting period may surrender all or part of it to another member of the same group. The group is the seventy five per cent group used for group relief. Both companies must have the same accounting period and be in the group from the start of that period until the notice. And they must give H M R C a joint notice before the refund is made. Once the refund is paid out, the chance is gone. Regulation nine of the instalment payment regulations then treats the surrendered tax as paid by the recipient on the dates the surrendering company actually paid it. That neutralises the gap between the two interest rates.

Group Year Two shows why this matters, before the arrangement exists. Tarnmoor Estates paid its instalments on a forecast that included the sale of its depot. The sale slipped to the thirtieth of April in Group Year Three, so Estates overpaid by five hundred and sixty nine thousand, three hundred and seventy five pounds, the tax on the gain it expected. Tarnmoor Engineering, meanwhile, had a stronger second half than forecast. Its liability came in at two million, two hundred and twenty six thousand, two hundred and fifty pounds, and its instalments had totalled one million, eight hundred and twenty six thousand, two hundred and fifty. It was four hundred thousand pounds short.

Before any repayment is made, the two companies give joint notice. Estates surrenders four hundred thousand pounds of its refund to Engineering and takes the rest, one hundred and sixty nine thousand, three hundred and seventy five pounds, as a repayment. Engineering is treated as having paid on Estates' payment dates, so its instalment interest disappears. On a simplified timing, the reading edition shows the group saving about thirteen thousand pounds. The rough rule of thumb is simpler. On four hundred thousand pounds, the gap of two point seven five per cent between the two rates costs eleven thousand pounds a year.

The enquiry window.

Once a return is filed, H M R C may open an enquiry into it, but only within a window. Paragraph twenty four of Schedule eighteen sets it. For a return delivered on time, the window is normally twelve months from the day the return was delivered. File early, and the window closes early.

Not for a large group. For a company that is a member of a group that is not a small group, as defined in the Companies Act, the window runs for twelve months from the filing date, not from delivery. A large group's companies tend to file early. Without this rule they would shorten H M R C's time to look. With it, filing early buys a large group nothing in finality. For a return delivered late, or amended, the window runs to the quarter day after the first anniversary of the day it was delivered.

Each return has its own window. The window is the time within which H M R C must give notice that it is opening an enquiry. Once the notice is given in time, the enquiry itself can run on well beyond the window.

The examiners tested exactly this in May twenty twenty five. A company in a large group had a fifteen month period of account. Candidates applied the small company limits and missed the long period. The examiners reported that answers were poor. Put the two rules together. In our earlier example the fifteen month accounts give two returns, both due twelve months after the period of account ends. Both enquiry windows then run for a further twelve months from that filing date, whenever the returns were actually delivered. For the first period, that is twenty seven months after its own year end.

In our invented story, Calder's nine month return for Group Year Three is due by the thirty first of December in Group Year Four. Calder is in a large group, so H M R C's window runs to the thirty first of December in Group Year Five. On the first of June in Group Year Five, H M R C opens an enquiry into the price at which Calder sold its process valves business. Chapter twenty seven tells that dispute.

An enquiry ends with a closure notice, which states H M R C's conclusions and makes any amendments to the return. The company can appeal against those amendments. Throughout, the tax function's best defence is the file it built when the return was prepared. That means the computations, the reasoning on uncertain points and the evidence behind any transfer price.

Discovery.

When the enquiry window has closed, the return is normally final. The exception is discovery. If H M R C discovers that tax has been lost, for example because profits were left out or a relief was excessive, it may raise a discovery assessment. But not for ever.

Paragraph forty six of Schedule eighteen sets the time limits by reference to the end of the accounting period. The ordinary limit is four years. Where the loss of tax was brought about carelessly, it is six years. Where it was brought about deliberately, or arose from a failure to notify chargeability or certain failures under the disclosure of tax avoidance schemes rules, it is twenty years.

For a return that has been delivered, the statute also protects the company. In broad terms, H M R C can make a discovery assessment only if the loss of tax was brought about carelessly or deliberately, or if an officer could not reasonably have been expected to see the problem from the information the company made available before the window closed. That is why a well-run tax function discloses its contentious positions in the return or with it. Full disclosure narrows the door through which discovery can come.

There is a common misreading here too. Some think that once the enquiry window closes, the company is safe. It is safe from an enquiry, but not from discovery. The difference is that discovery needs something new or something hidden, while an enquiry needs no reason at all.

Take Calder's nine month period ending on the thirty first of December in Group Year Three. The ordinary discovery limit runs to the thirty first of December in Group Year Seven. If Calder had been careless, the limit would run to the end of Group Year Nine. If it had been deliberate, to the end of Group Year Twenty Three.

Records support all of this. Schedule eighteen requires a company to keep the records it needs to make a correct return, and a failure can cost a penalty of up to three thousand pounds for each accounting period. In a group, records also mean the transfer pricing documentation and the reasoning behind each judgement. Chapter twenty seven deals with the documentation rules, and chapter four with the duty to notify uncertain tax treatment, which shares the same logic of telling H M R C early.

Late, wrong or silent.

Penalties come in three families, for being late, for being wrong and for staying silent.

Start with lateness. The Finance Act twenty twenty six, section two hundred and sixty five, doubled the flat-rate late filing penalties for corporation tax returns with a filing date on or after the first of April, twenty twenty six. The statute now says two hundred pounds if the return arrives within three months after the filing date, and four hundred pounds otherwise. If the company was also liable to a late filing penalty for each of its two preceding periods, this third successive failure costs one thousand pounds within three months, or two thousand pounds otherwise. Before the change the figures were one hundred and two hundred pounds, and five hundred and one thousand pounds.

A labelled real-calendar example shows the commencement. A return for a year ended on the thirty first of March, twenty twenty five had a filing date of the thirty first of March, twenty twenty six, so the old figures applied. A return for a year ended on the thirtieth of April, twenty twenty five had a filing date of the thirtieth of April, twenty twenty six, so the new figures applied.

On top of the flat rates sit tax-geared penalties under paragraph eighteen. If the return is still missing eighteen months after the end of the accounting period, or the filing date if that is later, the penalty is ten per cent of the tax unpaid at that point. If it arrives more than two years after the period ends, twenty per cent.

Our invented case meets the persistence rule head on. When Tarnmoor buys Brackenwell on the first of July in Group Year Two, Brackenwell's return for its year to the thirty first of December in Group Year One is still unfiled. It carries a claim for the payable credit under enhanced research and development intensive support, worth nine hundred and forty three thousand, nine hundred and fifty pounds. Brackenwell's two previous returns, from before the story, were both late. The next late return would be the third in a row. Tom's team files it well before the thirty first of December in Group Year Two, and the one thousand or two thousand pound penalty never arises. The amount is small. The signal to H M R C about a newly acquired company's controls is not.

Being wrong comes next. Schedule twenty four to the Finance Act two thousand and seven charges a penalty on an inaccuracy in a return that is careless or deliberate. The maximum is thirty per cent of the potential lost revenue for a careless error, seventy per cent for a deliberate one and one hundred per cent for one that is deliberate and concealed, with reductions for disclosure. Silence is the third family. Failing to notify chargeability falls under Schedule forty one to the Finance Act two thousand and eight, as we saw.

Then interest, which is not a penalty but compensation. Corporation tax paid late carries late payment interest, at seven point seven five per cent when this chapter was written, which is Bank Rate plus four per cent. Tax repaid carries repayment interest at two point seven five per cent. For instalments, the rates in force from the twenty ninth of December, twenty twenty five are six point two five per cent on underpayments, which is Bank Rate plus two and a half per cent, and three point five per cent on overpayments, which is Bank Rate less a quarter of one per cent. The margins for instalment underpayments and late payment rose on the sixth of April, twenty twenty five. All of these move with Bank Rate.

Finally, a narrow change. Section two hundred and sixty four of the Finance Act twenty twenty six extends the late payment penalty regime to tax that becomes payable under the Management Act after a tribunal decision while a further appeal is pending, for amounts payable on or after the first of April, twenty twenty six. A group that loses at the tribunal and appeals further can no longer sit on the tax without that risk.

The rest of the calendar.

Corporation tax returns and instalments are the spine of the calendar, but a large group's year is crowded with other dates, and each belongs to its own chapter. The first research and development claim, or the first after a gap of three years, needs a claim notification within six months after the period of account ends. Chapter ten owns that rule. The interest restriction return belongs to chapter twenty eight, the Pillar Two returns to chapter thirty, and the country-by-country report and transfer pricing records to chapter twenty seven. The senior accounting officer's certificate, the published tax strategy and the uncertain tax treatment notification belong to chapter four.

Going further, three planning choices sit inside this chapter.

The first is the accounting date of an acquired company. Leaving Calder on a thirty first of March year end kept it out of the group payment arrangement and gave the tax function two calendars to run. Moving it with a nine month period aligned the group, but the short period brought its own forecasting problem. Moving to a long period instead would have pushed the filing date out, but not the payment dates.

The second is cash. Joining a group can move a company from large to very large one period later. The treasury team needs to know when, because six instalments can fall in one year.

The third is interest management. A group payment arrangement, refund surrenders made before the refund is paid, and forecasts updated before each instalment are worth real money in a group the size of Tarnmoor. None of them changes a single pound of tax. All of them change what the tax costs.

A typical year in our invented case shows the shape of the work. The companies with a thirty first of December year end pay through the group arrangement in March, June, September and December. Calder, until it moves its year end, runs a second calendar of its own. Returns for the calendar-year companies are due by the thirty first of December after the year, with group relief claims and surrenders lined up across them. And every new company, from a hive-down vehicle to a newly bought subsidiary, goes into the calendar on the day it arrives, with its own counting date for associates and its own history of late returns.

How the examiner tests it.

The examiners return to administration often, usually as a ten or fifteen mark question, or as the tail of a computation. In May twenty twenty five, one question asked for instalments of a very large company with changing forecasts, and another for enquiry windows and discovery for a company in a large group with a fifteen month period of account. In November twenty twenty four, a question on a multinational buying a small British company gave four marks to filing and instalments now that the target was in a large group. In November twenty twenty five, a question on a new subsidiary turned on the filing date following the period of account.

The marks are for precise dates and correct thresholds, with workings shown. Name the regime, large or very large. Show the divisor and say when the associates are counted. Give each due date in full. Explain truing up. For enquiries, say whether the group is small, then count from delivery or from the filing date. For discovery, give the four, six and twenty year limits from the end of the accounting period. The reading edition's exam lens has the detail.

Several traps recur. The months are counted from the start of the accounting period, not from the year end. A very large company's first instalment falls in month three, inside the period. Thresholds are divided and then scaled down for a short period, and the ten million pound limit for the year of grace is divided too. The expenditure credit does not reduce the instalments. Interest on a shortfall is not a penalty, and a penalty needs knowing or reckless conduct. And the rubric asks for workings to the nearest month and pound, so a date should be a date, not a phrase like nine months later.

The style is technical prose and computation, with no letter or report format. Marks come at half a mark or a mark per point, so each correct date, threshold and conclusion is worth stating separately. Where a question says consider the administrative obligations, it usually wants notification, filing, payment and enquiry in turn, and a sentence on penalties for each.

What to take away.

An accounting period lasts at most twelve months, so a fifteen month set of accounts gives two accounting periods and two returns. A change of ownership does not end an accounting period.

The filing date follows the period of account. It is the last of twelve months after the accounting period ends, twelve months after a period of account of up to eighteen months ends, thirty months after a longer period of account begins, and three months after the notice. Payment does not follow the period of account. Tax for each accounting period is due nine months and one day after it ends, unless instalments apply.

A company is large if its augmented profits exceed one point five million pounds, and pays in months seven, ten, thirteen and sixteen. It is very large above twenty million pounds, and pays in months three, six, nine and twelve. Both thresholds are divided by one plus the number of associated companies and scaled down for short periods. For instalments, H M R C counts associates at the day before the period begins. For marginal relief, at any time in the period.

Large companies get a year of grace if profits do not exceed ten million pounds, similarly divided, and they were not large before. Very large companies get none. No instalments are needed where the liability is ten thousand pounds or less.

Instalments are judged against the final liability, so true up when forecasts change. Interest runs on shortfalls, at six point two five per cent, and on overpayments at three point five per cent. Penalties apply only to knowing or reckless underpayment, up to twice the interest. The expenditure credit does not reduce instalments.

A group payment arrangement lets one company pay for its fifty one per cent group with the same accounting date. A refund surrender moves a refund to a seventy five per cent group member with the same accounting period, by joint notice before the refund is made.

The enquiry window is twelve months from delivery, but twelve months from the filing date for a company in a group that is not small. Discovery runs four years from the end of the period, six if careless and twenty if deliberate.

Late filing costs two hundred or four hundred pounds, and one thousand or two thousand for a third successive failure, plus ten or twenty per cent of unpaid tax at eighteen months and two years.

In our invented case, Calder pays as a large company in its first group year, one hundred and eighty seven thousand five hundred pounds four times, then becomes very large. Tarnmoor Estates surrenders four hundred thousand pounds of refund to Tarnmoor Engineering. Brackenwell's third late return never happens.

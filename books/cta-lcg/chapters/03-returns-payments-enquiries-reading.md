# Chapter three: Returns, payments and enquiries

*Part One: The Larger Company*

When should a company pay tax on profits it has not yet finished making? For most of the life of corporation tax the answer was simple. You made the profit, you closed the books, and months later you paid. Then the rules changed for accounting periods beginning on or after 1 April 2019. The very largest companies were told to pay inside the year itself. A company with a calendar year end now hands over the first quarter of its tax bill on 14 March, when nine months of the trading that will produce it have not yet happened.

That rule, and the machinery around it, is the subject of this chapter. Corporation tax for a larger company is not one event a year. It is a calendar. Instalments fall due every quarter. Returns fall due a year after the accounts close. HMRC's enquiry window stays open longer for a company in a large group than for a company on its own. Discovery can reach back 4, 6 or 20 years. And every one of those dates carries a price when it is missed, in interest or in penalties.

This is the third chapter of Part One. CT self-assessment, the charge to CT and accounting periods, disclosure and discovery, penalties and interest, and CTA 2010 Part 22 (which includes the Chapter 4 surrender of tax refunds) are all **core (grade 1)** on the 2026 LCG grid. *The Living Law* (TKS), chapter 5, built the compliance machine for a single company, Calder Valve Engineering Ltd, paying four equal instalments. Here we put Calder into a group, and the machine starts to behave differently. The law throughout is FY2026 (1 April 2026 to 31 March 2027).

**Abbreviations used in this chapter.** Sch 18 = FA 1998 Sch 18 (company returns); TMA = Taxes Management Act 1970; the QIP Regulations = Corporation Tax (Instalment Payments) Regulations 1998, SI 1998/3175; CTA 2009 / CTA 2010; QIP = quarterly instalment payment; AP = accounting period; POA = period of account; RDEC = research and development expenditure credit.

---

## The accounting period, again.

Every date in this chapter hangs from one idea, the accounting period (CTA 2009 ss 9–12; recapped from TKS chapter 20). An AP begins when a company comes within the charge to CT, or when its previous AP ends. It ends on the first of these events:

- 12 months after it began;
- the company's accounting date, or the end of a period for which it makes up accounts;
- the company starting or ceasing to trade, or coming within or ceasing to be within the charge to CT;
- the company ceasing to be UK resident (chapter 22);
- the start of a winding up, or the company entering administration (CTA 2009 ss 10, 12; chapter 14).

**What is missing from the list: a change of owner.** When our invented group, Tarnmoor, buys Calder on 1 April GY1, Calder's AP simply begins as usual that day, because its old year ended on 31 March. When Tarnmoor buys Brackenwell Sensors Ltd on 1 July GY2, Brackenwell's AP runs straight on to 31 December GY2. The acquisition matters enormously for losses (chapter 14), group relief (chapter 15) and the associated company count (below). It does not reset the tax calendar.

> **The running case.** Tarnmoor plc is an **invented**, widely held, London-listed engineering group. Every company, person and number in its story is invented for teaching. Its story years are Group Years (GY1, GY2, ...), never calendar years, and every computation applies FY2026 law. Short forms: TPLC (Tarnmoor plc), TEL (Tarnmoor Engineering Ltd), TES (Tarnmoor Estates Ltd), TFL (Tarnmoor Finance Ltd), CVE (Calder), BSL (Brackenwell).

**Long and short periods.** The 12-month ceiling creates the long period problem. A company may make up accounts for 15 months, for example on a change of year end or in its first period. Tax cannot follow: the first 12 months form one AP and the last 3 months a second. Two returns, two computations, two payment dates, from one set of accounts. A short POA (such as the 9 months Calder uses in GY3 to move its year end) is one AP, and every threshold in it is scaled by 9/12.

**Notification.** A company must notify HMRC that it has come within the charge to CT within **3 months** after the start of its first AP (FA 2004 s 55). A company chargeable for an AP that has not received a notice to deliver a return must notify chargeability within **12 months** after the AP ends (Sch 18 para 2). Failure to notify is penalised under FA 2008 Sch 41, geared to the potential lost revenue and the company's behaviour. In a large group the risk lies with newcomers: the hive-down vehicle, the new holding company formed for a deal. The group tax function keeps an entity list and adds every new company the day it is incorporated.

## When the return is due.

The return is built on the accounts, so its deadline is built on the accounts too. Sch 18 para 14 sets the **filing date** as the **last** of:

| | Date | Applies |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | 12 months after the end of the AP for which the return is made | always |
| (b) | 12 months after the end of the relevant POA | POA of 18 months or less |
| (b) | 30 months after the start of the relevant POA | POA longer than 18 months |
| (c) | 3 months after the notice to deliver the return | always |

The "relevant period of account" is the POA in which the AP's last day falls (para 14(2)). Put simply, **the filing date follows the period of account**: the point that decided marks in N25 Q4 (administration of a new subsidiary).

> **Worked example 3.1: a 15-month first period (labelled illustration, not part of the story)**
>
> A new subsidiary starts trading on 1 October (Year A) and makes up its first accounts for 15 months to 31 December (Year B). Assume it is outside the QIP regime and in a large (not small) group.
>
> | | AP 1 | AP 2 |
> |---|---|---|
> | Period | 1 Oct Year A – 30 Sep Year B (12 months) | 1 Oct – 31 Dec Year B (3 months) |
> | Normal payment date (9 months + 1 day after the AP end; TMA s 59D) | 1 July Year C | 1 October Year C |
> | Filing date (Sch 18 para 14: 12 months after the end of a POA of up to 18 months) | 31 December Year C | 31 December Year C |
> | Enquiry window closes (large group: 12 months from the filing date, Sch 18 para 24) | 31 December Year D | 31 December Year D |
> | Ordinary discovery limit (4 years after the AP end, para 46) | 30 September Year F | 31 December Year F |
>
> AP 1's return gets 15 months, not 12; its enquiry window runs 27 months from its own year end. Both returns must be delivered by the same day; the tax for AP 1 is due six months before that.

**The trap: payment does not follow the period of account.** For a company outside the instalment rules, the tax for each AP is due 9 months and 1 day after that AP ends (TMA s 59D). The law lets the paperwork wait for the accounts, but not the money: profit can be estimated, finished accounts cannot be rushed.

**In a group, the return is where the group regimes are switched on.** Group relief claims and surrenders, and many elections and claims, are made in or with the return of the company concerned. Each of Tarnmoor's UK companies files its own return with its own filing date; the tax function's real deadline is the earliest of them, because a surrender in one company's return must match a claim in another's (chapter 15).

> **Exam lens: accounting periods and filing dates**
> - **Grade:** the charge to CT and accounting periods 1; CT self-assessment 1.
> - **Past appearances:** N25 Q4 (20 marks, 4/8/8; part (a) admin on incorporation: notification, APs, "the filing date is based on the period of account"); M25 Q6 (10 marks, 5/5; a 15-month POA shifting filing and enquiry dates, answered poorly).
> - **Traps:** moving the payment date with the filing date; forgetting that a long POA gives two APs with the same filing date; treating an acquisition as ending an AP.
> - **Layout:** a short table of APs with payment date, filing date, enquiry window close and discovery limits, each as a full date.

## Paying before the bill is known.

**History.** Quarterly instalments arrived with CT self-assessment, for APs ending on or after **1 July 1999** (SI 1998/3175), and were phased in. HMRC's COTAX manual (COM95005) records that 60% of a large company's liability went through instalments for APs ending 1 July 1999 to 30 June 2000, 72% for the next year, 88% for the year after, and 100% for APs ending on or after **1 July 2002**. The effect was to bring large companies' tax forward, closer to the time the profits were earned. The **very large** company regime, inserted by SI 2017/1072, applies to APs beginning on or after **1 April 2019** (CTM92520).

**Analogy.** Paying the bill while the meal is still being eaten: a large company pays for two courses before the meal ends and two shortly after; a very large company pays the whole bill before it puts down its fork.

**The rules (SI 1998/3175 reg 3, as substituted).**

| | Large company | Very large company |
|---|---|---|
| Test | Augmented profits > **£1.5m** | Augmented profits > **£20m** |
| Instalments (12-month AP) | 14th day of months **7, 10, 13, 16** from the start of the AP | 14th day of months **3, 6, 9, 12** |
| Calendar-year AP | 14 July, 14 October, 14 January, 14 April | 14 March, 14 June, 14 September, 14 December |
| Amount of each instalment | 3/n × total liability (n = months in the AP, maximum 12) | same |
| Thresholds | ÷ (1 + associated companies); × months/12 for a short AP | same |
| Year of grace | Not large if profits ≤ **£10m** (also divided) **and** not large in the previous 12 months (reg 3(5)) | **None**: very large in the first AP in which the threshold is exceeded (CTM92520, CTM92800) |
| De minimis | Not large if total liability ≤ **£10,000** (pro rata for a short AP) (reg 3(4)) | — |

**Augmented profits** are TTP plus exempt distributions received, other than those from companies in the recipient's own group (TKS chapter 20 recap). Calder held no shareholdings, so its augmented profits equal its TTP.

**The misconception: "a company always gets a year's grace before instalments".** The grace is for **large** companies only. HMRC's manual is explicit that the CTM92530 period of grace does not apply in deciding whether a company is very large. There is no warning year.

## Counting the family.

The thresholds are divided by **one plus the number of associated companies** and scaled down for a short AP. Since 1 April 2023 the associated company test is control (CTA 2010 ss 18E–18J, as recapped from TKS chapter 20): companies are associated if one controls the other or both are under the control of the same person or persons. Dormant companies and passive holding companies are disregarded; non-UK companies count.

**When do you count? Two different answers.**

| Purpose | When associates are counted | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Small profits rate and marginal relief | Associated **at any time** in the AP (even for part of it) | CTA 2010 s 18E; CTM03580 |
| QIP thresholds (£1.5m, £20m, £10m) | **As at the day before the AP begins** (or on its first day if the day before was not in an AP) | HMRC's reading of SI 1998/3175 reg 3: CTM92530 |

So the same acquisition has two timings. Join a group and your marginal relief limits are divided at once, for the whole AP in which you join; your **instalment** thresholds are divided only from the next AP that begins after you joined. The logic is practical: a very large company's first instalment falls in month 3, so it must know its regime at the start of the year. **Status:** this is HMRC's manual; the regulation's current text was not opened for this chapter (see the notes). In an exam, cite reg 3 and HMRC's reading.

> **Worked example 3.2: Tarnmoor's thresholds (invented)**
>
> | Company / AP | Counting date | Associates | Divisor | Large threshold (£) | Very large threshold (£) | £10m grace limit (£) |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | 31 December companies, GY1 | 31 Dec before GY1 (TPLC, TEL, TES, TFL, TWS, TVS, TCM, TIL) | 7 | 8 | 187,500 | 2,500,000 | 1,250,000 |
> | 31 December companies, GY2 | 31 Dec GY1 (+ Calder) | 8 | 9 | 166,667 | 2,222,222 | 1,111,111 |
> | 31 December companies, GY3 and GY4 | 31 Dec GY2 / GY3 (+ Brackenwell) | 9 | 10 | 150,000 | 2,000,000 | 1,000,000 |
> | Calder, AP 1 Apr GY1 – 31 Mar GY2 | 31 Mar GY1 (owned by the Oldroyd family, who own no other companies) | 0 | 1 | 1,500,000 | 20,000,000 | 10,000,000 |
> | Calder, AP 1 Apr GY2 – 31 Mar GY3 | 31 Mar GY2 (Brackenwell joins 1 July GY2: too late) | 8 | 9 | 166,667 | 2,222,222 | 1,111,111 |
> | Calder, 9-month AP 1 Apr – 31 Dec GY3 | 31 Mar GY3 | 9 | 10 | 112,500 (× 9/12) | 1,500,000 (× 9/12) | 750,000 (× 9/12) |
>
> Tarnmoor Pumps Ltd (dormant) never counts. For **marginal relief** the ledger's divisors apply (GY1 9, because Calder is associated for part of GY1; GY2 10; GY3 10): irrelevant in practice, since every trading company is far above £250,000. Every main trading company is far above every QIP threshold too, so conclusions do not change; but the divisor and the counting date earn the marks.

## Calder joins the group.

*The Living Law*, chapter 5: in its year to 31 March 2027, Calder was a standalone company with TTP **£2,000,000**, no associated companies, CT **£500,000**, paid as a large company (large in the prior year, so no grace) in four instalments of **£125,000** on 14 October 2026, 14 January, 14 April and 14 July 2027.

Some years later, in our invented story, TPLC buys Calder from the Oldroyd family on **1 April GY1** for **£32m**. Calder keeps its 31 March year end for now. Graham Pike, Calder's finance director, asks Tom Hesketh, Tarnmoor's head of tax, whether Calder is now very large: eight associates, forecast profits £3.0m, and £20m ÷ 9 = £2,222,222.

> **Worked example 3.3: Calder's first two group APs (invented)**
>
> **AP 1 April GY1 – 31 March GY2**
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Taxable total profits (including taxable RDEC £400,000) = augmented profits | 3,000,000 |
> | Divisor for QIPs (associates counted at 31 March GY1) | 1 |
> | Large threshold £1,500,000: exceeded? | Yes |
> | Very large threshold £20,000,000: exceeded? | No |
> | Year of grace? (large in the previous 12 months) | No |
> | CT at 25% | 750,000 |
> | Less RDEC set off at step 1 (chapter 10), given effect in the return | (400,000) |
> | Net CT for the AP | 350,000 |
>
> **Instalments.** HMRC's view (CIRD89870) is that the RDEC is not taken into account when computing QIPs; it is claimed in the return. So the instalments are 3/12 × £750,000:
>
> | Due date | Month of AP | £ |
> |---|---|---|
> | 14 October GY1 | 7 | 187,500 |
> | 14 January GY2 | 10 | 187,500 |
> | 14 April GY2 | 13 | 187,500 |
> | 14 July GY2 | 16 | 187,500 |
> | Total | | 750,000 |
>
> The £400,000 of credit is recovered once the return is filed and the claim made; CIRD89870 says the credit may then be used to discharge later QIPs. Calder files its return early, in summer GY2, so the credit goes towards its next AP's instalments.
>
> **AP 1 April GY2 – 31 March GY3.** Associates counted at 31 March GY2: 8; divisor 9; very large threshold £2,222,222. Calder's forecast profits are comfortably above it (the AP's figures are fixed in later chapters), so Calder is **very large** (no grace): instalments on **14 June GY2, 14 September GY2, 14 December GY2 and 14 March GY3**.
>
> **Calder's instalment dates in calendar GY2:** 14 January, 14 April, 14 July (AP to 31 March GY2) and 14 June, 14 September, 14 December (AP to 31 March GY3): **six instalments in twelve months**.

The plan for this book had assumed that Calder would be very large in its first group AP, with instalments based on the CT after the RDEC set-off. Both assumptions fail on HMRC's guidance: the associates are counted on 31 March GY1, and the RDEC does not reduce the QIPs. The corrected position is used here and recorded in the chapter notes.

> **Going further: acquisitions and the instalment calendar**
> - **Diligence point:** on any acquisition, establish the target's QIP status for its current AP (its own history, not the buyer's divisor) and the date its first very large instalment will fall once the group's associates count.
> - **Cash:** a company moving from large to very large pays six (or more) instalments in the transition year. Treasury needs the dates before the first is due.
> - **Merged R&D scheme:** the RDEC raises TTP (it is taxable) but does not reduce QIPs, so it can tip a company into instalments and increases the cash paid in the year. Filing early lets the credit discharge the next AP's QIPs.

> **Exam lens: instalments**
> - **Grade:** CT self-assessment 1; penalties and interest 1.
> - **Past appearances:** M25 Q2 (15 marks, 10/5): very large company instalments with changing forecasts, done badly ("forecast changes were not trued up, and some candidates did not know the very large company rules"); N24 Q5 (20 marks): a US group buys a UK SME: filing and instalments now that the company is in a large group, thresholds divided, payment dates (4 marks); quarterly instalments also appear as the tail of a full computation in N25 Q1.
> - **Style:** compute the threshold (show the divisor and the counting date), state the regime, list every due date in full, then the amounts.
> - **Traps:** months counted from the start of the AP; very large has no grace; the £10m grace limit is divided too; the RDEC does not reduce QIPs; short APs scale the thresholds and change the number of instalments.

## Truing up.

An instalment is judged afterwards. Once the final liability is settled, each instalment "should have been" 3/n of it, and interest runs on any shortfall from each due date until it is paid (or until the normal due date, after which ordinary late payment interest applies). The company's duty is to pay on its best estimate and to correct as the estimate moves: if the forecast rises, the next instalment catches up so that the cumulative total matches what should by then have been paid on the new forecast; if it falls, the company can claim back an overpayment (reg 6). The examiners' M25 Q2 comment is the warning: forecast changes were not trued up.

> **Worked example 3.4: truing up (labelled illustration, not part of the story)**
>
> A very large company with a calendar year forecasts a liability of £4.0m in March and June, £6.0m in September; the final liability is £6.4m. QIP debit interest 6.25%.
>
> | Due date | Forecast (£) | Cumulative due on forecast (£) | Paid (£) | Cumulative paid (£) | Should have been, on final £6.4m (£) | Shortfall (£) | Interest to next date, 3 months at 6.25% (£) |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | 14 March | 4,000,000 | 1,000,000 | 1,000,000 | 1,000,000 | 1,600,000 | 600,000 | 9,375.00 |
> | 14 June | 4,000,000 | 2,000,000 | 1,000,000 | 2,000,000 | 3,200,000 | 1,200,000 | 18,750.00 |
> | 14 September | 6,000,000 | 4,500,000 | 2,500,000 | 4,500,000 | 4,800,000 | 300,000 | 4,687.50 |
> | 14 December | 6,400,000 | 6,400,000 | 1,900,000 | 6,400,000 | 6,400,000 | nil | nil |
> | **Total** | | | **6,400,000** | | | | **32,812.50** |
>
> Honest forecasting avoids penalties, not interest. (Interest simplified to three-month blocks; HMRC computes it daily.)

**Penalty.** Reg 13 allows a penalty where a company knowingly or recklessly pays too little by instalments (EM8330's description) (or makes a fraudulent or negligent repayment claim). The maximum is **twice the interest** on the shortfall (EM8330). HMRC's manual adds that a penalty arises only after the return has been filed and the normal due date has passed (COM95070). In the example above, had the March and June instalments been deliberately low, the maximum would be £65,625.

**Short APs for very large companies (CTM92815).** Where the AP ends on the last day of a month, the final instalment is due on the 14th of that last month; the first is due 2 months and 13 days after the AP starts; later ones at 3-month intervals; an instalment date that would fall on or after the final date drops out, the liability being spread over the remaining dates.

> **Worked example 3.5: Calder's 9-month AP, 1 April – 31 December GY3 (invented)**
>
> | | |
> |---|---|
> | Associates at 31 March GY3 | 9 (divisor 10) |
> | Very large threshold | £20m ÷ 10 × 9/12 = **£1,500,000** |
> | First instalment | 1 April + 2 months 13 days = **14 June GY3** |
> | Second | **14 September GY3** |
> | Third (would be 14 December = the final date) | **14 December GY3** (final) |
> | Amount of each | 3/9 = **one third** of the total liability |
>
> The June instalment rests on a trading forecast. On 15 June Calder announces the closure of its process valves division (restructuring provision £1.8m), and on 1 October it sells the business to Tarnmoor Vallaria SA (TVS, in the invented country of Vallaria) for £6.0m of intangible assets (a taxable realisation credit; chapter 11) and £1.4m of plant. Neither was in the June forecast; the December instalment carries the catch-up and interest runs on the earlier shortfalls. Calder's TTP for this AP is computed in later chapters; this chapter fixes only the dates and the threshold.

**The tax function's lesson:** a known transaction belongs in the forecast as soon as it is probable, not when it completes.

## Paying as a family.

Each company is a separate taxpayer with its own instalments, and that separateness costs money: one company overpays and earns 3.50%, another underpays and is charged 6.25%. Two tools close the gap.

**Group payment arrangements (TMA s 59F).** HMRC may agree that one company pays CT on behalf of members of its group. The group is a company and its **51%** subsidiaries (and theirs). The nominated company must be UK resident; members must have the **same accounting date** and be up to date with filing and payments; and **the liability stays with each company** (GOV.UK, "Corporation Tax: group payment arrangements"). The arrangement changes who writes the cheque, not who owes the tax. HMRC's guidance describes a single payment on each due date by the nominated company, allocated among the members once their liabilities are known: one member's overpayment covers another's shortfall from the day it was paid.

In our invented case Tom Hesketh sets up a group payment arrangement from **GY3** for the 31 December companies, with **TFL** as nominated company. Calder cannot join while its year ends on 31 March: one reason for its 9-month AP in GY3. From **GY4** it has a calendar year and joins.

**Surrender of tax refunds within a group (CTA 2010 Part 22 Ch 4, ss 963–966).** A group company due a tax refund for an AP may surrender all or part of it to another member of the same group (s 963(1)). Conditions:

- the group is the **75% group relief group** (COM122020);
- both companies have the **same AP**, and are members of the group from the start of that AP until the notice;
- the companies give a **joint notice** to HMRC **before the refund is made**.

QIP Regulations **reg 9** extends the rule to instalment overpayments so that the recipient is treated as having paid the surrendered tax on the dates the surrendering company actually paid it: the debit/credit interest differential is neutralised (CTM92740). Payments between the companies for the surrender are dealt with by s 966.

> **Worked example 3.6: TES surrenders a refund to TEL, GY2 (invented)**
>
> Both companies are very large in GY2 (divisor 9; threshold £2,222,222) and pay on 14 March, 14 June, 14 September and 14 December GY2. No group payment arrangement exists yet.
>
> | | TES (£) | TEL (£) |
> |---|---|---|
> | Final CT for GY2 | (forecast included the depot gain; sale slipped to 30 April GY3) | 2,020,000 |
> | Overpaid / (underpaid) in total | 569,375 (tax at 25% on the expected £2,277,500 gain) | (400,000) |
> | Per instalment | 142,343.75 over | 100,000 short |
> | Refund surrendered (joint notice before repayment) | (400,000) | +400,000 |
> | Repaid to TES | 169,375 | — |
>
> TEL paid four instalments of £405,000 (£1,620,000) on an early forecast; a stronger second half took its liability to £2,020,000. Because TES's overpayment on each date (£142,343.75) exceeds TEL's shortfall on that date (£100,000), reg 9 leaves TEL with no QIP shortfall at any date.
>
> **Interest saved (simplified):** assume that without the surrender TEL's shortfalls and TES's overpayments would each have been settled on the normal due date, 1 October GY3; months to the nearest month from each instalment date: 19, 16, 13 and 10 (58 in total).
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | TEL's QIP debit interest avoided: £100,000 × 6.25% × 58/12 | 30,208 |
> | TES's credit interest forgone: £100,000 × 3.50% × 58/12 | (16,917) |
> | Net saving to the group (before tax effects) | **13,291** |
>
> Rule of thumb: £400,000 × (6.25% − 3.50%) = **£11,000 a year**.

> **Exam lens: group payment arrangements and refund surrenders**
> - **Grade:** CT self-assessment 1; Part 22 CTA 2010 (excluding Chs 3 and 8 and ss 990–995) 1.
> - **Traps:** the GPA group is 51% with the same accounting date, the refund surrender group is the 75% group relief group with the same AP; the surrender notice must precede the refund; a GPA does not transfer liability.

## The enquiry window.

HMRC may open an enquiry into a return only by giving notice within a window (Sch 18 para 24):

| Return | Enquiry window |
|---|---|
| Delivered on or before the filing date, company **not** in a group, or in a **small** group | 12 months after the day the return was delivered |
| Delivered on or before the filing date by a member of a group that is **not a small group** (Companies Act 2006 ss 383, 474) | 12 months after the **filing date** |
| Delivered late, or amended | To the quarter day after the first anniversary of the day of delivery (or amendment) |

Large groups file early; without the group rule, early filing would shorten HMRC's time to look. With it, filing early buys a large group nothing in finality. Each return has its own window; once notice is given in time, the enquiry itself can run on well beyond it.

**M25 Q6** combined the rules: a company in a large group with a 15-month POA. Candidates applied the small-company limits and missed the long period. In worked example 3.1, both returns are due 31 December Year C and both windows close 31 December Year D: for AP 1, 27 months after its own year end.

**The story.** Calder's return for its 9-month AP to 31 December GY3 is due by **31 December GY4**. Calder is in a large group, so the window runs to **31 December GY5**, however early it files. On **1 June GY5** HMRC opens an enquiry into the price of the GY3 sale to TVS (chapter 27).

An enquiry ends with a **closure notice** stating HMRC's conclusions and making any amendments to the return; the company can appeal. The tax function's best defence is the file built when the return was prepared: computations, reasoning on uncertain points, the evidence behind transfer prices.

## Discovery.

Once the window has closed, the return is normally final; the exception is a **discovery assessment** where HMRC discovers that tax has been lost (profits omitted, relief excessive). Sch 18 para 46 time limits run from the **end of the AP**:

| Behaviour | Time limit |
|---|---|
| Ordinary | **4 years** |
| Loss of tax brought about carelessly | **6 years** |
| Brought about deliberately; failure to notify chargeability; certain DOTAS failures (including failure to notify a scheme reference number) | **20 years** |

For a delivered return the statute also protects the company: in broad terms, a discovery assessment needs careless or deliberate conduct, or a loss of tax that an officer could not reasonably have been expected to be aware of from the information made available before the window closed (Sch 18 discovery provisions, paras 41 onwards). Hence the practice of disclosing contentious positions in or with the return: full disclosure narrows the door through which discovery can come.

**Misconception: "once the enquiry window closes, we are safe".** Safe from an enquiry, not from discovery. Discovery needs something new or something hidden; an enquiry needs no reason at all.

> **Worked example 3.7: Calder's 9-month AP to 31 December GY3 (invented)**
>
> | Limit | Last date for a discovery assessment |
> |---|---|
> | Ordinary (4 years) | 31 December GY7 |
> | Careless (6 years) | 31 December GY9 |
> | Deliberate (20 years) | 31 December GY23 |

**Records.** A company must keep the records needed to deliver a correct return; failure can cost up to **£3,000** per AP (Sch 18 para 23). In a group, "records" also means TP documentation (chapter 27) and the reasoning behind each judgement; the UTT notification (chapter 4) shares the same logic of telling HMRC early.

> **Exam lens: enquiries and discovery**
> - **Grade:** disclosure and discovery 1; CT self-assessment 1.
> - **Past appearances:** M25 Q6 (10 marks, 5/5: large group enquiry windows with a 15-month POA; discovery for careless behaviour; poor); M24 Q3 (15 marks; part (c) 3 marks on HMRC enquiry/discovery on a loss return).
> - **Traps:** small-group time limits applied to a large group; ignoring the long POA; counting discovery limits from the filing date rather than the AP end.

## Late, wrong or silent.

**Late filing: flat-rate penalties (Sch 18 para 17, as amended by FA 2026 s 265).** For returns with a filing date on or after **1 April 2026**:

| Failure | Within 3 months after the filing date | Otherwise |
|---|---|---|
| Late return | **£200** (was £100) | **£400** (was £200) |
| Third successive failure (liable to a fixed penalty for each of the two preceding periods) | **£1,000** (was £500) | **£2,000** (was £1,000) |

The statute says £200 then £400 in total (GOV.UK's "another £200" means the same). FA 2026 s 265 also lets HMRC change the amounts by regulations.

> **Real-calendar illustration (labelled):** year ended 31 March 2025 → filing date 31 March 2026 → old amounts; year ended 30 April 2025 → filing date 30 April 2026 → new amounts.

**Late filing: tax-geared penalties (Sch 18 para 18).** If the return is not delivered within **18 months** after the end of the AP (or by the filing date if later): **10%** of the tax unpaid at that time; **20%** if delivered more than **2 years** after the AP end.

> **Story: Brackenwell's third return (invented).** At completion on 1 July GY2, Brackenwell's return for its year to 31 December GY1 is still unfiled. It carries the ERIS payable credit claim of **£943,950** (chapter 10). Its two previous returns, from before the story, were both late, so the next late return would be a third successive failure: **£1,000**, or **£2,000** if more than 3 months late. Tom Hesketh's team files well before the filing date, **31 December GY2**. The amount is small; the signal to HMRC about a newly acquired company's controls is not.

**Inaccuracy (FA 2007 Sch 24).** Careless or deliberate inaccuracies in a return: maximum **30%** (careless), **70%** (deliberate), **100%** (deliberate and concealed) of the potential lost revenue, reduced for disclosure.

**Failure to notify (FA 2008 Sch 41).** Penalties for failing to notify chargeability, geared to the potential lost revenue and behaviour.

**Interest (rates in force when this chapter was written; they move with Bank Rate).**

| | Rate | From |
|---|---|---|
| Late payment interest (Bank Rate + 4%) | **7.75%** | 9 January 2026 |
| Repayment interest (Bank Rate − 1%, minimum 0.5%) | **2.75%** | 9 January 2026 |
| QIP underpayments | **6.25%** | 29 December 2025 |
| QIP overpayments | **3.50%** | 29 December 2025 |

**Late payment penalty pending further appeal (FA 2026 s 264).** FA 2009 Sch 56 item 18 is extended to amounts payable under TMA s 56(3)(b), tax due after a tribunal decision while a further appeal is pending, for amounts payable on or after **1 April 2026**. A group that loses at the tribunal and appeals further can no longer sit on the tax without that risk.

> **Exam lens: penalties and interest**
> - **Grade:** penalties and interest on under/overpayments/failure to notify etc 1.
> - **Traps:** using the pre-FA 2026 amounts (an FA 2026 sitting will expect £200/£400 and £1,000/£2,000); describing the persistent penalty as an extra £1,000 on top; forgetting the tax-geared 10%/20%; treating QIP interest as a penalty.

## The rest of the calendar.

Returns and instalments are the spine of the calendar, but a large group's year is crowded with other dates, each owned by its own chapter:

| Obligation | Chapter |
|---|---|
| R&D claim notification (first claim, or first for 3 years: within 6 months after the POA ends) and the additional information form | 10 |
| Interest restriction return and reporting company | 28 |
| Pillar Two registration, GloBE information return and top-up tax returns | 30 |
| Country-by-country report; TP master file and local file | 27 |
| SAO notification and certificate; tax strategy; uncertain tax treatment notification | 4 |

> **Going further: three planning choices**
> 1. **The accounting date of an acquired company.** Leaving Calder on 31 March kept it out of the group payment arrangement and gave the tax function two calendars. The 9-month AP aligned it but created a forecasting problem. A long POA instead (up to 18 months) would have pushed the filing date out but not the payment dates.
> 2. **Cash.** Joining a group moves a company from large to very large one AP later (on HMRC's counting date); treasury needs the six-instalment year in the forecast.
> 3. **Interest management.** A group payment arrangement, refund surrenders made before the refund is paid, and forecasts updated before each instalment are worth real money in a group the size of Tarnmoor. None changes a pound of tax; all change what the tax costs.
>
> **A typical Tarnmoor year (invented):** 31 December companies pay through the GPA on 14 March, June, September and December; Calder (until GY4) runs its own calendar; returns are due by 31 December after the year, with group relief claims and surrenders lined up across them; every new company enters the calendar on the day it arrives, with its own counting date for associates and its own history of late returns.

## How the examiner tests it.

Administration is a regular 10–15 mark question or the tail of a computation (exam-intel: compliance and governance in N23 Q5, M25 Q3, M25 Q6, M24 Q3(c), N24 Q5, N25 Q4(a), M26 Q1(a), N23 Q1(b)).

- **M25 Q2** (15 marks, 10/5): CT computation then very large instalments with changing forecasts: badly done (no truing up; very large rules unknown).
- **M25 Q6** (10 marks, 5/5): enquiry windows for a large-group company with a 15-month POA; discovery for careless behaviour: poor (small-group limits applied; long period missed).
- **N24 Q5** (20 marks): UK compliance after a US group buys a UK SME: filing and QIPs now that the company is in a large group (4 marks), alongside CbC, TP, CIR and stamp duty.
- **N25 Q4** (20 marks, 4/8/8): admin on incorporation of a new subsidiary: notification, APs, the filing date following the POA.

**Style:** technical prose and computation; no letter or report format; 0.5–1 mark per point, so state each date, threshold and conclusion separately; the rubric wants workings to the nearest month and pound, so give full dates. **Layout:** threshold working (limit ÷ divisor × months/12) → regime → table of due dates and amounts → truing up and interest. **Where the question says "administrative obligations":** notification, filing, payment, enquiry, then penalties for each.

## What to take away.

An AP lasts at most 12 months; a 15-month POA gives two APs and two returns; a change of ownership does not end an AP. The filing date follows the POA; payment (9 months and 1 day after each AP, outside QIPs) does not. Large (> £1.5m) pay in months 7, 10, 13, 16; very large (> £20m) in months 3, 6, 9, 12; both divided by 1 + associates and scaled for short APs; for QIPs, HMRC counts associates at the day before the AP begins. Large companies have a year of grace (≤ £10m, divided; not large before); very large have none; no QIPs if the liability is £10,000 or less. True up when forecasts change; interest 6.25%/3.50%; penalties only for knowing or reckless underpayment (up to twice the interest); the RDEC does not reduce QIPs. GPAs (51%, same accounting date) and refund surrenders (75%, same AP, joint notice before the refund) cut the interest cost. Enquiry window: 12 months from delivery, but from the filing date for a company in a group that is not small. Discovery: 4/6/20 years from the AP end. Late filing: £200/£400; £1,000/£2,000 for a third successive failure; 10%/20% tax-geared.

*The calendar tells a group when to pay and when to file. The next chapter asks who answers for it: the senior accounting officer, the board that publishes a tax strategy, and the rules that make large groups tell HMRC what they are unsure about.*

# Introduction.

The prologue followed one loan through three courts and found two answers. Keep that picture in mind, because it is a fair picture of the ground ahead. A larger company is not a small company with more noughts on the end. It borrows from its own relatives. It owns companies that own companies. It trades across borders, reports to investors, and answers to a board that must publish its approach to tax. Almost every rule you met in *The Living Law* (TKS) still applies to it. But around those rules sits a second layer of law, written for scale.

This introduction is a map. It sets out the four questions the book keeps asking, the paper the book serves, the way the chapters are arranged, the invented group whose story carries the law, and how the two editions of each chapter work. It ends with some advice on using the book alongside the question practice you already have. The law throughout is the law for **FY2026** (1 April 2026 to 31 March 2027), under **Finance Act 2026** (Royal Assent 18 March 2026).

## Five ways the larger company is different.

**1. Scale.** Size switches rules on. The ladder, for FY2026 (chapter 1 builds it rung by rung):

| Threshold | What it switches on | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Augmented profits > £1.5m (÷ 1 + associated companies) | Large company: quarterly instalment payments (QIPs), months 7, 10, 13, 16 | 1, 3 |
| Augmented profits > £20m (÷ 1 + associated companies) | Very large company: QIPs in months 3, 6, 9, 12 | 1, 3 |
| Net interest > £2m a year | Corporate interest restriction (CIR) can bite | 28 |
| £5m | Deductions allowance for carried-forward losses (one per group) | 14 |
| UK turnover > £200m or balance sheet > £2bn | Senior accounting officer (SAO), published tax strategy, uncertain tax treatment (UTT) notification | 4 |
| Leaving the SME category | Transfer pricing applies | 27 |
| Consolidated revenue ≥ / > €750m | Country-by-country reporting, TP records (Master File and Local File), Pillar Two | 27, 30 |

**2. The group.** UK law taxes each company separately; there is **no group return** for corporation tax. Yet a group plans, borrows and invests as one business, and the law builds bridges between its companies: group relief for losses, no gain/no loss transfers of assets, group roll-over, a shared AIA and deductions allowance, shared thresholds. Each bridge has its own entry test, and the tests do not match (chapter 1's "guest lists").

**3. The border.** A large group earns profits in many countries and moves money, goods, people and ideas between them. Most of the second half of this book is about the rules that decide where those profits are taxed, and the rules that stop them drifting to wherever tax is lowest.

**4. Governance.** A large company's tax is not only computed. It is managed, certified and reported. HMRC's Large Business directorate says it works with around 2,000 of the largest businesses; its customer compliance managers assess each group's tax risk and how well the group manages it (HMRC's Tax Compliance Risk Management manual, TCRM1000). The law adds personal duties for a named officer (the SAO) and a criminal offence for failing to prevent the facilitation of tax evasion (Criminal Finances Act 2017 ss 45–46).

**5. The accounts.** For a larger company the accounts are not a starting point that tax quickly leaves behind. They decide the timing of loan relationship profits, intangible asset profits and much else (CTA 2009 s 46 and Parts 5, 6 and 8). And the accounts must also show a **tax charge** that investors read, current and deferred, which is a different number from the tax bill (chapter 6).

## Four questions.

Those five differences produce four questions, and the book asks them in every chapter.

1. **One taxpayer or many?** The law taxes each company on its own profits, yet it lets losses travel, assets move and gains be rolled over across the group. Where does it build those bridges, and where does it refuse? Chapters 1, 15 and 17 carry this question furthest, and the CIR (chapter 28) and the global minimum tax (chapter 30) push it in a new direction.
2. **Where are profits made, and what stops them drifting?** Residence, permanent establishments, treaties and the foreign branch exemption decide where profits are taxed. Transfer pricing, controlled foreign companies, hybrid mismatches, the CIR and the global minimum tax stop them moving to where they are not. Part Four is built around this question.
3. **What does the law follow: the accounts, the deal or its own purpose?** Tax starts from the accounts. It respects many commercial transactions, such as share exchanges and demergers, on their own terms. And then, again and again, it asks why a company did something, and overrides both the accounts and the deal when the answer is tax. The prologue's loan was the first example. It will not be the last.
4. **How does a large group govern its tax, and what must an adviser spot?** The CIOT says candidates for this paper "are not expected to have deep knowledge of all tax areas but should be able to identify what the potential tax issues are". So every chapter asks what the tax function must do, by when, and what a good adviser would flag.

## The paper this book serves.

This book is written for the **CTA Advanced Technical paper, Taxation of Larger Companies and Groups** (LCG). Until 2023 it was called Taxation of Major Corporates.

| Feature | Detail |
|---|---|

| Shape | Normally 6 questions of 10, 15 or 20 marks; 100 marks; usually three 20s, two 15s and one 10 |
| Pass mark | 50% |

## Where *The Living Law* ends.

This book assumes that you know *The Living Law*, the earlier book written for the Tax Knowledge and Skills paper. It does not teach those foundations twice. Where a chapter builds on one, it recaps it in one to three sentences and names the chapter ("*The Living Law*, chapter 24, on groups"). Then it goes further.

Going further means two things:

- **Taught again, in full, at Advanced Technical depth:** topics *The Living Law* touched, such as group relief, loan relationships, R&D, company gains and the foreign branch exemption, because the AT examiner expects mastery, not recall.
- **Taught from first principles:** topics *The Living Law* only signposted: transfer pricing, CFCs, the CIR, hybrid mismatches, the global minimum tax, company migration, deferred tax and demergers.

## Tarnmoor, an invented group.

Most chapters follow one group of companies, **Tarnmoor**. **Everything about Tarnmoor is invented for teaching**: its companies, people, numbers and its two overseas countries, **Vallaria** and **Marrovia**. Ireland is real, and so are its 12.5% trading rate and its treaty with the UK (as modified by the MLI), but Tarnmoor's Irish company is not.

**Tarnmoor plc** is a listed engineering group with its head office in Leeds and no controlling shareholder. It was built to match the examiner's description of a typical scenario, and to be large enough to switch on every regime the paper tests (GY1 consolidated revenue £1,180m). Its main trading company, Tarnmoor Engineering, makes industrial valves, actuators and pumps. Around it sit a property company, a treasury company, a joint venture (Helmside), overseas subsidiaries in Vallaria, Marrovia and Ireland, and the companies it buys and sells as the story runs.

The story begins in chapter 1, when Tarnmoor buys **Calder Valve Engineering** for **£32m**. If you have read *The Living Law*, you know Calder: the invented valve maker where **Dan Hartley** worked as an engineer, and his story continues here. So does his sister **Jess**'s. Some years after the group's story begins, Tarnmoor buys her business, Ridgeway, and chapter 31 tells that deal.

**Group Years.** Tarnmoor lives in Group Years (GY1, GY2, ...), not calendar years. Group Years are Tarnmoor's accounting years to 31 December; GY1 is the year in which Calder joins. No calendar year is ever attached to a Tarnmoor event, and **every Tarnmoor computation applies FY2026 law**, whichever Group Year the story has reached. Real rules start on real dates (for example, "accounting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2026"). Where a chapter needs to teach a real commencement date, it uses a **labelled example with real dates**, outside the story. That keeps the story simple and the dates honest.

The story is the spine of the book, not its cage. When the examiner tests a variation the story never reaches, a chapter says "suppose" and works a labelled example. Those examples never become part of the story.

## Two editions.

Every chapter comes in two editions.

**The audio script** is written to be heard once. Numbers are spoken as words, and worked examples are told as stories with a few memorable figures. Each script ends with **"What to take away"**, a spoken recap. The spoken conventions:

| Written (reading edition) | Spoken (audio script) |
|---|---|
| CTA 2009 / CTA 2010 | "the two thousand and nine Act" / "the twenty ten Act" (full name first in each chapter) |
| TIOPA 2010 | "the Taxation, International and Other Provisions, Act twenty ten", then "the International Act" |
| TCGA 1992 | "the Gains Act" |
| CAA 2001 / TMA 1970 | "the Capital Allowances Act" / "the Management Act" |
| CTA 2010 s 1141 | "section eleven forty one" (four-digit sections in pairs) |
| TCGA 1992 s 171; CTA 2009 s 441 | "section one seven one"; "section four four one" (practitioners' digit-by-digit names) |
| HMRC; CFC | "H M R C"; "controlled foreign company", then "C F C" (spaced letters, declared first) |
| GAAR; DOTAS; GloBE | Spoken as words |
| £1.35m; 14.99%; €750m | "one point three five million pounds"; "fourteen point nine nine per cent"; "seven hundred and fifty million euros" |

**The reading edition** is the same chapter for the page: digits, statutory references ("s 441 CTA 2009"; "TIOPA 2010 s 371BC"), standard case citations (*BlackRock HoldCo 5, LLC v HMRC* [2024] EWCA Civ 330), and every computation laid out as the examiner expects, with **£ or £000, never both, in each table**. It adds three kinds of box:

- **Worked example**: the full numbers behind each story, and labelled extra examples.
- **Going further**: planning, the tax function's tasks and deadlines, interaction between regimes, judgement and ethics.
- **Exam lens**: each topic's grade on the 2026 grid, its past appearances (sitting, question, marks), what the examiners said, the style, the traps and the layout.

Each reading edition ends with a **Key rules and figures** table and a **Statutory and case references** list.

**Status words** are the same in both editions:

| Words | Meaning |
|---|---|
| "The law says" | Statute |
| "The courts have held" | Case law |
| "HMRC's view is" | Guidance: not law |
| "Disputed" / "being litigated" | Not settled |
| "Announced" | A government statement |
| "Proposed, not law" | A consultation or draft legislation Parliament has not passed |
| "In our invented case" | Tarnmoor and the Hartleys |

## How to use this book.

The book teaches. It does not test. There are no practice questions here, because you have the CIOT's past papers and your own question bank, and those are the right tools for practice.

A good rhythm:

1. **Listen** to a chapter for the story and the reasons.
2. **Read** its reading edition with a pen, working each computation yourself.
3. **Sit a past question** on the same topic, under time (about 2.1 minutes a mark), and mark it against the CIOT's suggested answer and marking guide.
4. **Read the examiners' report** for that sitting, and, from May 2024, the **banded example scripts**. Comparing a scraped pass with a strong answer to the same question is one of the best lessons available; chapter 32 shows what to look for.

Practise on the student legislation product you will use on the day, **Croner-i or Tolley**, because in the exam the legislation is your only reference. And practise with the right numbers:

| Item | 2026 tax tables (FA 2025) | Law for a 2027 sitting (FA 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Main pool WDA | 18% | **14%** (chargeable periods beginning on or after 1 April 2026; hybrid rate for straddling periods) |
| 40% FYA (new main-rate plant) | — | **Available** (expenditure from 1 January 2026) |
| Special rate WDA | 6% | 6% |

When the 2027 tables appear, check every rate against them.

Finally, keep the status words in your ear. Several things described in this book are **proposals, not law**. A draft Finance Bill 2026-27 was published on **13 July 2026**, and a Budget is due on **28 October 2026**. None of that changes the law for your sitting. The conclusion says what may be coming, and the note on sources says how to keep up.

So the book starts where Tarnmoor's story starts, on 1 April in GY1, with a family firm that becomes one company among many.

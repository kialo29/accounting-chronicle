Chapter nineteen: Reconstructions, demergers and distributions.

On the twenty third of June, twenty twenty six, H M R C and the Treasury published a consultation with a long title. It was called Modernising the taxation of distributions and repayments of capital from companies. Buried in its chapter on demergers was a candid admission. The relief that lets a trading group split itself in two, it said, is not currently well-used.

That is a striking thing for a government to say about its own law. The demerger rules have existed since nineteen eighty. They promise something remarkable. A group can hand a whole subsidiary to its shareholders, worth perhaps hundreds of millions of pounds, and nobody is treated as having received a dividend or sold anything. So why do advisers so often go round them? The answer lies in their conditions, and in the other routes the law offers. This chapter is about all of those routes, and about the rule that sits underneath them, the definition of a distribution.

Here is the question to hold on to. How can a business be divided between companies, or between owners, without the tax system treating the division as a sale or a dividend? And what stops that freedom being used to dress up income as capital?

This is the fifth chapter of Part Three. Company distributions, distributions received, reconstructions, demergers, the transfer of trade rules and transactions in securities are all core topics on the paper. None of them has been examined as a full question in the sittings from twenty twenty three to twenty twenty six, which is exactly why a prepared candidate should know them. The Living Law, chapter twenty, taught what a distribution is in outline, and chapter twenty two told the Leekes case on losses after a transfer of trade. The Living Law did not teach demergers or transactions in securities at all, so we start those from first principles.

Most of the law is in the Corporation Tax Act twenty ten, which I will call the twenty ten Act. Some sits in the Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, the two thousand and nine Act, and some in the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act nineteen ninety two, the Gains Act. We apply the law for the financial year twenty twenty six, including the changes made by Finance Act twenty twenty six to company reconstructions. The examples use our invented group, Tarnmoor. Its companies, people and numbers are invented for teaching, as they are throughout this book.

What the law calls a distribution.

Begin with the word itself, because every route in this chapter is a way of avoiding it, or of making it harmless. A distribution is a payment by a company to its members, in their capacity as members. Section one thousand of the twenty ten Act lists the categories, lettered A to H.

Category A is any dividend, including a capital dividend. Category B is any other distribution of the company's assets in respect of shares, except so far as it repays capital or is matched by new consideration given by the member. Categories C and D catch bonus issues of redeemable shares and of securities, where no new consideration is given. They stop a company issuing paper that can later be cashed in, as a disguised dividend.

Categories E and F reach into debt. Category E is interest on a security that exceeds a reasonable commercial return. Only the excess is a distribution. Category F is interest on special securities, such as securities whose interest depends on the company's results. Category G covers transfers of assets or liabilities between a company and its members, under section ten twenty. Category H covers bonus issues following a repayment of share capital.

Why does this matter to a large group, where dividends between companies are usually tax free? Because the label changes the payer's position as well as the recipient's. A distribution is never deductible for the company that pays it. So if interest is recharacterised as a distribution, the payer loses its deduction. Suppose, for this example, a company borrows ten million pounds from a connected lender at twelve per cent when seven per cent would be commercial. The excess, five hundred thousand pounds a year, is a category E distribution. The payer loses a deduction worth one hundred and twenty five thousand pounds a year. And as we will see in a moment, the recipient's exemption does not cover category E or F amounts.

Section ten twenty deserves a moment of its own. If a company transfers an asset to a member for less than its market value, the member's benefit, so far as it exceeds what the member gave, is a distribution. There used to be an exception for transfers between a company and its group members. Finance Act twenty twelve repealed it. So an undervalue transfer from a subsidiary up to its parent is a distribution of the shortfall, even inside a group. Section ten twenty only looks upwards, at transfers to members. A transfer between sister companies is not within it, although the gains rules and transfer pricing still have to be checked.

Company law runs alongside. A company that pays a distribution must have distributable reserves, and it cannot return capital to its shareholders except by a formal reduction. The Supreme Court considered the boundary in Progress Property Company Limited versus Moorgarth Group Limited, decided in twenty ten, with Lord Walker giving the leading judgment. One group company had sold a subsidiary to a fellow subsidiary at a price that later proved too low. The court held that a genuine commercial sale, made at arm's length in good faith, is not an unlawful distribution just because hindsight reveals an undervalue. Substance, not a retrospective valuation, decides.

One more definition finishes the picture. Under section ten twenty seven A, a reserve created by reducing share capital is treated for corporation tax as profits, not capital. So a company cannot reduce its capital and then call the payout a repayment of capital. And under section ten thirty, a distribution in respect of share capital in a winding up is not an income distribution at all. It is capital. Hold that thought, because it matters when a company is closed down.

Receiving a distribution.

Now turn to the company that receives the money. Since July two thousand and nine, Part nine A of the two thousand and nine Act has charged every distribution a company receives, and then exempted most of them. The structure is a charge with a wide set of doors out of it.

For a company that is not small, and every Tarnmoor company is large, a distribution is exempt if three things are true. It must fall into an exempt class. It must not be a category E or F distribution, the disguised interest we just met. And no deduction may be given to anyone outside the United Kingdom for the payment. That last condition stops a payment being deductible abroad and exempt here.

The exempt classes are wide. Distributions from a company the recipient controls are exempt. So are distributions on non-redeemable ordinary shares, and distributions on portfolio holdings of less than ten per cent. So too are dividends from what the law calls relevant profits, meaning profits that do not come from transactions designed to reduce United Kingdom tax. A joint venture dividend can also fall in the controlled company class. Two parties must together control the payer. The recipient must hold at least forty per cent, and the other party between forty and fifty five per cent.

The anti-avoidance rules that follow, sections nine three one J to nine three one Q, target schemes that manufacture an exempt class, quasi-preference shares that behave like debt, and payments for distributions. Small companies have a separate, narrower exemption that depends on where the payer is resident.

There is one election to know. A company can elect that a particular distribution is not exempt. It must do so by the second anniversary of the end of the accounting period in which the distribution is received. Why would anyone choose to be taxed? Because some foreign countries reduce their withholding tax only if the dividend is taxable in the recipient's hands. Chapter twenty five, on going abroad, works through that choice.

In our invented case the rules almost never bite. Tarnmoor plc receives dividends from its wholly owned subsidiaries under the controlled company class. Its twelve per cent stake in the listed instrument maker Coldwater Instruments pays ordinary dividends on non-redeemable ordinary shares, also exempt. And when Tarnmoor Actuators pays Tarnmoor Engineering a two million pound dividend before its sale in Group Year Six, a story chapter twenty tells, that is exempt too. The exam work in this area lies in spotting the exceptions. Look for a payment that is really interest, a payer that gets a foreign deduction, or a dividend engineered to fall into a class.

The quiet end of a company.

Large groups accumulate dormant companies the way old houses accumulate keys. Each one must file, and each one sits on the senior accounting officer's list. Sooner or later someone asks why it still exists.

There are two ways to close a solvent company. A formal liquidation appoints a liquidator. Then section ten thirty applies, and what the shareholders receive in the winding up is capital, not income. The other way is cheaper. The directors apply to the registrar of companies to strike the company off. For many years, by concession, H M R C let shareholders treat a distribution made before an informal strike-off as capital too. Since the first of March twenty twelve the rule has been in the statute, in section ten thirty A of the twenty ten Act, and it has a limit.

A distribution made in anticipation of striking off is not a distribution, so it is capital in the shareholder's hands, if two conditions are met. The company must intend to collect its debts and settle its liabilities, or have done so. And the total of such distributions must not exceed twenty five thousand pounds. Section ten thirty B adds a sting. If the company is not dissolved within two years of the distribution, or does not collect its debts and pay its liabilities, the distribution is treated as an income distribution after all.

The rule was written with individual shareholders in mind, for whom capital usually beats income. For a corporate shareholder the arithmetic can run the other way. That is the misconception to clear away first. Capital treatment is not always a favour.

In our invented case, Tarnmoor Pumps Limited has been dormant throughout the story. In Group Year Seven the group decides to strike it off. It holds eighteen thousand pounds of reserves in cash. Tarnmoor plc's cost for its shares was one hundred pounds, paid after December twenty seventeen, so there is no indexation. Before the application, Tarnmoor Pumps distributes its eighteen thousand pounds to Tarnmoor plc. Because the amount is within twenty five thousand pounds, it is capital.

So Tarnmoor plc has a chargeable gain of seventeen thousand, nine hundred pounds, and corporation tax at twenty five per cent of four thousand, four hundred and seventy five pounds. The shareholding exemption cannot help, because Tarnmoor Pumps does not trade. Had the same cash come up as an ordinary dividend, it would have been exempt under the controlled company class. Tom Hesketh, the group's invented head of tax, notes the point in his file. Paying a dividend first would have needed board approval and a check against the value shifting rule in chapter seventeen, all to save less than five thousand pounds of tax. The group pays the tax.

There is a nice irony in the two year rule. If the strike-off stalled, section ten thirty B would turn the payment into an income distribution, which for Tarnmoor plc would be exempt. A sanction for individuals is a relief for a parent company. Notice also what the rule leaves out. The share capital itself cannot lawfully be paid out before dissolution without a formal reduction. Anything left in the company when it is struck off passes to the Crown.

Moving a trade inside the group.

Now we move from closing companies to reshaping them. A group often wants to move a trade from one company to another. It may be tidying up after an acquisition, or preparing a business for sale. If the tax system treated every such move as a cessation and a fresh start, the old company would face balancing charges on its plant, and its unused trading losses would die with the trade.

Chapter one of Part twenty two of the twenty ten Act prevents that, for transfers within common ownership. It applies automatically. No claim is needed. Two conditions must be met.

The first is the ownership condition. At least a seventy five per cent interest in the trade must belong to the same persons at some time in the year before the transfer. It must also belong to them at the moment of transfer or at some time in the two years after. A trade carried on by a company belongs to the company's owners, so inside a group the test is usually passed through the common parent. Notice the shape of the windows. The test can be met at the very moment of transfer, so a later sale of the new company does not undo it.

The second is the tax condition. Throughout, the trade must be carried on by a company within the charge to United Kingdom corporation tax. A trade moving to a company outside the charge is not covered.

When both are met, three things follow. First, capital allowances continue. The successor is treated as if it had carried on the trade all along, and the transfer itself produces no allowances and no balancing charges. Plant moves at tax written down value. Chapter nine showed how that sits with the succession rules in the Capital Allowances Act. Second, the successor takes over the predecessor's unused trading losses and can carry them forward, subject to any current year or carry-back claims the predecessor makes first. Third, the predecessor cannot make a terminal loss claim to carry its final losses back three years. The losses go forward with the trade instead.

The rule also works for part of a trade. A division moved into a new subsidiary is treated as a separate trade for these purposes, with receipts, expenses and assets apportioned on a just and reasonable basis.

What can the successor do with inherited losses? The Leekes case, which The Living Law, chapter twenty two, told, answers that for older losses. Leekes Limited bought a loss-making furniture retailer, Coles, and hived its trade up into its own. The case was Leekes Limited versus H M R C, decided by the Court of Appeal on the twenty third of May twenty eighteen, with Lord Justice Henderson giving the judgment. The court held that the inherited losses could be set only against profits of the trade that had been transferred. The successor had to stream its profits. Since twenty seventeen, further sections deal with losses that arose after the first of April twenty seventeen, and the change in ownership rules in chapter fourteen add their own limits when a company changes hands.

The liabilities left behind.

There is one trap in this chapter of the law, and it is a good one. Picture a company whose trade has made heavy losses and which owes a great deal of money. It transfers the trade, with the best assets, to a fellow subsidiary. It leaves its debts behind. Its creditors are never paid, and they may claim relief for their bad debts. Meanwhile the successor uses the losses that the unpaid debts helped to create. Relief would be given twice.

Section nine hundred and forty five stops it. Call the liabilities the predecessor keeps, and does not pass to the successor, L. Call the assets it keeps, plus any consideration the successor pays for the trade, A. If L exceeds A, the excess is taken off the losses the successor can use. If the excess is larger than the losses, nothing passes.

Here is an example, invented for this purpose and not part of the Tarnmoor story. A predecessor has four million pounds of unused trading losses. It keeps liabilities of three million pounds. It keeps assets of one point two million pounds, and the successor pays it eight hundred thousand pounds for the trade. L exceeds A by one million pounds. So only three million pounds of the losses pass to the successor. The full working is in the reading edition.

H M R C's guidance calls this the relevant liabilities restriction, and it treats it as the main defence where a subsidiary is sold soon after receiving a trade. Tribunals have applied it strictly, arguing over whether particular items, such as a tax repayment due or a director's loan account, count as assets or liabilities.

A second, older rule sits in Chapter two of Part twenty two. Suppose a company ceases a trade and another company takes it over, and a main purpose of the cessation is to obtain a balancing allowance. Then the allowances and charges pass to the successor as if the trade had continued. A group cannot manufacture a loss on its plant by stopping and restarting.

The actuators hive-down.

Now the rules at work. Early in Group Year Six, Tarnmoor Engineering decides to sell its actuators business. The buyer, a United States group called Brennock Industries, is as invented as everything else here. The business is part of Tarnmoor Engineering's single company, so the group first creates a new subsidiary, Tarnmoor Actuators Limited. On the first of February in Group Year Six, Tarnmoor Engineering transfers the actuators trade and its assets into it. On the thirty first of December the shares are sold.

That two-step, the hive-down, is a staple of corporate deals, and each tax regime meets it differently. The trade transfer is a transfer of part of a trade within common ownership. Before the transfer Tarnmoor Engineering owned the trade. Immediately after, Tarnmoor Engineering owns it through its new subsidiary. Both companies are within United Kingdom corporation tax. So Chapter one of Part twenty two applies. The plant passes at tax written down value with no balancing charge. The actuators business has no unused losses, so there is nothing to pass and no liabilities restriction to test.

The other assets each have their own rule, and other chapters own them. The factory, which cost Tarnmoor Engineering five million pounds and is worth seven and a half million, passes at no gain, no loss under the Gains Act group rules, chapter seventeen. The actuator patents, with a tax written down value of one point two million pounds, pass tax-neutrally under the intangibles rules, chapter eleven. Stamp duty land tax group relief covers the factory, until the sale claws it back, chapter twenty one.

Why not sell the assets directly? Because a sale of shares by a group company can fall within the shareholding exemption, and a sale of assets cannot. The hive-down turns a business into shares. The law accepts that, with conditions. The shareholding exemption needs a twelve month holding, and a special rule treats it as met where the hived-down assets were used in the group's trade before. Chapters eighteen and twenty apply that rule to this sale.

The hive-down is also where the parts of this book meet. A trainee who sees only one regime in it will miss most of the marks. The plant, the losses, the factory, the patents, the stamp taxes and the eventual share sale are six separate questions, answered by six separate rule books, about one commercial event.

What makes a reconstruction.

So far the business has stayed with the same owners. A reconstruction goes further. It reorganises a business between companies and their shareholders, and it may split one company's business into two.

For gains purposes the Gains Act defines a scheme of reconstruction in Schedule five A A. There are four conditions. A scheme qualifies if it meets the first two and either the third or the fourth.

Condition one is that the successor company issues ordinary shares only to the holders of ordinary shares in the original company, or of the relevant classes. Condition two is that each shareholder's entitlement is the same per share, so the shareholders stand in the same proportions as before. Condition three is continuity. The whole or substantially the whole of the business must be carried on by the successor company or companies afterwards. Condition four is the alternative. The scheme is carried out under a court-approved compromise or arrangement under the Companies Act, and no part of the business goes to anyone else.

Two Gains Act sections then give relief, at two levels. At the shareholders' level, section one three six treats the shareholders as exchanging their old shares for the new ones, with no disposal. The new shares stand in the shoes of the old. At the company level, section one three nine treats a transfer of the whole or part of a company's business to another company under a scheme of reconstruction as taking place at no gain, no loss. Three conditions matter. The transferor must receive no part of the consideration, apart from the transferee taking on its liabilities. The assets must stay within the United Kingdom charge. And trading stock is excluded.

Think of it as a family business splitting into two shops. The same family owns both shops in the same proportions, and both keep trading. Nobody has sold anything. The law says so, in four conditions.

The same commercial event is also tested by stamp duty and by stamp duty land tax, each with its own reconstruction relief and its own purpose test. Chapter twenty one deals with those. For the exam, train yourself to ask four questions of every reconstruction. What happens for the shareholders, for the company, for stamp duty, and for land?

A purpose test for every reconstruction.

Until recently, section one three nine had a narrow anti-avoidance rule. Relief was denied unless the transfer was for bona fide commercial reasons and did not have tax avoidance as a main purpose. Finance Act twenty twenty six, section thirty eight, recast it.

The new rule applies where the main purpose, or one of the main purposes, of the arrangements is to reduce or avoid capital gains tax, corporation tax or income tax. Note the third tax. Section one three seven, the matching rule for share exchanges that chapter eighteen teaches, mentions only capital gains tax and corporation tax. Section one three nine now reaches income tax too. Where the purpose test is met, the tax advantage is counteracted by just and reasonable adjustments, which may include switching off the no gain, no loss treatment.

Clearance is available, and the acquiring company makes the application, using the same procedure as share exchange clearances. H M R C have thirty days to respond. If they refuse, the applicant can ask for the matter to go to the tribunal. The new rule applies to arrangements involving the transfer of assets on or after the twenty sixth of November, twenty twenty five, Budget day. There is transitional protection for clearances applied for before that day.

Collective investment schemes got the same recast, in a parallel rule. That is awareness only.

Why the change? The pattern across this book is that mechanical tests with narrow exceptions are being replaced by purpose tests, from the unallowable purpose rule for loans to the share exchange rules after the Delinian case. The gain is fairness. A transaction designed to avoid tax should not get relief because it fits a template. The cost is certainty. A main purpose test asks a judge to weigh motives, and that is why clearance matters so much more than it used to. For a large group, a reconstruction without clearance is now a reconstruction with an open question in the accounts.

Why demergers exist.

A reconstruction under section one three nine moves a business to a company owned by the same shareholders. A demerger does something more drastic. It ends the group relationship. One group becomes two, each owned directly by the original shareholders.

Without special rules, that is expensive. Picture Tarnmoor plc handing its shares in a subsidiary to its own shareholders. Those shareholders receive assets in respect of their shares. That is a distribution, and for individuals a taxable dividend. Tarnmoor plc disposes of the subsidiary shares at market value. And any asset the subsidiary received from a group company in the last six years would trigger a degrouping charge as it leaves.

The demerger rules, introduced in nineteen eighty and now in sections ten seventy three to ten ninety nine of the twenty ten Act, remove those costs for trading businesses. The statute states its own purpose. It is to facilitate transactions that divide trading activities carried on by a single company or group. After the division, the activities are carried on by two or more companies not in the same group, or by two or more independent groups.

The key idea is the exempt distribution. Here lies the misconception this chapter most wants to correct. People say a demerger is just a big dividend. It is not. If the conditions are met, the law says an exempt distribution is not a distribution at all. The shareholders have no income. For gains, section one nine two of the Gains Act treats them as if their company had reorganised its share capital. Their original base cost is split between their old shares and their new ones. And the degrouping charge does not apply to a company that leaves the group only because of an exempt distribution.

There are three kinds. A direct demerger, under section ten seventy six, is the simplest. The parent distributes the shares of a seventy five per cent subsidiary straight to its own shareholders. An indirect demerger, under section ten seventy seven, has three parties. The parent transfers a trade, or the shares of a seventy five per cent subsidiary, to a new company, and that new company issues its shares to the parent's shareholders. A third kind, under section ten seventy eight, is a cross-border division, linked to the Gains Act rules for businesses moving between European states. It is rarely seen.

The conditions for an exempt demerger.

The conditions are lettered A to M. That sounds forbidding. They fall into four groups, and each group has a reason.

The general conditions, A to D, apply to every demerger. Condition A is residence. Each company concerned must be resident in the United Kingdom or in a European Union member State when the distribution is made. Condition B is the trading test. The distributing company must be a trading company or a member of a trading group, and each subsidiary demerged must be a trading company or the holding company of a trading group. The relief is for dividing trades, not for parcelling out investments.

Condition C is purpose. The distribution must be made wholly or mainly to benefit some or all of the trading activities. A board paper that says the two businesses will do better apart, each with its own management, its own capital and its own strategy, is the evidence.

Condition D is the anti-avoidance core. The distribution must not be part of a scheme whose main purpose, or one of whose main purposes, is the avoidance of tax, including stamp duty and stamp duty land tax, or the making of chargeable payments. Nor may it be part of arrangements for someone other than the existing shareholders to gain control of any of the companies, or for a trade to be ceased or sold after the distribution. A demerger to prepare a business for a pre-arranged sale is exactly what condition D forbids.

The direct demerger adds conditions E and F. Condition E is that the shares distributed are not redeemable, and that they are the whole or substantially the whole of the parent's holding of the subsidiary's ordinary share capital and voting rights. A parent cannot demerge half a subsidiary. Condition F is that after the distribution the parent is still a trading company or the holding company of a trading group. There are two exceptions. Condition F does not apply where the parent is itself a seventy five per cent subsidiary. Nor does it apply where the parent demerges two or more subsidiaries and is then dissolved with nothing left for distribution.

The indirect demerger has its own conditions, G to K. In outline, the new companies must issue shares only to the parent's members. They must take the whole or substantially the whole of the transferred trade or holding, and carry on the business without simply passing it on.

Conditions L and M apply where the distributing company is itself a seventy five per cent subsidiary. In outline, the shares must travel up and out through further exempt distributions until they reach the members of the top company. A demerger cannot leave the shares stranded halfway up the group.

Clearance and the five-year shadow.

The conditions turn on purpose and on arrangements, so certainty matters. Section ten ninety one of the twenty ten Act offers advance clearance. If H M R C notify the company before the distribution that they are satisfied it will be an exempt distribution, it is treated as one. H M R C must decide within thirty days of the application, or of receiving further information they ask for. If they refuse, or do not answer in time, the company can require the application to go to the tribunal, within thirty days. H M R C still publish Statement of Practice thirteen of nineteen eighty, which explains the practice. Clearance cannot be obtained after the event.

In practice one application goes to H M R C's clearance and counteraction team and covers every relevant clearance at once. That includes the demerger, the share exchange and reconstruction clearances, and the transactions in securities clearance we meet later in this chapter. Clearance binds H M R C only on full and accurate disclosure.

The second safeguard is the chargeable payment. A demerger could be used to hand shareholders companies that then pay them cash. So for five years after an exempt distribution, a chargeable payment is taxed as income. Broadly, that is a payment made by a company concerned in the demerger to its members, in connection with their shares, that is not made for genuine commercial reasons or is part of a tax avoidance scheme. Ordinary distributions and payments within the group are excluded. A chargeable payment also revives the degrouping charge that the demerger switched off. Separate clearance is available that a proposed payment is not a chargeable payment.

There is also a filing duty. A company that makes an exempt distribution must make a return to H M R C within thirty days, giving the particulars. Payments that are, or may be, chargeable payments also have to be reported within thirty days, unless they have been cleared.

So a demerger casts a five year shadow. Tax functions on both sides of the split keep a register of payments to shareholders for those five years, and test each one.

The Tarnwater demerger.

Now the story. In our invented case, Tarnmoor's water systems business, Tarnmoor Water Systems Limited, has grown into something its owners would value differently from engineering. Its customers are regulated water companies, its contracts are long and its capital needs are different. The board decides it should stand alone, with its own listing. On the first of July in Group Year Five, Tarnmoor plc distributes its entire holding in Tarnmoor Water Systems, worth one hundred and eighty million pounds, to its own shareholders. The subsidiary re-registers as a public company and lists as Tarnwater plc.

The board considered three routes. An indirect demerger would have needed a new company and relief under section one three nine and section one three six, now with the new main purpose test. A capital reduction demerger, the route practitioners often prefer for its flexibility, would have inserted a new holding company and reduced its capital, the very route the June twenty twenty six consultation proposes to close. The board chose the simplest, a direct demerger under section ten seventy six.

Tom Hesketh's clearance application walked through the conditions. Both companies are resident in the United Kingdom. Tarnmoor plc is the holding company of a trading group and remains one afterwards, and Tarnmoor Water Systems trades. The board minutes record the commercial purpose, which is to let each business focus. There is no arrangement for anyone to acquire control of either company, and no planned sale. The shares distributed are the whole of Tarnmoor plc's holding. H M R C gave clearance under section ten ninety one. The return followed within thirty days.

The consequences fall into three parts. First, Tarnmoor's shareholders, mostly pension funds and insurers, receive no income. Each simply splits the base cost of its Tarnmoor shares between the Tarnmoor shares and the new Tarnwater shares, by their market values. The reading edition works an illustration.

Second, Tarnmoor plc itself. The demerger rules do not stop it making a disposal of the Tarnwater shares at market value. But the gain falls within the shareholding exemption. Tarnmoor plc has held all the shares for far more than twelve months, and Tarnwater trades. As H M R C's own consultation paper notes, in most direct demergers that is how the distributing company's gain is covered.

Third, the property. On the first of October in Group Year Three, Tarnmoor Estates had transferred the water systems factory to Tarnmoor Water Systems at no gain, no loss. That was less than six years ago, so the company leaving the group still holds an asset acquired from a group company. Without the demerger rules a degrouping charge would arise. Section one nine two switches it off, unless a chargeable payment is made within five years. But stamp duty land tax does not share that forgiveness. The transfer was less than three years before, so the group relief it enjoyed is clawed back, two hundred and eighty nine thousand, five hundred pounds, as chapter twenty one explains. The tax function had priced that into the decision.

Transactions in securities.

The last rule in this chapter is the oldest kind of anti-avoidance in the corporate code. Its target is simple to state. A company should not be able to take value out of another company in a form that escapes tax, when the same value taken as income would have been taxed.

The corporation tax version is in Part fifteen of the twenty ten Act. A company is liable to counteraction if it obtains, or is in a position to obtain, a corporation tax advantage from a transaction in securities, or a combination of them, in one of three circumstances. A corporation tax advantage is widely defined. It includes relief or increased relief, a repayment, and the avoidance or reduction of a charge or an assessment.

The three circumstances are lettered C, D and E. The first, circumstance A, was repealed in twenty ten. Circumstance C involves an abnormal dividend received by someone else, with the company receiving consideration that represents distributable assets and is not taxed as income. Circumstances D and E involve a relevant company. The company receives consideration, or assets, connected with the distribution, transfer or realisation of a relevant company's assets, and does not pay corporation tax on income on it. A relevant company is one under the control of five or fewer persons, or any unquoted company, unless it is controlled by companies that are not themselves relevant companies.

The courts have given the phrase transaction in securities a wide meaning. As H M R C's manual records, it has been held to include the redemption of securities, in the Parker case of nineteen sixty six. It has also been held to include an agreement altering shareholders' rights in a liquidation, in the Joiner case. But the House of Lords held in the Laird Group case in two thousand and three that paying a dividend is not, by itself, a transaction in securities.

There is a defence. The company must show both that the transactions were carried out for genuine commercial reasons, or in the ordinary course of making or managing investments, and that obtaining a corporation tax advantage was not a main object. Both limbs are needed.

The procedure is formal. H M R C give preliminary notice, the company can respond with a statutory declaration within thirty days, a tribunal may decide whether there is a case, and then a counteraction notice makes the adjustments. No counteraction can be made more than six years after the accounting period concerned. Clearance is available under section seven forty eight. H M R C have thirty days to ask for more information and thirty days after that to decide. A clearance is void without full and accurate disclosure.

How much does this matter to a large group today? Less than it once did, in the book's reading. Since two thousand and nine most dividends a large company receives are exempt, so a company usually gains nothing by taking value as capital instead. The rule still bites where the income route would have been taxed, for example a category E or F distribution, or a dividend outside every exempt class. Individual shareholders face a parallel income tax rule, which is where most clearance applications arise.

Here is the shape of a case where it could bite, invented for this purpose and not part of the Tarnmoor story. An unquoted company is owned by three investors, so it is a relevant company. One investor, a company, holds preference shares whose dividends fall outside every exempt class, so any dividend it receives is taxed. The unquoted company has large reserves. Instead of paying the dividend, the shareholders arrange for a new company to buy the investor's preference shares, at a price that includes the unpaid dividend. The new company funds the price from the unquoted company's own cash. The investor receives capital, perhaps covered by a capital loss, rather than taxable income. That is circumstance D. The investor would need to show commercial reasons and no main object of a tax advantage, or face a counteraction notice taxing the amount as income.

For the exam, a short, accurate answer wins. Name the circumstance, test the relevant company, apply the two-limb defence, and mention clearance.

Law in motion.

Return to the June twenty twenty six consultation. It is proposed, not law, and it closed on the fourteenth of September twenty twenty six. You must answer exam questions on the conditions as they stand. But its proposals show where the pressure lies.

On demergers, it proposes to liberalise almost everything. The residence condition would go. The trading condition would extend to other activities. The purpose condition and several of the type-specific conditions would be removed or relaxed. Condition D would get clear five year limits for onward sales, changes of control and winding up. In exchange, H M R C would lose a burden, because the automatic right to take a refused clearance to the tribunal would be removed.

On the other side, it proposes to close the routes practitioners use instead. Share capital in a new holding company would be frozen at the original subscription value. That would stop capital reduction demergers, and share exchanges followed by capital reductions, from releasing value as capital. The paper says it is aimed mainly at shareholders within income tax, not at corporate shareholders, but it flags possible effects on share exchanges within groups.

The debate is a familiar one. Should the law offer a generous statutory relief with tight conditions, or a simpler relief with purpose tests and fewer side doors? The consultation chooses the second. Whether Parliament follows is for a later Finance Act.

How the examiner will test this.

Demergers, transactions in securities and liquidations have not appeared as full questions from May twenty twenty three to May twenty twenty six. They are core, so expect them inside a reorganisation question. The hive-down has appeared, in May twenty twenty three, with a factory hived down with its trade. Pre-sale dividends and exempt distributions appeared in November twenty twenty four, where many candidates missed that the value shifting rule ignores a fall in value caused only by an exempt distribution.

The questions are technical prose and computations, usually worth fifteen or twenty marks, at half a mark to a mark a point. In a demerger question, list the conditions and apply each to the facts. Spot the arrangement that breaks condition D. Then follow the consequences for shareholders, the distributing company, degrouping, chargeable payments and stamp duty land tax. In a transfer of trade, state the ownership and tax conditions, the capital allowances result, the losses and the liabilities restriction.

What to take away.

A distribution is any payment to members in respect of their shares, in the categories A to H. Disguised interest is a category E or F distribution, never deductible and never exempt. Since twenty twelve, an undervalue transfer to a member is a distribution even inside a group. A reserve from a capital reduction counts as profits.

A large company receiving a distribution is taxed unless it falls in an exempt class, such as control, non-redeemable ordinary shares or a portfolio holding below ten per cent, and is not disguised interest or deductible abroad. An election to tax it is due by the second anniversary of the end of the period.

On a strike-off, distributions up to twenty five thousand pounds in total are capital, provided the company is dissolved within two years. For a parent company that can cost tax. Tarnmoor plc paid four thousand, four hundred and seventy five pounds on the eighteen thousand pound distribution from Tarnmoor Pumps, where a dividend would have been exempt.

A transfer of trade under common ownership needs seventy five per cent common ownership in the year before and at or within two years after, between companies within corporation tax. It passes plant at tax written down value and carries the losses forward to the successor. The predecessor loses its terminal loss claim. Losses are cut by the excess of liabilities left behind over assets left behind plus consideration. In our example, a one million pound excess cut four million pounds of losses to three.

A scheme of reconstruction needs conditions one and two and either three or four. Section one three six relieves the shareholders and section one three nine the company. Since the twenty sixth of November twenty twenty five, section one three nine carries a main purpose test that reaches income tax, with clearance on the acquirer's application.

An exempt demerger is not a distribution. It may be direct or indirect. It needs conditions A to D, and E and F or G to K, and L and M for a subsidiary distributor. The shareholders get reorganisation treatment, and there is no degrouping charge. The distributing company relies on the shareholding exemption. Clearance comes within thirty days, a return within thirty days, and chargeable payments are watched for five years. Stamp duty land tax clawback is not switched off, as the two hundred and eighty nine thousand, five hundred pounds on Tarnwater shows.

Transactions in securities need a corporation tax advantage, circumstance C, D or E, and a defence of commercial reasons with no main object of tax advantage. Clearance takes thirty plus thirty days, and counteraction must come within six years.

So, how can a business be divided without the tax system treating the division as a sale or a dividend? By keeping the same owners in the same proportions, keeping the trade going, and showing the purpose is commercial. The law then looks through the paper.

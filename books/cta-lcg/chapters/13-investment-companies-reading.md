# Chapter thirteen: Companies with investment business.

## Two and a half million pounds of advice.

On 16 July 2024 the Supreme Court gave judgment in a case about a bundle of invoices. The company was Centrica Overseas Holdings Ltd, the overseas investment arm of the energy group Centrica. In 2005 the group had bought Oxxio, a Dutch energy supplier. The investment failed. By mid-2009 Centrica had resolved to sell Oxxio, and it instructed advisers to make it happen. Deutsche Bank, PwC and the Dutch law firm De Brauw worked on the disposal, which completed in March 2011. Their fees, paid between July 2009 and March 2011, came to £2,529,697.

The holding company claimed those fees as expenses of management. HMRC refused. The case climbed through every tier of the tribunals and courts. Five Justices decided it in the end: Lord Hodge (Deputy President), Lord Stephens, Lady Rose, Lord Richards and Lady Simler. By then everyone accepted that the fees were expenses of management. But the Supreme Court held that they were also capital, so nothing could be deducted (*Centrica Overseas Holdings Ltd v HMRC* [2024] UKSC 25).

That outcome holds the whole of this chapter in miniature. A company that holds investments, rather than trading, gets a special deduction for the cost of running itself. But the deduction has edges. It stops at capital. It stops at costs that belong to someone else's trade. It stops at expenses the company cannot use, and it can be frozen when the company changes hands. The question for this chapter: **when the parent of a large group spends money, which of its costs does the tax system recognise, and when?**

This is chapter 13, the last but one chapter of Part Two (measuring profit). Companies with investment business are **core (grade 1)** on the LCG Advanced Technical paper, and so is change in company ownership. *The Living Law* (TKS), chapter 11, drew the line between trading and investing. Here we take the investing side to Advanced Technical depth. Throughout, the law is that for **FY2026**, under Finance Act 2026.

## A business of making investments.

The relief belongs to a **company with investment business**. CTA 2009 s 1218B defines it as "any company whose business consists wholly or partly in the making of investments". Credit unions are excluded.

"Wholly or partly" matters, and it is newer than it looks. Before 1 April 2004 the relief belonged only to an *investment company*, whose business consisted wholly or mainly of making investments. FA 2004 relaxed that test. HMRC's manual (CTM08040) explains that the change helped "hybrid companies", which held investments but could not claim because they also ran a substantial trade. Since then one company can be both a trader and an investor: its trade is taxed under the trading rules, and the costs of managing its investment business are management expenses.

Who has an investment business in practice? The parent of a group almost always does: holding and managing shares in subsidiaries is making investments. A property company that lets buildings rather than dealing in them is another example; so is a company holding a portfolio of shares or bonds. HMRC's manual (CTM08060) sorts parent companies into those whose income is only dividends, interest and rents from subsidiaries, those whose income also includes management charges, and hybrids that also trade.

**Our invented group, Tarnmoor.** Everything about it is invented for teaching. Its parent, **Tarnmoor plc (TPLC)**, is listed in London, widely held, with its head office in Leeds. It does not trade. It owns the shares in the group's companies, borrows on a bank revolving credit facility (RCF), holds 12% of Coldwater Instruments plc (AIM-quoted), and provides head office services to its overseas subsidiary **Tarnmoor Vallaria SA (TVS)** in the invented country of Vallaria. It is the group's main company with investment business. A second is **Tarnmoor Estates Ltd (TES)**, the property company, which owns sites and lets them, mostly to group companies.

The trading companies sit beside them: **Tarnmoor Engineering Ltd (TEL)** (valves, actuators, pumps) and **Calder Valve Engineering Ltd (CVE)**, bought on 1 April GY1. Their costs are deducted under the trading rules (CTA 2009 ss 53–54). Nothing in this chapter applies to a cost of their trades.

Recognising the investment business is the first mark in any question about a parent company. The examiners reported after the **May 2024** sitting (Q1) that most candidates did not identify that the company had an investment business at all.

## Why the relief exists.

Imagine an orchestra. The conductor plays no instrument, yet without the conductor the music falls apart, and the conductor's fee is a real cost of the concert. A holding company is the conductor: it makes no product, but owns, directs and finances the companies that do.

The tax system has a structural gap. Corporation tax charges income under separate heads: trading profits, property income and loan relationships each have their own computation and deductions; dividends from subsidiaries are usually exempt (CTA 2009 Part 9A). The cost of running a non-trading company (its board, listing, finance team, audit) belongs to none of those heads. Without a special rule it would vanish. Management expenses relief fills the gap.

**The rule (CTA 2009 s 1219).** Expenses of management of the company's investment business, so far as referable to the accounting period, are deducted **from total profits**: not just from investment income, but from all the company's profits for the period.

**Priority (s 1219(1A)).** Current-period management expenses are deducted **before any other deduction** at Step 2 of CTA 2010 s 4(2): ahead of brought-forward losses, qualifying charitable donations and group relief claimed. In a computation, show them as the first deduction from total profits, with the authority beside them.

**Exempt dividends do not reduce the deduction.** Many people assume a parent cannot deduct the costs of holding shares that pay exempt dividends. Section 1222 does reduce the deduction by income from a source not charged to tax, but s 1222(4) carves out "exempt ABGH distributions" (distributions within paras A, B, G or H of CTA 2010 s 1000(1) that are exempt under Part 9A). So TPLC's exempt dividends from its subsidiaries do not reduce its management expenses. Parliament has chosen to relieve the cost of holding investments even when the income they produce is exempt.

## What counts as an expense of management.

**Misconception:** "a holding company's costs are all deductible management expenses." They are not. The statute gives no definition; the courts and HMRC's manual fill it in, and three ideas do most of the work.

1. **A cost of managing the company's own investment business, not of buying or selling a particular investment.** On the authority of *Capital and National Trust Ltd v Golder* and *Sun Life Assurance Society v Davidson*, which HMRC's manual still cites (CTM08190), brokerage and stamp duty on changing investments are not expenses of management: they are part of the price.
2. **The company's own business, not a subsidiary's trade.** CTM08180: a holding company gets no special position because it belongs to a group; its deductible costs are those of managing its own investments, not of running its subsidiaries' trades.
3. **Not every corporate cost is a cost of managing investments.** In *Dawsongroup plc v HMRC* [2010] EWHC 1061 (Ch) (Mann J, 11 May 2010) the company, having floated 25% of its shares in 1988, decided in 2000 to delist. The costs (just over £433,000) were held not to be expenses of management. (Reports differ on the judge's view of whether Dawsongroup was an investment company at all; both versions agree on the result.)

**HMRC's 2025 letter campaign.** HMRC sent "one-to-many" letters to agents in the week beginning 15 September 2025 and to companies from the week beginning 29 September 2025, aimed at large-business UK holding companies of overseas subsidiaries that claim management expenses (CIOT summary; Deloitte, KPMG and Saffery commentary). As advisers report it, **HMRC's view** is that "shareholder costs" are management expenses (unless capital), but costs that serve a subsidiary's business are not, unless the subsidiary is recharged on arm's length terms.

That is exactly TPLC's position in our invented case.

> **Worked example 13.1: TPLC's management expenses, GY1 (invented)**
>
> | Component | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Cost of services provided to TVS (group purchasing, engineering standards, IT support) | 2,000,000 |
> | Strategic review of acquisition targets (pre-decision; see 13.2) | 300,000 |
> | Other head office costs (board, listing, investor relations, group finance and tax, plc audit) | 5,700,000 |
> | **Management expenses, GY1** | **8,000,000** |

> TPLC charged TVS **£1,600,000**. Transfer pricing (chapter 27) requires an arm's length charge of cost + 5% = **£2,100,000**, so TPLC's income is adjusted upwards by **£500,000** (CT £125,000). With the recharge at arm's length, the £2,000,000 of service costs can stay in management expenses, matched by £2,100,000 of taxable income.

## The capital line.

For accounting periods beginning on or after **1 April 2004** the law says expenses of a capital nature are not expenses of management (now s 1219(3)(a)). That exclusion was first enacted by **FA 2004 s 38**, as the Supreme Court recorded in *Centrica* (para 18).

Before that date there was no express exclusion. In *Camas plc v Atkinson* [2004] EWCA Civ 541, on the earlier law, the costs of an abortive bid for another company were treated as expenses of management, because they were spent in deciding whether to acquire. HMRC's manual (CTM08250) accepts that for periods up to 31 March 2004 capital expenditure could qualify if it was an expense of management. The capital exclusion closed that door for later periods.

That left the question *Centrica* answered: what does "capital" mean for a company with no trade? The Upper Tribunal had found the Oxxio fees to be revenue, partly because, when they were incurred, nobody could be sure a sale would happen. The Court of Appeal allowed HMRC's appeal, and the Supreme Court dismissed the company's appeal.

**The holding to remember.** The courts have held that the capital test in s 1219(3)(a) is the **same as the test for a trader's expenses under s 53**: a question of law applied to the facts. As commentators summarise the reasoning, the acquisition or disposal of an identifiable asset with an enduring benefit is a strong indication of capital, and money spent to dispose of a capital asset is generally capital itself. The uncertainty of a sale does not turn disposal costs into revenue.

**Where does management stop and capital start?** HMRC's manual (CTM08260) gives the working line: expenditure on appraising and investigating investments will in general be revenue, but the appraisal eventually reaches the stage where the company decides which companies, if any, it is seeking to acquire. HMRC's view (CTM08190) is that costs do not become acquisition costs until, **at the earliest, the date of the decision** to acquire. *Centrica* applied the same thinking to a sale: once Centrica had decided to sell an identifiable asset, the fees of selling it were capital.

The decision is not a magic date. It is evidence of what the money was spent on. A cost incurred after the decision, on the deal itself, is capital. A cost incurred before it, choosing between options, is more likely to be revenue. A cost that straddles the two must be looked at for what it actually bought.

## Drawing the line at Tarnmoor.

In our invented case TPLC bought Calder on 1 April GY1 for £32.0m. The tax team, led by the group head of tax, Tom Hesketh, had to sort £1.2m of advisers' fees.

>
> | Cost | When | Treatment | Authority | £ |

> | Strategic review: market mapping, long-list screening (Calder one of many; no target chosen) | January GY1 | Revenue: expense of management | s 1219; CTM08260 | 300,000 |
> | *Board minute: decision to make an offer for Calder* | *3 February GY1* | | | |

> | Legal: sale and purchase agreement | after 3 Feb | Capital: excluded | as above | 300,000 |
> | Corporate finance adviser | after 3 Feb | Capital: excluded | as above | 150,000 |
> | **Total advisers' fees** | | | | **1,200,000** |
> | of which deductible as management expenses | | | | 300,000 |
> | of which capital (excluded) | | | | 900,000 |
>
> Two further deal costs take other routes:
> - **Stamp duty £160,000** on the Calder shares: part of the price (like brokerage in *Sun Life*); not a management expense.
> - **RCF arrangement fee £300,000**: a **loan relationship debit** (CTA 2009 s 306A), recognised as the accounts amortise it (chapter 12). Section 1219(3)(b) bars a management expense deduction for anything deductible elsewhere.
>
> TPLC's management expenses for GY1: **£8,000,000** (after excluding the £900,000; including the £300,000).

**Does capital mean lost?** Not quite. Some of the £900,000 may be incidental costs of acquiring the Calder shares (TCGA 1992 s 38). But if TPLC ever sold Calder, the substantial shareholding exemption would very likely make the gain exempt and the base cost irrelevant. As practitioners noted after *Centrica*, where the SSE applies, capital deal costs usually get no relief at all.

The facts carried the analysis: the board minute of 3 February fixed the turning point, and the consultants' invoices showed that the review was about the market, not Calder. Without those papers HMRC could argue that the review was the first step of the Calder deal.

## Other gates, and deemed expenses.

Passing the capital test is not enough. Each of these gates is an examiner favourite.

| Gate | Rule | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Overlap | No deduction for amounts deductible in computing profits under another provision: interest and loan fees are loan relationship debits; a property company's repairs and agents' fees are property business expenses. TES deducts its buildings' running costs against its rents; only its company-level costs (board, audit) are management expenses | s 1219(3)(b) |
| Unallowable purpose | No deduction for expenses referable to an investment held for an unallowable purpose (not a business or commercial purpose of the company, including tax-advantage arrangements, or held for activities outside the CT charge); just and reasonable apportionment | s 1220 |
| Timing | Referable to the AP in which debited in GAAP-compliant accounts (time-apportioned if the period of account differs) | ss 1224–1227 |
| Clawback | A credit reversing a deducted expense (for example a released provision) first reduces that period's management expenses; any balance is taxed as income | s 1229 |
| Mirror restrictions | Unpaid remuneration (deductible when paid if not paid within 9 months of the period end); car hire; expenses connected with tax avoidance | ss 1248–1251 |

Picture the year-end cost ledger as a hall with several doors: capital, overlap, purpose, timing. Only what walks through every one of them is an expense of management.

**Deemed management expenses (Part 16 Ch 3).** Some items are treated as management expenses whatever their nature.
- **Excess capital allowances (s 1233).** Managing the business of a company with investment business is a qualifying activity (CAA 2001 s 15(1)(g); for SBA s 270CA(f)). Allowances are first set against the investment business's income (CAA 2001 s 253(2)); so far as they cannot be, s 1233 treats them as management expenses of the period for which the company is entitled to them. So TPLC's allowances on head office computers and furniture end up with the priority and flexibility of management expenses.
- **Other deemed items** mirror trading deductions: payments for restrictive undertakings, seconded staff, payroll giving costs, counselling and retraining, redundancy payments (ss 1239–1243), contributions to local enterprise organisations and flood defence, and certain levies.

**Charitable donations.** A trading company's excess qualifying charitable donations die at the end of the period. A company with investment business can carry forward unrelieved donations made for the investment business as management expenses of the next period (s 1223).

## When expenses outrun income.

A parent's management expenses usually exceed its taxable income. In GY1 TPLC's only taxable income is the £2.1m arm's length recharge. Its £8.0m of management expenses come off total profits first, wiping out the £2.1m and leaving **£5.9m** unused.

The obvious user of the surplus is TEL, which pays 25%. Group relief lets a parent surrender current-period management expenses (CTA 2010 s 99(1)(f)); chapter 15 teaches the mechanics. Here the question is **how much is available**.

**The profit-related threshold (CTA 2010 s 105).** Management expenses are one of the "relevant amounts" in s 99(1)(d)–(g) (with QCDs, UK property business losses and non-trading intangibles losses). They can be surrendered only so far as their total exceeds the surrendering company's **profit-related threshold**, and only the excess may be surrendered (s 105(2)–(3)). For surrender periods ending on or after 20 March 2013 (FA 2013), the threshold (s 105(3A)) is the sum of:
- the company's **gross profits** of the period (broadly its profits ignoring trading losses, excess CAs, NTLR deficits, property losses, management expenses, QCDs and non-trading intangibles losses of the period, and amounts from other periods: s 105(5); CTM80142); and
- any **CFC chargeable profits apportioned** to it under step 3 of TIOPA 2010 s 371BC where it is a chargeable company at step 4.

TPLC's Marrovian finance company, **Tarnmoor Capital Ltd (TCM)** (invented country), is a CFC (chapter 26). Each year GY1–GY4, **£825,000** of its chargeable profits (25% of £3.3m, after the Ch 9 exemption) is apportioned to TPLC.

And there is no escape against the CFC charge: TIOPA 2010 s 371UD (relief against the sum charged) was **omitted by F(No.2)A 2015 s 36** for CFC accounting periods beginning on or after 8 July 2015. So TPLC's management expenses can neither shelter the CFC charge nor be surrendered up to the amount of the apportionment.

## Carrying the surplus forward.

Surplus management expenses do not die. Section 1223 carries them forward as management expenses of the next AP. Since the 2017 loss reform, three conditions apply that current-period expenses do not have (s 1223(3B)–(3E)):

1. **A claim** is needed, within 2 years after the end of the later AP; the claim may be partial.
2. **No priority:** carried-forward amounts lose the s 1219(1A) first place and queue with other brought-forward losses.
3. **The loss restriction** (CTA 2010 Part 7ZA, s 269ZD): carried-forward amounts can relieve only the **£5m deductions allowance** plus **50%** of relevant profits above it, counted together with carried-forward trading losses, deficits and capital losses. Chapter 14 covers the group's single allowance and its nomination.

**Group route:** carried-forward management expenses from APs beginning on or after 1 April 2017 can be surrendered under the rules for group relief for carried-forward losses (CTA 2010 Part 5A; s 1223(3A)), taught in chapter 15.

For TPLC, that route is closed on this book's reading. HMRC's guidance (CTM82030, on CTA 2010 s 188BE) says a company may not surrender carried-forward losses under Part 5A so far as it could deduct them from its own total profits. TPLC's recharge income can always absorb carried-forward expenses, but using them there only displaces current expenses that the s 105 cap then traps. So TPLC's carried-forward management expenses are **stranded**: cumulative **£825,000** (end GY1), **£1,650,000** (GY2), **£2,475,000** (GY3) and **£3,300,000** (GY4), plus a further **£1,275,000** in GY5 if TPLC's excess still exceeds the threshold. No deferred tax asset is recognised on them (chapter 6).

> - **Style:** "Calculate, with explanations"; 0.5–1 mark per point; technical prose and computation, no letter format.
> - **Traps:** deducting post-decision capital deal costs; deducting brokerage or stamp duty; deducting finance fees as management expenses instead of loan relationship debits; forgetting that current management expenses come off total profits **first**; forgetting the **claim** and the **50% restriction** on carried-forward amounts; treating the whole excess over own profits as surrenderable when the s 105 threshold (including any CFC apportionment) limits it; reducing management expenses for exempt dividends.
> - **Layout:** income by type (exempt dividends shown as exempt) → management expenses as the first relief, with a one-line reason for each exclusion → surplus → threshold → amount available for group relief → amount carried forward.

## When the owner changes.

Carried-forward reliefs have value, and value attracts buyers. If a company with surplus management expenses could be bought, its business replaced by a profitable one, and the old expenses set against new profits, the tax system would be selling relief. **CTA 2010 Part 14 Chapter 3 (ss 677–691)** stops that.

**Change in ownership (s 719):** Condition A, one person acquires more than half the ordinary share capital; Condition B, two or more persons each acquire at least 5% and together more than half; Condition C, two or more persons acquire holdings together more than half, ignoring holdings under 5% unless they are top-ups. Indirect ownership counts (s 723); the insertion of a new holding company is disregarded (s 724A).

**The three conditions (s 677).** Chapter 3 applies on a change in ownership of a company with investment business if:

| Condition | Test |
|---|---|
| A: significant increase in capital (ss 688–691) | Amount B exceeds amount A by **at least £1m** *and* is **at least 125%** of amount A (both; the "or twice" alternative applied only to changes before 1 April 2014). Amount A: the lower of capital immediately before the change and the highest 60-day minimum amount in the year before it (s 689). Amount B: the highest 60-day minimum amount in the post-change period (s 690; HMRC's manual CTM08750 says five years, formerly three). Capital: paid-up share capital (including share premium), certain outstanding debts and redeemable loan capital (s 691) |
| B: major change | A major change in the nature or conduct of the business **within the 8 years beginning 3 years before** the change (including a major change in the nature of investments held, even if gradual) |
| C: small or negligible | The change occurs while the business is small or negligible, before a significant revival |

**Effect.** The AP is split notionally at the change (s 678). Pre-change management expenses, non-trading LR deficits and debits, non-trading intangibles losses and property losses cannot be carried forward against post-change profits (ss 679–684), with apportionment under s 685; excess capital allowances treated as management expenses are caught too (s 682(4)). Chapter 2A (post-1 April 2017 reliefs; "major change in the business") does not apply where Chapter 3 (or Chapter 2) can apply instead (s 676AB; CTM06775); it is taught with the trading company rules in chapter 14.

**A labelled hypothetical (not part of the story).** Suppose an activist investor bought more than half of TPLC's shares (a change in ownership under s 719 Condition A). Suppose TPLC was then carrying forward £2m of surplus management expenses, and within the window the new owner sold the engineering subsidiaries and turned TPLC into the holder of a property portfolio: a major change in the nature of its investments (Condition B). The £2m could not be used against post-change profits. For a listed parent with a large capital base, Condition A is hard to trigger (Case 2); Condition B is the realistic risk.

## Going further.

> **Going further: the tax function's tasks**
> - **Record the decision.** The *Centrica* boundary turns on what the money was spent on. A board minute fixing when the group decided to pursue a specific target, and engagement letters that separate the strategic review from the deal, are the best evidence. Tom Hesketh's team asks for both at the start of every project.
> - **Price the recharges.** After HMRC's 2025 letter campaign, check that costs serving subsidiaries (especially overseas ones) are recharged at arm's length and documented under the transfer pricing records rules (SI 2023/818; chapter 27). Otherwise the costs fall out of management expenses, and a transfer pricing adjustment may still follow.
> - **Characterise the recharge income.** The book treats TPLC's recharge as non-trading income (income not otherwise charged, CTA 2009 s 979), with the service costs inside management expenses. If HMRC argued that TPLC trades in providing services, the £2.0m of costs would move into a trading computation (trading profit £100,000; management expenses £6.0m). The surrenderable amount would be unchanged: threshold £100,000 + £825,000 = £925,000; £6,000,000 − £925,000 = **£5,075,000**; £825,000 carried forward.
> - **Map the surplus.** Calculate the s 105 threshold, including any CFC apportionment, before deciding the surrender, and keep the calculation in the same working paper as the group relief schedule. Over-surrendering means a reduced claim, more tax for the claimant and interest.
> - **Keep a register** of carried-forward management expenses with the 2-year claim deadlines and the Part 14 windows.
> - **Deferred tax.** Carried-forward management expenses work like unused tax losses: a deferred tax asset is recognised only so far as future taxable profits (including group relief) make recovery probable (chapter 6).
> - **Interaction.** For CIR, each company's tax-EBITDA includes its current-period management expenses but not brought-forward amounts (TIOPA 2010 ss 405–407), which is why TPLC's tax-EBITDA is negative (−£5.9m in GY1; chapter 28). An arguable capital/revenue position may need an uncertain tax treatment notification if it crosses the £5m threshold (chapter 4).

## How the examiner tests it.

The topic appears often but quietly: four papers in a row (M23 Q6, M24 Q1, M25 Q5, M26 Q4; details in the Exam lens boxes above). The investment company rarely appears on its own. It arrives as the parent in a group relief question, the landlord in a property question, or the seller in an SSE question. The management expenses marks are often the easy marks hidden inside a bigger question: bank them first. ## What to take away.

- A **company with investment business** is any company whose business consists wholly or partly in making investments (s 1218B). Since 1 April 2004 that includes a company that also trades. Group parents and property letting companies are the classic examples.
- **Management expenses** of the investment business are deducted from **total profits**, and current-period expenses come **first** (s 1219(1A)). Exempt dividends do not reduce them (s 1222(4)).
- An expense of management is a cost of managing the company's **own** investment business: not brokerage or stamp duty, not a subsidiary's trade, not a cost deductible elsewhere.
- **Capital is excluded** (s 1219(3)(a), FA 2004 s 38). *Centrica* [2024] UKSC 25: the capital test is the trader's test; £2,529,697 of Oxxio disposal fees were capital. HMRC's view: appraisal is revenue; acquisition costs begin, at the earliest, at the decision to acquire. Record that decision.
- In our invented case TPLC allowed **£300,000** of strategic review costs and excluded **£900,000** of post-decision Calder deal costs. Management expenses: **£8.0m** (GY1), **£8.5m** (GY2).
- Other gates: unallowable purpose (s 1220), accounts timing (s 1225), clawback (s 1229), 9-month remuneration rule. Excess CAs on head office assets become management expenses (s 1233); an investment business's excess QCDs can be carried forward.
- Surplus can be group-relieved only above the **profit-related threshold**: gross profits **plus apportioned CFC profits** (s 105(3A)). TPLC: excess over gross profits £5.9m, but only **£5,075,000** to TEL; **£825,000** carried forward (stranded).
- Carried-forward management expenses need a **claim within 2 years**, lose priority, and sit within the **£5m allowance and 50% restriction**; post-2017 amounts can go via **Part 5A**, but not where the company could deduct them itself (s 188BE, HMRC's view): TPLC's are stranded.
- After a **change in ownership**, pre-change management expenses are lost if there is a **significant increase in capital (≥ £1m and ≥ 125%)**, a **major change within 8 years beginning 3 years before**, or a change while the business is **small or negligible** (Part 14 Ch 3).

The question was which of a parent's costs the tax system recognises, and when. The answer: the costs of running its own investments, judged as a trader's would be, used first, and used fully only if the company keeps its business and remembers its claims.

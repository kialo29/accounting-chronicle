Chapter thirteen: Companies with investment business.

Two and a half million pounds of advice.

On the sixteenth of July, twenty twenty four, the Supreme Court gave judgment in a case about a bundle of invoices. The company was Centrica Overseas Holdings Limited, the overseas investment arm of the energy group Centrica. In two thousand and five the group had bought Oxxio, a Dutch energy supplier. The investment failed. By the middle of two thousand and nine, Centrica had resolved to sell Oxxio, and it instructed advisers to make it happen. Deutsche Bank, the accountants P w C and the Dutch law firm De Brauw worked on the disposal until it completed in March twenty eleven. Their fees came to just over two and a half million pounds.

The holding company claimed those fees as expenses of management. H M R C refused. The case climbed through every tier of the tax tribunals and courts. Five Justices decided it in the end. They were Lord Hodge, the Deputy President, with Lord Stephens, Lady Rose, Lord Richards and Lady Simler. By then everyone accepted that the fees were expenses of management. But the Supreme Court held that they were also capital. So nothing could be deducted.

That outcome holds the whole of this chapter in miniature. A company that holds investments, rather than trading, gets a special deduction for the cost of running itself. But the deduction has edges. It stops at capital. It stops at costs that belong to someone else's trade. It stops at expenses that the company cannot use, and it can be frozen when the company changes hands. The question for this chapter is simple to ask. When the parent of a large group spends money, which of its costs does the tax system recognise, and when?

This is chapter thirteen, the last but one chapter of Part Two, on measuring profit. Companies with investment business are a core topic on the Advanced Technical paper, and so is the change of ownership rule for them. The Living Law, chapter eleven, drew the line between trading and investing for individuals and companies. Here we take the investing side to Advanced Technical depth. Throughout, we apply the law for the financial year twenty twenty six, under the Finance Act twenty twenty six.

A business of making investments.

The relief belongs to a company with investment business. The Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, which I will call the two thousand and nine Act, defines it in section twelve eighteen B. It is any company whose business consists wholly or partly in the making of investments. Credit unions are excluded.

The words wholly or partly matter, and they are newer than they look. Before the first of April, two thousand and four, the relief belonged only to an investment company, a company whose business consisted wholly or mainly of making investments. The Finance Act two thousand and four relaxed that test. H M R C's manual explains that the change helped so-called hybrid companies, which held investments but could not claim because they also ran a substantial trade. Since then, one company can be both a trader and an investor. Its trade is taxed under the trading rules, and the costs of managing its investment business are management expenses.

Who has an investment business in practice? The parent of a group almost always does. Holding shares in subsidiaries, and managing those holdings, is making investments. A property company that lets buildings rather than dealing in them is another example. So is a company that holds a portfolio of shares or bonds.

Meet our invented group, Tarnmoor. Everything about it is invented for teaching. Its parent, Tarnmoor P L C, is listed in London, widely held, and has its head office in Leeds. It does not trade. It owns the shares in the group's companies, borrows on a bank facility, holds a twelve per cent stake in a company quoted on the Alternative Investment Market, and provides head office services to its overseas subsidiary, Tarnmoor Vallaria, in the invented country of Vallaria. It is the group's main company with investment business. A second is Tarnmoor Estates, the group's property company, which owns sites and lets them, mostly to other group companies.

The trading companies sit beside them. Tarnmoor Engineering makes valves, actuators and pumps, and Calder Valve Engineering, bought in Group Year One, makes valves too. Their costs are deducted under the trading rules, by reference to the wholly and exclusively test and the capital rule. None of what follows applies to a cost of their trades.

Recognising the investment business is the first mark in any question about a parent company. The examiners reported, after the May twenty twenty four sitting, that most candidates did not identify that the company in front of them had an investment business at all. They wrote about trading expenses for a company that had no trade.

Why the relief exists.

Imagine an orchestra. The conductor plays no instrument, yet without the conductor the music falls apart, and the conductor's fee is a real cost of the concert. A holding company is the conductor. It makes no product. It owns, directs and finances the companies that do. Its costs are real costs of the group's business.

The tax system has a structural gap here. Corporation tax charges a company's income under separate heads. Trading profits have their own computation, with their own deductions. So do property income and loan relationships. Dividends from subsidiaries are usually exempt. But the cost of running a non-trading company, such as its board, its listing, its finance team and its audit, belongs to none of those heads. Without a special rule, that cost would vanish. Management expenses relief fills the gap.

The rule is in section twelve nineteen of the two thousand and nine Act. Expenses of management of the company's investment business, so far as they are referable to the accounting period, are deducted from the company's total profits. Total profits matters. The deduction is not limited to investment income. It comes off the whole of the company's profits for the period, whatever their source.

It also comes first. The law says that current period management expenses are deducted before any other deduction at the second step of the taxable total profits calculation in section four of the Corporation Tax Act twenty ten, which I will call the twenty ten Act. They rank ahead of brought forward losses, ahead of qualifying charitable donations and ahead of group relief claimed. The examiner rewards that ordering. Management expenses first. In a computation, show them as the first deduction from total profits, with the authority beside them, and the marker can see at once that you know the order.

One misconception needs to go straight away. Many people assume that a parent company receiving exempt dividends cannot deduct the costs of holding the shares that pay them. Section twelve twenty two does reduce the deduction by income from a source not charged to tax. But it expressly leaves out ordinary dividends and similar distributions that are exempt under Part nine A of the two thousand and nine Act. So Tarnmoor P L C's exempt dividends from its subsidiaries do not reduce its management expenses. Parliament has chosen to relieve the cost of holding investments even when the income they produce is exempt.

What counts as an expense of management.

Start with the misconception that most often costs marks. It is that every cost a holding company incurs is a deductible management expense. It is not. The statute gives no definition of an expense of management. The courts and H M R C's manual fill it in, and three ideas do most of the work.

First, an expense of management is a cost of managing the company's own investment business. It is not a cost of buying or selling a particular investment. The old cases, Capital and National Trust versus Golder and Sun Life Assurance Society versus Davidson, which H M R C's manual still cites, held that brokerage and stamp duty on changing investments are not expenses of management. They are part of the price of the investment.

Second, the expense must belong to this company's business, not to a subsidiary's trade. H M R C's manual on groups makes the point directly. A holding company gets no special position because it belongs to a group. Its deductible costs are those of managing its own investments, not those of running the trades of its subsidiaries. If the parent pays for a marketing campaign that helps a subsidiary sell valves, that cost belongs to the subsidiary.

Third, not every cost a company incurs is a cost of managing investments. In Dawsongroup P L C versus H M R C, decided by the High Court in twenty ten, the company had floated a quarter of its shares in nineteen eighty eight. The flotation disappointed, and in two thousand it went private again. Mr Justice Mann held that the costs of delisting, about four hundred and thirty three thousand pounds, were not expenses of management.

The group context brings a live dispute. In September twenty twenty five, H M R C began a letter campaign aimed at large U K holding companies with overseas subsidiaries that claim management expenses. Advisers' summaries describe H M R C's concern. Some groups deduct, in the parent, costs that mainly benefit an overseas subsidiary or another connected company. H M R C's view, as advisers report it, is that shareholder costs are management expenses, but costs that serve a subsidiary's business are not, unless the subsidiary is recharged on arm's length terms.

That is exactly Tarnmoor P L C's position, in our invented case. Of its eight million pounds of management expenses in Group Year One, two million pounds is the cost of services it provides to Tarnmoor Vallaria, such as group purchasing, engineering standards and information technology support. It recharged only one point six million pounds. Transfer pricing, which chapter twenty seven teaches, required an arm's length charge of cost plus five per cent, two point one million pounds. So the group adjusted Tarnmoor P L C's income upwards by half a million pounds. Once the recharge was at arm's length, the two million pounds of costs could stay in management expenses, matched by two point one million pounds of taxable income.

The capital line.

For accounting periods beginning on or after the first of April, two thousand and four, the law says that expenses of a capital nature are not expenses of management. That exclusion, now in section twelve nineteen, subsection three, was first enacted by section thirty eight of the Finance Act two thousand and four, as the Supreme Court recorded in Centrica.

Before that date there was no express exclusion. In Camas P L C versus Atkinson, decided by the Court of Appeal in two thousand and four on the earlier law, the costs of an abortive bid for another company were treated as expenses of management, because they were spent in deciding whether to acquire. H M R C's manual accepts that, for periods up to the thirty first of March, two thousand and four, capital expenditure could qualify if it was an expense of management. The capital exclusion closed that door for later periods.

That left the question Centrica answered. What does capital mean for a company with no trade? The Upper Tribunal had found the Oxxio fees to be revenue, partly because, when the fees were incurred, nobody could be sure that a sale would happen. The Court of Appeal allowed H M R C's appeal, and the Supreme Court dismissed the company's appeal against that.

The Supreme Court's central holding is the one to remember. The courts have held that the capital test for management expenses is the same as the capital test for a trader's expenses under section fifty three of the two thousand and nine Act. It is a question of law, applied to the facts. Commentators summarising the judgment draw out the reasoning. The acquisition or disposal of an identifiable asset with an enduring benefit is a strong indication of capital, and money spent to dispose of a capital asset is generally capital itself. The uncertainty of a sale does not turn disposal costs into revenue.

So where does management stop and capital start? H M R C's manual gives the working line. Expenditure on appraising and investigating investments will in general be revenue. But the appraisal eventually reaches the stage where the company decides which companies, if any, it is seeking to acquire. H M R C's view is that costs do not become acquisition costs until, at the earliest, the date of that decision. Centrica applied the same thinking to a sale. Once Centrica had decided to sell an identifiable asset, Oxxio, the fees of selling it were capital.

The decision is not a magic date. It is evidence of what the money was spent on. A cost incurred after the decision, on the deal itself, is capital. A cost incurred before it, choosing between options, is more likely to be revenue. A cost that straddles the two must be looked at for what it actually bought.

Drawing the line at Tarnmoor.

In our invented case, Tarnmoor P L C bought Calder Valve Engineering on the first of April in Group Year One for thirty two million pounds. The tax team, led by the group head of tax, Tom Hesketh, had to sort one point two million pounds of advisers' fees.

The first three hundred thousand pounds came from a strategic review in January of Group Year One. Consultants mapped the market for industrial valves and screened a long list of possible targets, of which Calder was one among many. No target had been chosen. The board was deciding whether to buy anything at all. In H M R C's language, that was appraisal. The tax team treated it as an expense of management, deductible in Group Year One.

On the third of February in Group Year One, the board minuted its decision to make an offer for Calder. Everything after that was the acquisition of an identifiable asset. Financial and tax due diligence cost four hundred and fifty thousand pounds. Lawyers drafting the sale and purchase agreement cost three hundred thousand pounds. The corporate finance adviser charged one hundred and fifty thousand pounds. Those nine hundred thousand pounds are capital, and they are excluded from management expenses. So Tarnmoor P L C's management expenses for Group Year One are eight million pounds, after leaving out the nine hundred thousand pounds and including the three hundred thousand pounds.

Two other costs of the deal took different routes. The one hundred and sixty thousand pounds of stamp duty on the Calder shares is part of the price, like the brokerage in the old cases. The three hundred thousand pounds arrangement fee on the bank facility that funded the purchase is not a management expense either. It is a loan relationship debit, recognised over the life of the facility as the accounts amortise it. Chapter twelve, on loan relationships, explains why. Management expenses never cover a cost that another part of the tax code already deducts.

Does capital mean lost? Not quite. Some of the nine hundred thousand pounds may count as incidental costs of acquiring the Calder shares for chargeable gains purposes. But if Tarnmoor P L C ever sold Calder, the substantial shareholding exemption would very likely make the gain exempt, and the base cost would then be irrelevant. As a practitioner remarked after Centrica, where the exemption applies, capital deal costs usually get no relief at all.

The facts carried the analysis. The board minute of the third of February fixed the turning point, and the consultants' invoices showed that the strategic review was about the market, not about Calder. Without those papers, H M R C could argue that the review was the first step of the Calder deal.

Other gates, and deemed expenses.

Passing the capital test is not enough. An expense of management must also pass several other gates, and each is a favourite of the examiner.

The first gate is overlap. Section twelve nineteen gives no deduction for an amount that is deductible in computing profits under another provision. Interest and loan fees are loan relationship debits. A property company's repairs and agents' fees are deducted in its property business. So Tarnmoor Estates deducts the running costs of its let buildings against its rents, and only its company-level costs, such as its board and its audit, are management expenses.

The second gate is unallowable purpose. Section twelve twenty denies the deduction for expenses referable to an investment held for an unallowable purpose. That means an investment held for a purpose that is not a business or commercial purpose of the company, including holdings that are part of arrangements to obtain a tax advantage, or held for activities outside the charge to corporation tax. The expenses are apportioned on a just and reasonable basis.

The third gate is timing. Expenses are referable to the accounting period in which they are debited in accounts drawn up under generally accepted accounting practice. So the accounts decide the year, as they do for traders. If a credit later reverses an expense that was deducted, such as a provision released, section twelve twenty nine claws it back. The credit first reduces that period's management expenses, and any balance is taxed as income.

The fourth gate is the set of restrictions that mirror the trading rules. Remuneration not paid within nine months after the end of the period is deductible only when paid. Car hire is restricted as it is for traders. There is also a specific restriction for expenses connected with tax avoidance.

Picture a holding company's cost ledger at the year end as a hall with several doors. The capital door, the overlap door, the purpose door and the timing door all have to open. Only what walks through every one of them is an expense of management.

Some expenses go the other way. Chapter three of Part sixteen of the two thousand and nine Act adds some items that the law treats as management expenses, whatever their nature.

The most useful for groups is excess capital allowances. Managing the business of a company with investment business is a qualifying activity for capital allowances. If Tarnmoor P L C buys computers and furniture for its Leeds head office, it can claim allowances on them. The Capital Allowances Act two thousand and one first sets those allowances against the income of the investment business. So far as they cannot be used that way, section twelve thirty three treats them as management expenses of the period. They then share the priority and the flexibility of management expenses.

The other deemed items are more specialised. They include payments for restrictive undertakings, the cost of seconding staff to charities and educational bodies, the costs of payroll giving schemes, counselling and retraining for departing employees, and redundancy payments. They allow an investment company the same relief a trader would get for the same spending, which is the purpose of the whole Chapter.

Qualifying charitable donations are a special case. A trading company's excess donations simply die at the end of the period. But a company with investment business can carry its unrelieved donations made for the investment business forward as management expenses of the next period. It is one of the few ways a charitable gift survives the year in which it was made.

When expenses outrun income.

A parent company's management expenses usually exceed its taxable income. Tarnmoor P L C is typical. In Group Year One, in our invented case, its only taxable income is the two point one million pounds recharge to Tarnmoor Vallaria. Its management expenses are eight million pounds. They come off its total profits first, wiping out the two point one million pounds and leaving five point nine million pounds unused.

The unused amount is the surplus, and the group wants to use it now. The obvious buyer is Tarnmoor Engineering, the group's big trading company, which pays tax at twenty five per cent. Group relief lets a parent surrender current period management expenses to a fellow group member. Chapter fifteen teaches the mechanics. Here the question is how much is available.

The answer is not simply what is left after the company's own income. Management expenses are a restricted kind of group relief. Section one hundred and five of the twenty ten Act allows them to be surrendered only so far as they exceed the surrendering company's profit-related threshold. Since twenty thirteen that threshold has two parts. The first is the company's gross profits for the period, its profits before any of the losses and other amounts that group relief deals with. The second is any profit of a controlled foreign company apportioned to the company under the controlled foreign company rules in the Taxation, International and Other Provisions, Act twenty ten, which I will call the International Act.

That second part catches Tarnmoor P L C. Its finance subsidiary in the invented country of Marrovia, Tarnmoor Capital, is a controlled foreign company, or C F C, as chapter twenty six explains. Each year from Group Year One to Group Year Four, eight hundred and twenty five thousand pounds of its profits are apportioned to Tarnmoor P L C and taxed there. So the threshold for Group Year One is two point one million pounds plus eight hundred and twenty five thousand pounds, which is two million, nine hundred and twenty five thousand pounds. Only the excess over that threshold, five million and seventy five thousand pounds, can be surrendered to Tarnmoor Engineering.

What happens to the other eight hundred and twenty five thousand pounds? Until twenty fifteen, a company could set its own losses and management expenses against a C F C charge. That relief was repealed. So the eight hundred and twenty five thousand pounds can neither shelter the C F C charge nor be surrendered this year. It is carried forward as surplus management expenses. The trap is real. At twenty five per cent, delaying eight hundred and twenty five thousand pounds of relief means two hundred and six thousand two hundred and fifty pounds of tax paid earlier than the group expected.

Group Year Two repeats the pattern. Management expenses are eight point five million pounds, and the arm's length recharge is two point two million pounds. The excess over gross profits is six point three million pounds. The threshold adds the same eight hundred and twenty five thousand pounds of C F C profit, so the group can surrender five million, four hundred and seventy five thousand pounds.

A separate pot runs alongside. Tarnmoor P L C's interest costs give it a non-trading loan relationship deficit, six point five nine million pounds in Group Year One after the corporate interest restriction. A deficit is not a restricted amount, so it can be surrendered in full whatever the company's own profits. Chapter twelve explains it.

Now the surplus that stays behind. Surplus management expenses do not die. Section twelve twenty three of the two thousand and nine Act carries them forward and treats them as management expenses of the next accounting period. But since the twenty seventeen reforms, they come with three conditions that current period expenses do not have.

First, the deduction needs a claim. The company must claim within two years after the end of the later period in which it wants the deduction, and it may claim only part.

Second, carried forward expenses lose their priority. They do not come off total profits first. They wait in line with other brought forward losses.

Third, they fall within the loss restriction in Part seven Z A of the twenty ten Act. A company or group can set carried forward losses against profits up to a deductions allowance of five million pounds a year, and then against only half of the profits above it. Carried forward management expenses count towards that limit with carried forward trading losses, deficits and capital losses. Chapter fourteen covers the group's single allowance and the nomination that shares it out.

There is also a group route. Carried forward management expenses from accounting periods beginning on or after the first of April, twenty seventeen, can be surrendered to group companies under the rules for group relief of carried forward losses. Chapter fifteen explains those rules too.

For Tarnmoor P L C, this matters because its own profits are always consumed by its current management expenses, which come first. Its carried forward eight hundred and twenty five thousand pounds a year will rarely find room in its own computation. The practical route is a later surrender to Tarnmoor Engineering, within the group's five million pounds allowance. Relief delayed, not lost, provided someone remembers the claim.

When the owner changes.

Carried forward reliefs have value, and value attracts buyers. If a company with a pile of surplus management expenses could be bought, its business replaced by a profitable one, and the old expenses set against the new profits, the tax system would be selling relief. Chapter three of Part fourteen of the twenty ten Act stops that.

It applies where there is a change in the ownership of a company with investment business and one of three conditions is met. A change in ownership broadly means that one person acquires more than half of the ordinary share capital, or that several people, each acquiring at least five per cent, together acquire more than half. Inserting a new holding company on top of an existing group is disregarded.

Condition A is a significant increase in the company's capital after the change. The capital after the change must exceed the capital before it by at least one million pounds and must also be at least one hundred and twenty five per cent of it. Both tests must be met. Capital here means paid-up share capital, including share premium, together with certain debts and redeemable loan capital. The statute measures each side by the highest amount of capital that was kept in place for at least sixty days, and H M R C's manual says the period looked at after the change is now five years.

Condition B is a major change in the nature or conduct of the business within a window of eight years beginning three years before the change. That includes a major change in the nature of the investments the company holds, and a change that happens gradually still counts.

Condition C is that the change happens while the business is small or negligible, before any significant revival.

If any condition is met, the accounting period is notionally split at the change. Management expenses, non-trading deficits and similar amounts from before the change cannot be carried forward against profits after it. Chapter two A of the same Part runs alongside for reliefs from periods beginning on or after the first of April, twenty seventeen, and chapter fourteen covers it with the trading company rules.

Now a labelled hypothetical, not part of our story. Suppose an activist investor bought more than half of Tarnmoor P L C's shares. That is a change in ownership. Suppose Tarnmoor P L C was then carrying forward two million pounds of surplus management expenses. Suppose too that within the window the new owner sold the engineering subsidiaries and turned the parent into a holder of a property portfolio. That is a major change in the nature of its investments. The two million pounds could not be used against profits after the change. A share issue large enough to lift the capital by a million pounds and by a quarter would have the same effect. For a listed parent with a large balance sheet, Condition B is the realistic risk.

Going further.

Management expenses are where tax evidence is won or lost before anyone files a return.

Record the decision. The Centrica boundary turns on what money was spent on. A board minute that fixes when the group decided to pursue a specific target, and engagement letters that separate the strategic review from the deal, are the best evidence a tax team can have. Tom Hesketh's team asks for both at the start of every project.

Price the recharges. After H M R C's twenty twenty five letter campaign, every large parent should check that costs which serve subsidiaries, especially overseas ones, are recharged at arm's length and documented under the transfer pricing records rules. Otherwise the costs fall out of management expenses, and the parent is still exposed to a transfer pricing adjustment.

Map the surplus. Each year the group should calculate the profit-related threshold, including any C F C apportionment, before deciding how much to surrender. The group head of tax should also keep a register of carried forward management expenses, with the two year claim deadlines and the change of ownership windows.

Think about deferred tax. Surplus management expenses carried forward work like unused tax losses. A deferred tax asset is recognised only so far as it is probable that future taxable profits will be available to use them. Chapter six covers the judgement.

Finally, interaction. For the corporate interest restriction, each company's tax earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, its tax E B I T D A, include its current period management expenses but not amounts brought forward. That is why Tarnmoor P L C shows a negative figure in chapter twenty eight. And for a large business, an arguable position on the capital boundary may need to be reported under the uncertain tax treatment rules if it crosses their threshold, as chapter four explains. A good adviser spots all of these and calls in the specialists.

One more task belongs to the tax function. When a surrender is planned, the parent's consent and the claimant's claim must be made in the returns, and the amounts must match the threshold calculation. A group that surrenders the whole excess over gross profits, forgetting the C F C apportionment, will have over-surrendered. The claim will then have to be reduced, and the claimant will owe more tax, with interest. The cure is cheap. The threshold calculation should sit in the same working paper as the group relief schedule, reviewed by the same person, before the returns are filed.

How the examiner tests it.

This topic appears often but quietly. Four papers in a row have used it. In May twenty twenty three, a ten mark question listed acquisition costs, including due diligence, legal fees, a loan arrangement fee, compensation for loss of office and retention bonuses. Most candidates did well, but some missed that the arrangement fee is a loan relationship cost. In May twenty twenty four, a twenty mark question on a multinational parent began with its administrative expenses, and most candidates did not spot the investment business. In May twenty twenty five, a twenty mark question dealt with share disposals by an investment company. In May twenty twenty six, a twenty mark computation combined an investment company landlord with a loss-making trading subsidiary, group relief and lease premiums.

The style is technical prose and computation, with half a mark to a mark per point. Expect the instruction to calculate, with explanations. The traps are consistent. Deducting capital deal costs after the decision to proceed. Forgetting that management expenses come off total profits first. Forgetting the claim and the fifty per cent restriction on carried forward expenses. Missing the loan relationship treatment of finance fees. And, in a group question, treating the whole excess as surrenderable when the threshold, or a C F C apportionment, limits it.

The layout the examiner expects is the corporation tax computation. For the investment company, list its income by type, with exempt dividends shown and labelled as exempt so the marker sees you considered them. Then deduct management expenses as the first relief, with a short note for each item you excluded and why. Capital deal costs after the decision. Brokerage and stamp duty. Finance fees, which go to loan relationships. Costs of a subsidiary's trade. Then show the surplus, the threshold and the amount available for group relief. Keep the whole table in pounds or the whole table in thousands of pounds, never a mix. The examiners warned about exactly that mixing after the May twenty twenty six sitting, in the investment company question.

One more pattern is worth knowing. The investment company rarely appears on its own. It arrives as the parent in a group relief question, as the landlord in a property question, or as the seller in a shareholding exemption question. The marks for management expenses are often the easy marks hidden inside a bigger question, so bank them first.

What to take away.

A company with investment business is any company whose business consists wholly or partly in making investments. Since the first of April, two thousand and four, that includes a company that also trades. A group parent and a property letting company are the classic examples.

Expenses of management of the investment business are deducted from total profits, and current period expenses come first, before any other deduction. Exempt dividends do not reduce them.

An expense of management is a cost of managing the company's own investment business. It is not brokerage or stamp duty on buying an investment, not the cost of a subsidiary's trade, and not a cost that another provision already deducts.

Capital expenses are excluded. Centrica Overseas Holdings versus H M R C, decided by the Supreme Court in twenty twenty four, held that the capital test is the same as for traders. Just over two and a half million pounds of fees for selling Oxxio were capital. H M R C's view is that appraisal costs are revenue, and acquisition costs begin, at the earliest, at the decision to acquire. Record that decision.

In our invented case, Tarnmoor P L C allowed three hundred thousand pounds of strategic review costs and excluded nine hundred thousand pounds of post-decision Calder deal costs. Its management expenses were eight million pounds in Group Year One and eight point five million pounds in Group Year Two.

Other gates apply. Unallowable purpose, the accounts timing rule, the clawback of reversed expenses, and the nine month rule for remuneration. Excess capital allowances on head office assets become management expenses, and so do some other deemed items. Excess charitable donations of an investment business can be carried forward.

Surplus management expenses can be surrendered as group relief only above the profit-related threshold. That is gross profits plus any apportioned C F C profits. Tarnmoor P L C's surplus over gross profits was five point nine million pounds, but only five million and seventy five thousand pounds could go to Tarnmoor Engineering. Eight hundred and twenty five thousand pounds is carried forward.

Carried forward management expenses need a claim within two years, lose their priority, and sit inside the five million pounds allowance and the fifty per cent restriction.

After a change in ownership, carried forward management expenses are lost if there is a significant increase in capital of at least one million pounds and twenty five per cent, a major change in the business within eight years beginning three years before the change, or a change while the business is small or negligible.

The question was which of a parent's costs the tax system recognises, and when. The answer is the costs of running its own investments, judged as a trader's would be, used first, and used fully only if the company keeps its business and remembers its claims.

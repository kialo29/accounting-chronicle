# Chapter sixteen: Company gains: property and leases

On 30 April GY3, Tarnmoor Estates Ltd (TES) exchanged contracts to sell its depot for £6.0m. Tarnmoor, its companies, its people and its numbers are invented for teaching, as they are throughout this book. TES is the group's property company. It had bought the depot in June 2004 for £2.5m, and for all those years it had let the building to Tarnmoor Engineering Ltd (TEL), which used it for its trade.

A gain of £3.5m, on the face of it. Yet the group's tax function knew that the corporation tax due on that sale could be anything from about £200,000 to more than £800,000, depending on three things: what the law lets the company deduct; what the group does with the money next; and when the sale is treated as happening.

This chapter works through those questions for land, buildings and leases. It is the second chapter of Part Three. Corporation tax on chargeable gains, computation of gains, market value and connected parties, assets and disposals, allowable deductions, leases of land, part disposals, chattels, post-transaction valuations, replacement of business assets, stock in trade, property income and transactions in land are all **core (grade 1)** on the LCG grid; wasting assets, ss 48–52 and indexation are **non-core (grade 2)**. The chapter builds on *The Living Law* (TKS), chapter 23, which taught company gains at the first level: indexation, the matching rules, the shareholding exemption and roll-over relief in outline. Here we take the property side to Advanced Technical depth.

The law is mostly in the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act 1992 (TCGA 1992). Property income sits in the Corporation Tax Act 2009 (CTA 2009) and the transactions in land rules in the Corporation Tax Act 2010 (CTA 2010). We apply the law for FY2026.

## A gain in the corporation tax computation

A company does not pay capital gains tax. Its chargeable gains are computed under TCGA 1992 and then fed into its corporation tax computation, where they become part of **total profits** (TCGA 1992 s 2A; CG40200) and are taxed at the company's ordinary rate: for a large company in FY2026, **25%**. There is no annual exempt amount for a company and no separate rate for gains.

**Territorial scope (s 2B).** A UK-resident company is chargeable on gains on assets wherever situated, subject to the foreign branch exemption (CTA 2009 Part 2 Ch 3A; chapter 25). A non-resident company is chargeable on gains on **UK land**, on assets deriving **at least 75%** of their value from UK land where it has a substantial indirect interest (Sch 1A), and on assets used for a **UK permanent establishment**. FA 2026 Sch 7 para 28 rewrote the PE link (attribution under CTA 2009 ss 20 and 24) for chargeable periods beginning on or after **1 January 2026**; FA 2026 s 40 treats each cell of a protected cell company as a separate company for the Sch 1A test (disposals on or after 26 November 2025). Chapter 23 owns the non-resident rules.

**Order of computation.** For each accounting period: chargeable gains, less allowable losses of the same period, less capital losses brought forward. The net figure enters total profits. Excess losses carry forward, without time limit, against future gains only.

**Traps.**

- A **capital loss can never be set against income** (trading profits, rents, interest). The examiners reported after M26 (Q4) that candidates still did so. The reverse works: a trading loss under CTA 2010 s 37 is set against total profits, which include gains.
- A capital loss must be **quantified and notified** to HMRC (TCGA 1992 s 16(2A)), in practice through the company's return.
- Since **1 April 2020**, carried-forward capital losses fall within the **loss restriction** (CTA 2010 Part 7ZA; chapter 14): above the £5m deductions allowance they shelter only 50% of chargeable gains. In a group whose allowance is allocated elsewhere (Tarnmoor allocates the whole £5m to TEL in GY1–GY4), a capital loss brought forward may be worth much less than its face value.

Inside a group a gain in one company and a loss in another can be matched by a **joint election** without moving any asset (s 171A). Chapter 17 owns that election and the no gain, no loss transfers beside it. This chapter stays with a single company and its property.

## Disposals, dates and connected persons

**Assets and disposals (ss 21–28).** All forms of property are assets, including options, debts, rights and property created by the company. A disposal includes a sale, a gift, a part disposal and the receipt of a **capital sum derived from an asset** (s 22: compensation, insurance proceeds, sums for forfeiture or surrender of rights). Under s 24 a company can make a **negligible value claim**, being treated as selling and reacquiring the asset at that value, which crystallises the loss.

**Date of disposal (s 28).** For an asset sold under a contract, the disposal is made **when the contract is made**, not when the asset is conveyed; for a **conditional contract**, when the condition is satisfied. TES's depot was sold on **30 April GY3**, the date contracts were exchanged unconditionally, although completion came later. The date decides the accounting period, the instalment payments and the roll-over window.

**Market value (s 17) and connected persons (ss 18, 286).** The consideration is replaced by market value where the disposal is not a bargain at arm's length, or is a gift. Between **connected persons** the transaction is always treated as not at arm's length (s 18(2)), so market value applies whatever the contract says. Companies under common control are connected with each other; a company is connected with the person who controls it (s 286(5)–(6)).

**Clogged losses (s 18(3)).** A loss on a disposal to a connected person can be set only against gains on other disposals **to that same person**, at a time when they are still connected (CG14561). Within a gains group, s 171 overrides market value altogether (s 171(6)), so in practice the rule bites on disposals to companies that are **connected but outside the gains group**: a 60% subsidiary, or a company controlled by the same outside investor.

**Series of transactions (ss 19–20).** Where two or more **linked transactions** with connected persons fall within **6 years** ending with the last of them, and the market value of the assets in a transaction is less than their **appropriate portion of the aggregate market value** of everything disposed of in the series, each is treated as made for that appropriate portion. Each later transaction reopens the earlier computations (s 19(4); CG14650, CG14740). The rule stops a group breaking a valuable whole into cheap pieces. HMRC's guidance on its interaction with group transfers is at CG14700–CG14702.

## What it cost, and the frozen thermometer

**Allowable deductions (s 38).**

1. **Acquisition cost** and the incidental costs of acquisition (legal fees, surveyors' fees, SDLT) (s 38(1)(a)).
2. **Enhancement expenditure**: expenditure wholly and exclusively incurred to enhance the asset's value, "being expenditure reflected in the state or nature of the asset at the time of the disposal"; and expenditure on establishing, preserving or defending title (s 38(1)(b)).
3. **Incidental costs of disposal** (s 38(1)(c)).

**Exclusions.** Nothing deductible in computing income (s 39), so repairs deducted against rents are not deducted again. **Capital allowances never reduce a gain**: they restrict only a **loss** (s 41; chapter 9).

> **Worked example 16.1: the demolished extension (hypothetical, not a story fact; the M26 Q3 pattern)**
>
> A company bought a freehold factory for £3,000,000 (after December 2017: no indexation). It later spent £400,000 on a workshop extension, which it demolished before the sale, and £600,000 on a new wing that still stands. It sells for £5,000,000 with £50,000 of agents' and legal fees.
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Proceeds | 5,000,000 |
> | Less: incidental costs of disposal | (50,000) |
> | Less: cost | (3,000,000) |
> | Less: enhancement reflected in the asset at disposal (new wing) | (600,000) |
> | Demolished workshop: not reflected in the asset at disposal | nil |
> | **Chargeable gain** | **1,350,000** |

**Indexation allowance (s 53).** Companies kept indexation after it ended for individuals. It increases each item of allowable expenditure by the rise in the RPI from the month it was incurred, **but only to December 2017** (s 53(1B), inserted by FA 2018; RPI December 2017 = 278.1). *The Living Law*, chapter 23, compared it to a thermometer that stopped. Assets bought after 2017 carry none. Indexation **cannot create or increase a loss** (s 53(2A)): if proceeds are below cost, indexation is nil; if the unindexed gain is smaller than the indexation, the result is no gain, no loss. The examiners reported after M26 (Q4) and M25 (Q5) that candidates still used indexation to enlarge a loss. The indexation factor is (RPI December 2017 − RPI month of expenditure) / RPI month of expenditure, **rounded to three decimal places**; the exam's tax tables supply the RPI.

> **Worked example 16.2: TES's depot, GY3 (invented)**
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Proceeds (contract exchanged 30 April GY3) | 6,000,000 |
> | Less: cost (June 2004) | (2,500,000) |
> | Unindexed gain | 3,500,000 |
> | Less: indexation 0.489 × £2,500,000 (June 2004 → December 2017) | (1,222,500) |
> | **Chargeable gain before reliefs** | **2,277,500** |
> | CT at 25% with no relief | 569,375 |
>
> The factor: RPI June 2004 186.8; (278.1 − 186.8) / 186.8 = 0.48876 → **0.489** (verified in TKS chapter 23).

**The chapter 3 link.** TES paid its GY2 instalments on a forecast that included this gain. The sale slipped to 30 April GY3, so TES overpaid by exactly **£569,375** and surrendered £400,000 of the refund to TEL (chapter 3, worked example 3.6). The date of disposal moved the tax by a whole year.

## Selling part of an asset, and proving the value

**Part disposals (s 42).** The cost attributed to the part disposed of is

> cost × **A / (A + B)**

where **A** is the consideration for the part disposed of and **B** is the market value of the part retained. The balance of cost stays with the retained part. Expenditure wholly attributable to one part (for example a road built only on the land sold) is allocated directly. The apportionment is made before any no gain, no loss or roll-over adjustment.

> **Worked example 16.3: a strip of land (hypothetical, not a story fact)**
>
> Cost of site (after December 2017) £1,200,000; strip sold for £500,000; land retained worth £1,500,000.
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Proceeds | 500,000 |
> | Cost: £1,200,000 × 500,000 / (500,000 + 1,500,000) | (300,000) |
> | **Chargeable gain** | **200,000** |
> | Cost carried forward for the land retained | 900,000 |

**Valuations.** B is not a price anyone paid: it is a valuation. So are market value on a connected-party disposal and the value of a reversion on a lease grant. HMRC offers **post-transaction valuation checks** on form **CG34**, for CT as well as CGT: after the disposal and, on HMRC's guidance, at least **3 months before the filing date**. If HMRC agrees the valuation, it will not challenge it when the return is filed, provided full and accurate facts were given. Shares and Assets Valuation values shares and other assets; the **Valuation Office Agency** values land. (The CG manual's PTVC pages, CG16600–CG16601, were archived on 22 April 2026, but the CG34 publication page and the SAV guidance remain live.)

> **Going further: valuation as a control.** A tax function builds the CG34 step into the timetable for every material disposal that rests on a valuation: part disposals of land, lease grants (the reversion value), connected-party transfers outside the gains group, and appropriations to stock. The valuation report, the instructions given to the valuer, and HMRC's response go on the disposal file. It is free, and it removes one of the commonest causes of enquiry into a gains computation.

## Wasting assets, chattels and money paid later

**Wasting assets (ss 44–47).** A wasting asset has a predictable life of **50 years or less**; plant and machinery is always treated as having such a life (s 44(1)(c); CG15440). For most wasting assets the cost is written off on a **straight line** over the life (s 46). Leases of land follow their own curve (Sch 8; below).

**Plant and machinery that qualified for capital allowances.**

- Wasting **chattels** are normally exempt (s 45(1)), but **not** if they qualified (or could have qualified) for capital allowances (s 45(2); CG76721).
- Where the asset qualified for allowances in full, the straight-line write-off is switched off (s 47), and any **loss** is restricted by the allowances given (s 41).
- Result: plant sold **below cost** gives no gain and, after s 41, **no allowable loss** (the capital allowances system has dealt with the fall in value). Plant sold **above cost** gives a chargeable gain on the excess, while the capital allowances disposal value is capped at cost.
- **Chattels (s 262):** a chattel disposed of for **£6,000 or less** is exempt, even if it qualified for allowances (CG15440); above £6,000 the gain is capped at **5/3 × (consideration − £6,000)**.
- **Fixed plant** forming part of a building is not a chattel: it is part of the land.

The examiners mixed these rules into M26 Q3 (15 marks). The best answers sorted each asset first: building, fixed plant or movable plant.

**Deferred consideration (s 48).** Ascertainable deferred consideration is brought in **at once, without discount** for waiting and without regard to the risk of non-recovery; if it later proves irrecoverable, the computation is adjusted on a claim. **For companies**, s 48(1) does not apply to consideration consisting of rights under a **creditor relationship**: it is brought in at **fair value** (s 48(2)–(4); CG14930), and the debt is then taxed under the loan relationship rules (chapter 12).

**Unascertainable consideration** (an earn-out dependent on future profits) is a separate asset, a chose in action (*Marren v Ingles*): chapter 18 tells that story with the sale of Tarnmoor Actuators.

**Tax by instalments (s 280).** Where consideration is payable by instalments over more than **18 months**, the tax may be paid by instalments over up to **8 years**, as HMRC allow (CG14910; HMRC normally expects 50% of each instalment of consideration). It is a cash-flow relief, not a reduction of the gain, and it is not available for unascertainable consideration.

> **Worked example 16.4: plant on a site sale (hypothetical, not a story fact)**
>
> | Asset | Cost £ | Proceeds £ | Gains result |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Movable press (main pool) | 80,000 | 30,000 | No gain; loss restricted to nil by allowances (s 41). CA disposal value £30,000 |
> | Movable test cell (main pool) | 80,000 | 90,000 | Gain £10,000 (no wasting restriction: s 47; no s 45 exemption). CA disposal value capped at £80,000 |
> | Small compressor (chattel) | 4,000 | 5,000 | Exempt: consideration not above £6,000 (s 262) |
> | Fixed crane rail (part of building) | — | — | Part of the land: in the building's computation; no chattel rule |

## Leases as a melting block of ice

A lease ends. A lease with 999 years to run is, for practical purposes, as good as the freehold; one with two years to run is nearly worthless. So a lease of land is a **wasting asset only once 50 years or less remain** (Sch 8 para 1(1)); a lease with more than 50 years to run is a long lease and is not a wasting asset at all.

Think of a lease with 50 years left as a block of ice. It melts slowly at first, then faster towards the end. A lease with 25 years left is worth **much more** than half a 50-year lease, because the value collapses in the last few years. The law captures the curve in the **lease percentage table** (Sch 8 para 1(6)), reproduced in the CTA tax tables:

| Years unexpired | Percentage |
|---|---|
| 50 | 100 |
| 25 | 81.100 |
| 10 | 46.695 |
| 1 | 5.983 |

**The restriction (Sch 8 para 1(3)–(4)).** On the disposal of a short lease, the expenditure excluded is (P1 − P3)/P1 of the acquisition cost, where P1 is the table percentage for the duration at acquisition and P3 at disposal; in other words, **allowable cost = cost × P3/P1**. Enhancement expenditure is restricted separately, using P2 (the percentage when it was incurred) (CG71141). **Indexation** is computed on the **restricted** expenditure, not the original cost (CG17380).

**Two rules decide whether the table applies.**

1. **Status at the transaction date.** A lease bought with 60 years to run and sold with 45 left is a short lease when sold; HMRC's guidance confirms that the short-lease rules apply even though it was long when acquired. The examiners stressed this after N25 (Q5).
2. **Assignment v grant.** An **assignment** is the sale of an existing lease: the whole lease passes and the computation is a simple disposal of a wasting asset. A **grant** creates a new lease out of a larger interest: a part disposal and, for a short lease, an income charge on the premium. The examiners reported after N24 (Q1) that candidates treated assignments of short leases as grants.

**The misconception.** "Any premium received for a short lease is partly income." It is not. Only a premium on the **grant** of a lease of 50 years or less has an income element (CTA 2009 s 217). A price received on an **assignment** is pure capital (subject to the special rule for assigning a lease granted at an undervalue: CTA 2009 s 221; CG71210).

> **Worked example 16.5: TES assigns a leasehold office, GY3 (invented)**
>
> TES held a leasehold office as an investment (not used in any group trade, so no roll-over). It bought the lease with exactly **50 years** unexpired for **£800,000** and assigned it in GY3, with exactly **25 years** unexpired, to an unconnected buyer for **£1,000,000**.
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Proceeds | 1,000,000 |
> | Allowable cost: £800,000 × 81.100 / 100 | (648,800) |
> | (Cost wasted: £151,200, under a fifth of the cost for half the term) | |
> | Unindexed gain | 351,200 |
> | Less: indexation on restricted cost, 0.250 × £648,800 (factor assumed for the story) | (162,200) |
> | **Chargeable gain** | **189,000** |
>
> **Why indexation.** A lease bought with 50 years to run and sold with 25 left has been held for 25 years, so TES bought it long before December 2017. In our invented case the indexation factor from the month of purchase to December 2017 is **assumed to be 0.250**, a round figure chosen for the story (the book never dates Group Years). In the exam, compute the factor from the RPI in the tax tables and round it to three decimal places.

## Granting a lease for a premium

Why should a premium ever be income? Because rent and premium are two ways of being paid for the same thing. A landlord can let a warehouse for 30 years at a full rent, or at a lower rent plus a large payment up front. If the up-front payment were all capital, landlords would convert rent into premium. So **CTA 2009 ss 217–221** treat part of a premium on a short lease as **property income**.

**The income element (s 217).** Where a lease of **50 years or less** is granted for a premium, the amount taxed as property income is

> **P × (50 − Y) / 50**

where P is the premium and Y is the number of complete periods of 12 months in the term other than the first (so Y = n − 1 for a lease of n complete years). For a **30-year** lease: (50 − 29)/50 = **21/50 = 42%**. Each extra year of term takes 2% off: a premium for a one-year lease is taxed wholly as income, one for a 50-year lease only 2%. The rules also reach work the tenant is required to carry out (s 218), sums payable instead of rent (s 219) and sums for the surrender of a lease (s 220).

**The capital part and the part disposal.** The rest of the premium is capital, and the grant out of a freehold (or long lease) is a **part disposal** of the landlord's interest. The income element is excluded from the consideration (TCGA 1992 Sch 8 para 5(1)). **HMRC's guidance (CG70960)** then adjusts the s 42 fraction: the **numerator** uses only the premium **not** taxed as property income, while the **denominator** uses the **full premium** plus the value of the reversion:

> allowable cost = cost × (premium − income element) / (full premium + value of reversion)

HMRC's own example: premium £350,000, income element £35,000; allowable cost £450,000 × 315,000 / (350,000 + 500,000) = £166,765. Many study texts and candidates use the full premium in the numerator, or the capital part in both lines; neither matches CG70960.

> **Worked example 16.6: TES grants a 30-year lease, GY4 (invented)**
>
> In GY1 TES bought a run-down freehold warehouse for **£1,500,000**. Values in the area rose fast. In GY4 it grants a **30-year lease** to an unconnected logistics operator for a premium of **£2,000,000** plus a yearly rent. After the grant, the freehold reversion is worth **£4,000,000**.
>
> *Property income (CTA 2009 s 217)*
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Premium | 2,000,000 |
> | Income element: £2,000,000 × (50 − 29) / 50 | **840,000** |
>
> *Chargeable gain (TCGA 1992 s 42; Sch 8 para 5; CG70960)*
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Premium | 2,000,000 |
> | Less: amount taxed as property income | (840,000) |
> | Capital consideration | 1,160,000 |
> | Less: cost £1,500,000 × 1,160,000 / (2,000,000 + 4,000,000) | (290,000) |
> | Indexation (bought after December 2017) | nil |
> | **Chargeable gain** | **870,000** |
> | Cost carried forward with the reversion (£1,500,000 − £290,000) | 1,210,000 |
>
> *Effect for TES in GY4*
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Property income | 840,000 |
> | Chargeable gain | 870,000 |
> | Taxable in GY4 from a £2,000,000 premium | 1,710,000 |
> | CT at 25% | 427,500 |
>
> *For comparison only (not HMRC's method):* full premium in both lines: cost £500,000, gain £660,000; capital part in both lines: cost £337,209, gain £822,791.
>
> *The tenant:* a trading tenant deducts the landlord's taxed receipt spread daily over the receipt period (CTA 2009 ss 62–67): £840,000 / 30 = **£28,000 a year**. Rent is deductible as it accrues.

**For a tenant, rent beats premium on timing**; for a landlord, a premium brings tax forward. That is a negotiating point as much as a tax point.

**Two variations.**

- **Grant of a long lease** (more than 50 years): no income element; the whole premium is consideration for an ordinary part disposal, with the full premium as A. M26 Q4 (20 marks) set the grant of a long lease in an investment company, and also tested that indexation cannot create a loss.
- **Sub-lease granted out of a short lease** (Sch 8 para 4): the cost used up is measured with the lease table, not A/(A+B), and the income element is **deducted from the gain** rather than the proceeds, but not so as to create a loss (Sch 8 para 5(2)). (Para 4's formula was not re-opened for this chapter: check it in the legislation if a question needs it.)

## Property income for a company

TES's rents and its premium income fall into one **UK property business** (CTA 2009 Part 4). For a company, all its lettings of UK land form a single business; profits are computed on trading principles, following GAAP (s 210 applies the trading income rules). Rents are taxed as they accrue; repairs, insurance, agents' fees and building running costs are deducted; capital allowances are available on plant used in the business (lifts, heating), though not on plant in a dwelling.

**Interest is a loan relationship debit, not a property expense.** A company's interest on borrowings to buy or improve property is dealt with under the loan relationship rules (chapter 12). TES's £4.2m a year of interest on its £70m loan from TFL is a non-trading debit; TES sets its non-trading deficit against its own property profits by claim (CTA 2009 s 463B).

**Timing differs from the accounts.** A landlord's accounts usually spread a premium over the lease. The income element is taxed in the accounting period in which the lease is **granted**, so the accounts are adjusted and the difference is a timing difference for deferred tax (chapter 6). TES's £840,000 is taxed in GY4 whatever its accounts show for that year.

**Reverse premiums (CTA 2009 ss 96–98, 250).** A payment by a landlord to induce a company to take an interest in land is a **revenue receipt**: a trading receipt if the company takes the premises for its trade (s 98), otherwise a receipt of its property business (s 250). It is normally taxed as the accounts spread it, but where parties are **connected and the terms are not at arm's length**, the whole amount is taxed in the period of the transaction (s 250(5); BIM41055).

**Property losses (CTA 2010 s 62).** A UK property business loss is set against the company's **total profits** of the same period; any excess carries forward against total profits of later periods (within Part 7ZA) or can be surrendered as group relief, but only so far as it exceeds the company's gross profits (CTA 2010 s 105; chapter 15).

## Why roll-over relief exists, and how it works

A business that sells its factory to move to a better one has not cashed out: it has swapped one productive asset for another. Taxing the gain would take cash out of the business as it reinvests. **Roll-over relief (TCGA 1992 ss 152–158)** lets a trader postpone the gain by deducting it from the cost of the replacement. The tax is **deferred, not forgiven**: it returns when the new asset is sold, unless that gain is rolled over again.

**Conditions (state them: marking guides give marks for them).**

| Condition | Detail | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Old asset | Used **only** for the trade **throughout** the period of ownership; otherwise the gain is apportioned and only the trade part qualifies | s 152(1), (6)–(7) |
| New asset | Acquired for the trade and **taken into use** for it on acquisition | s 152(1) |
| Classes | Both assets in qualifying classes (not necessarily the same class). **Class 1**: Head A land and buildings occupied and used only for the trade; Head B fixed plant or machinery not part of a building. Also ships, aircraft, satellites, quotas and others | s 155 |
| Companies and goodwill | Classes 4–7A do not apply where the new asset is a chargeable intangible; goodwill and intangibles go to the CTA 2009 Part 8 reinvestment relief (chapter 11) | s 156ZB |
| Window | **12 months before to 3 years after** the disposal (HMRC may extend) | s 152(3) |
| Claim | Within the 4-year time limit for company claims | FA 1998 Sch 18 para 55 |
| Provisional claim | A declaration in the return of intention to reinvest; for a company it **lapses on the 4th anniversary of the end of the AP of disposal**, and tax becomes due with interest from the normal date | s 153A |
| Groups | Trades of group members treated as one; non-trading member holding assets used only for group trades can claim; both companies claim (chapter 17) | s 175 |

**Partial reinvestment (s 153): the rule at the heart of the topic.** Where only part of the proceeds is reinvested, the amount **chargeable now is the proceeds not reinvested, capped at the gain**; the rest of the gain is rolled over. If the proceeds not reinvested **equal or exceed the gain, no relief** is available at all.

**The misconception.** "Roll-over taxes the part of the gain you did not reinvest." It taxes the part of the **proceeds** you did not reinvest. The law assumes that cost comes back first and gain last: relief begins only once the company has reinvested more than cost plus indexation, and the whole gain is rolled over only when the whole proceeds are reinvested. The examiners reported after N24 (Q1) and M26 (Q3) that candidates computed the chargeable amount from the gain.

## The depot, the distribution centre and the proceeds not reinvested

On **1 September GY3**, TEL bought a new distribution centre from a developer for **£5,200,000** (land £1.2m; integral features £0.6m; structure £3.4m: chapter 9), four months after the depot sale and well inside the window, and took it straight into use for its trade. TES had let the depot to TEL, which used it **only for its trade throughout TES's ownership**. Two companies are involved, so the claim is a **group** claim under s 175, signed by both; chapter 17 sets out those conditions. The arithmetic is the same as for a single company.

> **Worked example 16.7: group roll-over of the depot gain, GY3 (invented)**
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Proceeds of depot (TES) | 6,000,000 |
> | Gain (worked example 16.2) | 2,277,500 |
> | Reinvested: TEL's distribution centre | 5,200,000 |
> | **Proceeds not reinvested: chargeable now** (lower of £800,000 and the gain) | **800,000** |
> | **Gain rolled over** (£2,277,500 − £800,000) | **1,477,500** |
> | TEL's base cost of the distribution centre (£5,200,000 − £1,477,500) | **3,722,500** |
> | CT on £800,000 at 25% | 200,000 |
> | CT deferred (£1,477,500 × 25%) | 369,375 |
>
> Chapter 17 moves £500,000 of the £800,000 to TEL by a s 171A election, to meet TEL's £500,000 capital loss.

> | 5,200,000 (story) | 800,000 | 800,000 | 1,477,500 |
> | 4,000,000 | 2,000,000 | 2,000,000 | 277,500 |
> | 3,722,500 (cost + indexation) | 2,277,500 | 2,277,500 | nil |
> | 3,500,000 | 2,500,000 | 2,277,500 (capped at the gain) | nil |

**Three consequences the examiner likes.**

1. **The rolled-over gain is not lost.** When TEL sells the centre, its gain runs from **£3,722,500**.
2. **SBA comes back on sale.** TEL's structures and buildings allowance on the centre (£102,000 a year; £34,000 in GY3) is **added to its disposal consideration** on that later sale (chapter 9).
3. **Deferred tax.** In the group accounts, roll-over postpones tax; it does not cancel it (chapter 6).

> **Going further: making the claim work.** (1) Date the disposal by the contract, and diarise the window (here 30 April GY2 to 30 April GY6). (2) Evidence trade use of the old asset throughout ownership: leases to group companies, occupation records. (3) If reinvestment is planned but not made by the filing date, make a provisional claim and track its lapse date. (4) Allocate the purchase price of the new property between land, building and integral features with a surveyor's report (chapter 9): the gains cost is the whole price, but the capital allowances split matters separately. (5) Model the cash: here the relief saves £369,375 now, at the price of a lower base cost later.

## Depreciating assets and holdover

Rolling a gain into an asset that wears out would let the gain evaporate as the asset is depreciated. So where the new asset is a **depreciating asset**, its cost is **not reduced**. Instead the gain is **held over** and comes back into charge on the **earliest** of (s 154(2)):

1. the disposal of the new asset;
2. its ceasing to be used for the trade;
3. **10 years** after its acquisition.

A depreciating asset is a wasting asset, or one that **will become a wasting asset within 10 years**: an expected life of **60 years or less** at acquisition (s 154(7); CG60295). Fixed plant and machinery is always depreciating; so is a lease with 60 years or less to run; a freehold is not. A lease with 55 years to run is not yet a wasting asset (its own cost is not written down) but **is** a depreciating asset: a gain reinvested in it is held over, not rolled over.

The held-over gain is the old asset's gain, computed in the usual way, and is charged in the accounting period of the triggering event. N24 (Q1) included a gain held over years earlier coming back into charge. The new asset's own gain or loss on sale is computed separately, on its full cost.

**Escape route (s 154(4)).** If, before the held-over gain crystallises, the company buys a **non-depreciating** qualifying asset, it can switch the held-over gain into a full roll-over against it (partly, if only part can be matched: CG60285).

> **Worked example 16.9: holdover into fixed plant (hypothetical, not a story fact)**
>
> Suppose the depot proceeds had gone into fixed plant for a new production line costing £6,000,000. The plant's cost (and its capital allowances) stays at £6,000,000. The whole gain of **£2,277,500** is held over and crystallises no later than 10 years after the plant was acquired, unless a non-depreciating asset (a freehold) is bought in time to take it over.

The examiners reported after M26 (Q3) that candidates spent time on holdover where only roll-over was asked, and earned nothing for it. Recognise holdover; do not reach for it by reflex.

## Land that becomes stock

A property company can change its mind. **TCGA 1992 s 161** draws the line between investment gain and trading profit at the moment of **appropriation**.

- **Capital asset → trading stock (s 161(1)).** A deemed disposal at **market value**: the gain to date is a chargeable gain, and the stock enters the trade at market value.
- **Election (s 161(3)).** Within **2 years of the end of the accounting period** of appropriation (for CT), the company elects for no chargeable gain or loss; the stock enters at market value **less the gain** (or plus the loss). The whole profit is then taxed later as trading profit.
- **Trading stock → capital asset (s 161(2)).** The trade is treated as selling at market value (trading receipt), and for gains purposes the company acquires the asset at the value brought into the trading accounts.
- **Groups (s 173).** A group company acquiring as stock an asset another member held as capital is treated as appropriating it on arrival (chapter 17).

> **Worked example 16.10: a field becomes a housing site (hypothetical, not a story fact)**
>
> A company bought a field for £400,000 (after 2017) as an investment and appropriates it to trading stock to build and sell houses when it is worth £1,000,000.
>
> | | No election £ | s 161(3) election £ |
> |---|---|---|
> | Chargeable gain on appropriation | 600,000 | nil |
> | Stock enters the trade at | 1,000,000 | 400,000 |
> | Extra trading profit on later sales (relative to no election) | — | 600,000 |
> | **Choose when** | capital losses are available (they cannot touch trading profit) | trading losses are available, or deferral to the sale is worth more |
>
> The rate is 25% either way: the choice turns on losses and timing.

The examiners built M23 Q4 (20 marks) around a building appropriated **from stock to fixed assets** by a development company, and reported that many candidates missed the market value appropriation.

## Transactions in UK land

Until 2016, offshore developers could develop UK land through non-resident companies, claim no UK permanent establishment, and pay no UK tax on development profits. **FA 2016 s 77** inserted **CTA 2010 Part 8ZB (ss 356OA–356OT)**, replacing the old Part 18 "artificial transactions in land" code, for **disposals on or after 5 July 2016** (FA 2016 s 81(1)); arrangements made from Budget day, **16 March 2016**, were caught by anti-forestalling rules (s 81(4)–(12); s 356OK).

**The charge (s 356OB).** A profit or gain on a disposal of UK land realised by the person acquiring, holding or developing it (or an associate or a person party to an arrangement) is **treated as trading profit** if any of:

| Condition | Test |
|---|---|
| A | A main purpose of **acquiring** the land was to realise a profit or gain from disposing of it |
| B | A main purpose of acquiring property **deriving its value** from the land was to realise a profit or gain from disposing of the land |
| C | The land is held as **trading stock** |
| D | (Where the land is developed) a main purpose of **developing** it was to realise a profit or gain from disposing of it when developed |

The whole profit, including what would otherwise be a capital gain, is trading profit. A **non-resident** company caught by the rule is treated as carrying on a trade of dealing in or developing UK land (CTA 2009 s 5B) and is charged to CT whether or not it has a UK PE.

**Refinements.**

- **Indirect disposals (ss 356OD–356OE):** disposals of property (such as shares) deriving **at least 50%** of its value from UK land, where the person is party to an arrangement whose main purpose is to deal in or develop the land and realise a gain from disposals of property deriving value from it.
- **Pre-intention carve-out (s 356OL):** under Condition D, the part of the gain fairly attributable to the period **before the intention to develop** was formed is excluded (and so remains a capital gain).
- **No double charge (s 356OC):** the rule does not apply where the profit is already taxed as income.
- Anti-fragmentation (s 356OH), tracing value (s 356OM), related parties (s 356OT).

**Applying it to the field.** If the field was bought as a long-term investment and the decision to develop came later, Condition D catches the development profit but not the pre-intention growth in value; s 161 reaches the same dividing line from the other side. Had the company bought the field intending to obtain planning consent and sell, **Condition A** would make the whole profit trading from day one, whatever its accounts called the land.

> **Going further: documenting intention.** For TES, which buys and holds sites to let to the group, Part 8ZB is a reminder to record why each site is held: board papers stating that a site is held for group use or investment, and valuations at any change of plan. That evidence protects capital treatment and the pre-intention carve-out if HMRC asks.

## What to take away

- **Gains in CT:** part of total profits at the company's rate (25% for a large company in FY2026). Capital losses: same-period gains, then carried forward against gains only, within Part 7ZA. Never against income.
- **Disposals:** at the contract date (s 28); market value for gifts, non-arm's-length bargains and connected persons; clogged losses (s 18(3)); linked transactions within 6 years (s 19).
- **Costs:** acquisition, enhancement still reflected in the asset, disposal costs; capital allowances restrict only losses. Indexation to December 2017 only; never creates or increases a loss. **Depot:** £6.0m − £2.5m − £1,222,500 (0.489) = **£2,277,500**.
- **Part disposals:** A/(A+B); B is a valuation: use CG34.
- **Plant:** no allowable loss after capital allowances; a gain only above cost; chattels £6,000 and 5/3.
- **Deferred consideration:** in full at once; a company's deferred debt goes to loan relationships at fair value.
- **Leases:** wasting once 50 years or less remain; table 50 = 100, 25 = 81.100; status at the transaction date; assignment has no income element. **TES lease assignment:** £1.0m − £648,800 − £162,200 = **£189,000**.
- **Premiums:** P × (50 − Y)/50 as property income. **TES 30-year grant:** income **£840,000**; gain **£870,000** (cost £290,000 using the capital part over the full premium plus reversion, CG70960).
- **Roll-over:** trade use, classes, 12 months before to 3 years after, claim; chargeable = proceeds not reinvested, capped at the gain. **Depot:** £800,000 chargeable; **£1,477,500** rolled over; TEL's base cost **£3,722,500**. Into a depreciating asset: holdover for up to 10 years.
- **Appropriations:** market value deemed sale, or a 2-year election to fold the gain into stock.
- **Part 8ZB:** land acquired or developed with a main purpose of selling at a profit: trading profit, for residents and non-residents.

So the depot's £6.0m left TES chargeable on £800,000, not on £3.5m: the law deducted what the depot had cost in real terms to December 2017, and the group put most of the money back into a building used for its trade.

Chapter seventeen, on gains inside the group, explains how the group made that claim across two companies, and how it moved assets, gains and losses between its members without paying tax on the way.

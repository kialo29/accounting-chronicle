# Chapter 9: Buildings, fixtures, leasing and successions

On 1 September in Group Year Three (GY3), Tarnmoor Engineering Ltd (TEL) takes the keys to a new distribution centre. Our invented group, Tarnmoor, is a listed engineering group, and everything about it in this book is made up for teaching. The building is brand new, bought from the developer who built it, for £5.2m. To the tax system it is at least three things, and possibly four.

The ground it stands on gets no allowance at all. The heating, the lighting and the electrical systems are plant, and they join a pool. The concrete, steel and roof are a structure, and they earn a slow, straight-line allowance that will outlive most of the people in the room.

This chapter is about the assets that sit between land and machinery, and about the moments when allowances move from one owner to another. It is the second capital allowances chapter in Part Two. Chapter 8 dealt with plant and machinery in the pools. Here we take the structures and buildings allowance (SBA) at Advanced Technical depth, then fixtures, long funding leases (LFLs), successions, contributions and the rules that stop allowances being bought and sold. Almost every topic here is **core** on the LCG syllabus; allowance buying and freeport special tax sites are **awareness** only. *The Living Law* (TKS), chapter 12, taught the basics of plant, pools and the SBA. We build on it.

**Abbreviations.** CAA 2001 = Capital Allowances Act 2001 (spoken "the Capital Allowances Act"); TCGA 1992 = Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act 1992 ("the Gains Act"); CTA 2010 = Corporation Tax Act 2010 ("the twenty ten Act"). All law is the law for **FY2026** (1 April 2026 to 31 March 2027) after Finance Act 2026. FA 2026 left the SBA, fixtures and LFLs unchanged; its 14% main pool WDA (FA 2026 s 28) matters here because fixtures and leased plant often land in the main pool.

## One building, four tax assets

Start with the price. A building purchase contract usually gives one figure; the tax rules need it split, because each piece follows a different regime. Where property is sold together, the price is apportioned on a **just and reasonable** basis, whatever the parties call each part (CAA 2001 s 562). A contractual label does not bind HMRC if it is not just and reasonable.

| Piece | Examples | Tax treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Land | The site; rights over land | No allowance. SBA expressly excludes land acquisition costs (s 270BG); HMRC's manual (CA94010) adds that fees and SDLT relating to the land are excluded. Base cost for gains only |
| Structure | Walls, floors, roof, foundations, car parks, roads, bridges | SBA at 3% a year (Part 2A) |
| Fixtures that are plant | Integral features (s 33A: electrical and lighting systems, cold water, space or water heating, powered ventilation, air cooling and others); other fixed plant (sprinklers, racking bolted down) | P&M allowances: integral features to the **special rate pool** (6%); other plant usually to the **main pool** (14%) |
| Everything else | Land alteration (reclamation, remediation, landscaping that does not create a structure) | Nothing |

People assume that buying a building means buying allowances. Often it means buying allowances only on the parts the law recognises, only if the paperwork is right, and only at the rate the law sets for each piece. **A building is a bundle of tax assets, and the bundle is only as good as its weakest document.**

For TEL's distribution centre, the developer's price is split in our invented case as follows (a surveyor's report supports the split as just and reasonable):

| Component | £ |
|---|---|
| Land | 1,200,000 |
| Integral features | 600,000 |
| Structure | 3,400,000 |
| **Total price** | **5,200,000** |

## Why buildings got an allowance again

For years before 2018, most new commercial buildings in the UK earned no capital allowance: a company could claim for the machines inside a factory and for some fixtures, but the shell was relieved only on sale, against a gain. The Budget of 29 October 2018 filled that gap with the SBA.

- **Start date.** The SBA is available only where the construction contract was entered into on or after **29 October 2018** (CAA 2001 s 270AB). If any contract for the physical construction predates it, the building does not qualify at all.
- **Rate.** 2% a year originally; **3% from 1 April 2020** for companies (6 April 2020 for income tax), so the allowance now runs for **33⅓ years** (s 270AA).
- **Three design ideas.** (1) A straight-line allowance on original qualifying cost, not a pool: no balancing allowance or charge on sale. (2) The allowance belongs to the building: a buyer carries on claiming the same annual amount for the rest of the 33⅓ years. (3) Clawback is through the **gains** computation: the seller adds the SBA it claimed to its disposal consideration (TCGA 1992 s 37B; GOV.UK: "add the total amount of structures and buildings allowance you've claimed to your disposal receipts").
- **Non-residential use.** Dwellings and residential use are excluded. A period of disuse after qualifying use does not stop the allowance; a switch to residential use does.
- **Allowance period start.** The later of (a) the date the building is first brought into non-residential use and (b) the date the qualifying expenditure is incurred.
- **Older buildings.** A building constructed under pre-29 October 2018 contracts earns nothing, but **capital expenditure on later alterations, extensions or renovations** can qualify in its own right if contracted for after the start date. The May 2026 paper tested exactly this with an office built in 2010: many candidates gave SBA on the whole office; only the alterations qualified.

**TEL's GY2 extension (ledger).** TEL's GY2 capital allowances (£16.0m in total, chapter 8) include SBA on a **£1.96m extension** to an older factory, contracted for after the start date and in use throughout GY2: 3% × £1,960,000 = **£58,800**. The older factory earns nothing.

## What qualifies, and what does not

**Construction by the claimant.** Qualifying expenditure is the capital cost of construction. Site preparation counts, and HMRC's manual treats demolition of an existing building to clear the site as site preparation. Excluded: land; land alteration (other than site preparation); plant (which qualifies under Part 2 instead); planning permission costs.

**Purchases.** Three rules (and the examiner likes them):

| Building bought | Qualifying expenditure for the buyer | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Unused, from a **developer** (sold in the course of its development trade) | The **price paid** for the relevant interest, apportioned to exclude land and non-structure items; if there were further sales before first use, the lower of the last buyer's price and the developer's sale price | CAA 2001 s 270BD; CA93650 |
| Unused, from a **non-developer** | **Lower of** the price paid and the original construction expenditure | s 270BC; CA93500 |
| **Used** (after first use) | No relief on the price. The buyer steps into the seller's shoes and claims 3% of the **original** qualifying expenditure for the rest of the period, if it has an **allowance statement** | Part 2A; GOV.UK guidance |

HMRC's guidance adds that if a buyer pays more than market value, the claim is limited to market value.

> **Worked example: buying a used building (hypothetical, not a story fact)**
> A warehouse cost £2,000,000 to construct (post-October 2018 contract). A buyer later pays £3,000,000, of which £800,000 is land.
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Original qualifying expenditure | 2,000,000 |
> | Buyer's SBA a year (3%) | **60,000** |
> | Price paid for the structure (irrelevant to SBA) | 2,200,000 |
>
> The buyer claims £60,000 a year for the remaining period, not 3% of £2.2m.

**Demolition.** HMRC's manual: where the **whole** building is demolished it ceases to qualify, any unclaimed allowance is lost, and allowances already claimed are added to the consideration in the gains computation. Partial demolition is different: restoration works are new capital expenditure under the renovation and conversion provisions.

**Time apportionment.** The allowance is an annual amount, proportionately reduced for a period shorter than a year or for part-period qualifying use (s 270AA). Exam answers work to the nearest month ("All workings should be shown and made to the nearest month and pound unless the question specifies otherwise"). In November 2025 (Q4), faced with a new company's short first period, some candidates cut SBA to 1/9th of 3% a month instead of 1/12th. **The annual figure is the starting point; a month is always a twelfth of it.**

> **Exam lens: SBA**
> - **Grade:** "Buildings and structures allowances" **1 (core)** (2026 grid; check the 2027 grid when published).
> - **Past appearances:** M26 Q2 (15 marks: warehouse bought from a developer on the price paid excluding land and integral features; office built in 2010 where only alterations qualified, not planning permission); M25 Q4 (20 marks: SBA on second-hand buildings with conversion costs; the examiners found SBA qualifying expenditure weak); N25 Q4 (20 marks: SBA time-apportioned, 1/12th not 1/9th); M23 Q1 (20 marks: SBA on a new factory in a CT computation); N23 Q3(b) (CAs/SBA on a training facility: land, levelling, buildings).
> - **Style:** computations with explanations; 0.5–1 mark per point; conventional CA layout with SBA shown separately from the pools.
> - **Traps:** SBA on land, land alteration or integral features; SBA on a pre-29 October 2018 building beyond its alterations; using the price paid for a used building; months wrongly counted; forgetting the gains add-back on sale.

## Tarnmoor's distribution centre

TEL buys and pays for the distribution centre on **1 September GY3** and first uses it the same day, so the allowance period starts then. TEL's year ends on 31 December: 4 months of qualifying use in GY3.

> **Worked example: TEL's distribution centre (story, GY3)**
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Price paid to developer | 5,200,000 |
> | Less: land (no allowance) | (1,200,000) |
> | Less: integral features (P&M, special rate) | (600,000) |
> | **SBA qualifying expenditure (s 270BD)** | **3,400,000** |
> | SBA a year: 3% × £3,400,000 | 102,000 |
> | **GY3 SBA: £102,000 × 4/12** | **34,000** |
> | GY4 onwards (full year) | 102,000 |
> | Total over 33⅓ years | 3,400,000 |
>
> *Check by days (for interest only): £102,000 × 122/365 = £34,093. The exam convention (months) gives £34,000, the ledger figure.*

**The integral features (£600,000).** These are special rate expenditure. Do they also earn the **50% special rate FYA** (CAA 2001 s 45S; chapter 8)? That needs the plant to be **unused and not second-hand**. Fixtures in a new building bought straight from the developer arguably meet the test, because nobody has used them, but no HMRC statement settling the point for fixtures in a building bought from a developer was found in the research for this chapter. In our invented case the tax team logs the question and the computation takes the cautious route:

| Integral features, GY3 | Cautious (no FYA) £ | If the 50% FYA is available £ |
|---|---|---|
| 50% FYA | — | 300,000 |
| Addition to special rate pool | 600,000 | 300,000 |
| WDA 6% on this addition | 36,000 | 18,000 |
| **Allowances on the £600,000 in GY3** | **36,000** | **318,000** |

(Special rate WDAs are not time-apportioned for part-year ownership within a 12-month period.)

**No fixtures requirements here.** The developer held the building as trading stock: it never incurred capital expenditure on the fixtures and was never entitled to allowances on them. The pooling and fixed value requirements (ss 187A–187B, below) apply only where a **past owner was entitled to claim**. Buying new from a developer avoids them; the split is governed only by s 562.

**The gains thread.** Chapter 16 tells how Tarnmoor Estates Ltd (TES) sells its depot on 30 April GY3 for £6.0m and rolls part of the gain into this distribution centre: proceeds not reinvested £0.8m chargeable; £1,477,500 rolled over; **TEL's base cost £3,722,500**. When TEL eventually sells, every pound of SBA claimed is added to its disposal consideration. So the SBA is a deduction now against trading profits at 25%, matched by a larger gain later, which may itself be rolled over, covered by capital losses, or never realised. That timing advantage is the real value of the SBA to a group that holds buildings for decades.

## The allowance statement and the life of a building

Because the allowance travels with the building, each new owner needs facts it cannot easily discover. The law answers with the **allowance statement** (CAA 2001 s 270IA).

- **No statement, no claim.** The person who incurred the expenditure must make one; a later buyer must obtain a copy from a past owner.
- **Contents:** a written statement identifying the building, and stating (a) the date of the **earliest contract for construction**, (b) the **amount of qualifying expenditure**, (c) the date the building was **first brought into non-residential use**, and (d) where further qualifying expenditure is incurred after that date, the date it is incurred (item (d) added by FA 2022 for expenditure from Royal Assent). Where the expenditure includes special tax site qualifying expenditure, the statement must show that amount separately (s 270IA(5)).
- Think of it as the building's **birth certificate**: lose it, and a later owner cannot prove the allowance life that is left.

**Due diligence.** On buying a used building: obtain the allowance statement, warranties that no construction contract predates 29 October 2018, and the seller's records of claims. On buying a company that owns buildings, ask the same questions inside the target. On selling, pass on the statement: a buyer who cannot claim will pay less.

**Life cycle.** On a sale the allowance for the year is apportioned between seller and buyer, and the buyer continues at the same annual amount; the seller adds its claims to its gains consideration. Demolition ends the allowance permanently.

> **Worked example: the seller's gain (hypothetical, not a story fact)**
> A company built a warehouse for £2,000,000 on land bought for £800,000 (all after December 2017, so no indexation). It claims £60,000 a year for 5 years and then sells for £3,000,000.
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Disposal proceeds | 3,000,000 |
> | Add: SBA claimed (5 × £60,000) (TCGA 1992 s 37B) | 300,000 |
> | Deemed consideration | 3,300,000 |
> | Less: cost (land £800,000 + building £2,000,000) | (2,800,000) |
> | **Chargeable gain** | **500,000** |
>
> The allowances are **added to proceeds**, not deducted from cost. The buyer continues at £60,000 a year.

This is why the SBA behaves so differently from plant allowances: a pool claws back excess relief through a balancing charge taxed as income; a building claws it back through the gain, which may be rolled over or covered by capital losses.

> **Going further: the SBA in deferred tax and in groups**
> - **Intra-group transfers.** A building moving between group companies at no gain, no loss (TCGA 1992 s 171) still carries its SBA: the transferee continues at 3% of the original cost and needs the allowance statement. Do not confuse the gains base cost (transferor's cost) with the SBA base (original qualifying expenditure).
> - **Deferred tax (chapter 6).** The SBA creates a temporary difference between the carrying amount of the building and its tax base; the land usually creates none.
> - **Pricing.** A buyer of a used building values the remaining SBA stream (and its clawback for the seller) in the price; a seller who has claimed SBA should model the add-back before agreeing a sale price.

## Fixtures, and who owns what is fixed

A **fixture** is plant or machinery so installed or fixed in or to a building or other land that it becomes, in law, part of that building or land (CAA 2001 Part 2 Ch 14, from s 172). Lifts, heating, air conditioning, wiring and sanitary fittings are classic examples.

Fixtures raise a problem ordinary plant does not. A machine moves with its buyer; a fixture belongs in law to whoever owns the land, which may be held by a freeholder, let, sublet, mortgaged and sold while the same boiler sits in the basement. Without special rules, several people could claim for one boiler, or none could.

- **Ownership rules (ss 176–184)** decide who is treated as owning a fixture: broadly, the person with an interest in the land who incurred the expenditure.
- **Ceasing to own (ss 188–192A)** and the **disposal value** table (s 196): on a sale of the qualifying interest, the seller's disposal value is normally the part of the sale price attributable to the fixtures.
- **Past-owner cap (s 185):** if a past owner claimed and brought a disposal value into account, the new owner's qualifying expenditure is capped at that disposal value (plus s 25 installation costs). It stops a fixture growing in value for tax as it passes from hand to hand.

**Analogy.** Fixtures are like luggage on a journey with connecting flights. Checked through properly at each change, it arrives with every passenger who comes after. If one owner fails to check it through, it is lost for every later owner too. The precise rule is the pooling requirement and the fixed value requirement (next section).

Two practical points for a group:
1. Integral features go to the **special rate pool**; other fixtures to the **main pool**. A landlord company such as TES can claim on fixtures in commercial buildings it lets, because a property business is a qualifying activity (s 15); allowances are given effect against the property business, with any excess added to management expenses (s 253; CTA 2009 s 1233).
2. Fixtures are plant, so they can attract the **AIA** even when second-hand; **full expensing and the 50% FYA cannot** be claimed on second-hand fixtures (s 45S: unused and not second-hand).

## The pooling and fixed value requirements

Before 2012, a seller could claim on fixtures for years, sell the building without a meaningful disposal value, and leave the buyer to claim again on its own valuation of the same fixtures: two claims on one boiler. Parliament closed this in two steps:

| Requirement | Applies from | Met if | Authority |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fixed value** (where the past owner must bring in a disposal value under s 196 items 1, 5 or 9) | 1 April 2012 | Within **2 years** of the transfer, a joint **s 198/s 199 election**, or an application to the **tribunal** to fix the value (or the statements route for intervening non-claimants) | s 187A |
| **Pooling** | **1 April 2014** (CT) | The past owner allocated the expenditure to a pool, or claimed an FYA on it, in a chargeable period beginning on or before the day it ceased to own the fixture | s 187A |
| **Disposal value statement** (other s 196 cases; s 61 item 7) | — | A written statement within 2 years | s 187A |

The law says that where a **past owner was entitled to claim** on the fixture, a buyer's new expenditure is treated as **nil** unless the requirements are met, and the nil carries through to every later owner whose title passes through it. The **burden** of showing the requirements were met is on the current owner (s 187B). In practice: copies of the seller's computations and the signed election.

**Misconception: "a property buyer can always claim allowances on the fixtures it buys."** Wrong. A buyer can claim only if the seller pooled and the value was fixed within 2 years. If the seller never claimed although entitled to, the buyer may claim nothing. The exception is where no past owner was ever entitled to claim (for example a developer holding the building as trading stock): then ss 187A–187B do not bite. A buyer who wants allowances must make them a condition of the deal before exchange; after completion, the seller has no reason to help.

## The section 198 election

The tool for meeting the fixed value requirement is the joint election in **CAA 2001 s 198** (s 199 is its twin on the **grant of a lease**).

- **Joint:** made by seller and buyer.
- **Effect:** fixes the part of the sale price attributable to the fixtures. It is the seller's disposal value and the buyer's qualifying expenditure. The balance of the price is the price of the rest of the property.
- **Cap:** cannot exceed the seller's original qualifying expenditure on the fixtures, or the actual price.
- **Irrevocable;** made within **2 years** of the sale (GOV.UK guidance).

**The negotiation.** The seller wants a **low** figure (smaller pool reduction, no balancing charge); the buyer wants a **high** figure (more qualifying expenditure). Every pound moved is a pound of allowances moved from one side to the other. A common outcome is a figure that leaves the seller with no balancing charge, with the price adjusted to compensate.

> **Worked example: TES's office fixtures (story, GY2)**
> TES buys an office building in GY2. The seller's original qualifying expenditure on the fixtures was **£700,000**. A s 198 election fixes the fixtures at **£400,000** (within the cap: lower than £700,000 and than the price). The fixtures are all integral features.
>
> | TES, GY2 | Special rate pool £ |
> |---|---|
> | Addition: s 198 elected value | 400,000 |
> | WDA 6% (if no AIA is allocated to cover it) | (24,000) |
> | c/f | 376,000 |
>
> If the group allocates enough of its single £1m AIA to TES (chapter 8 allocates the group AIA to Calder and TES in GY2), the whole £400,000 is relieved at once instead.

**The gains trap.** The election does **not** change the gains computation. TES's base cost is the **whole price** paid for the office, including the fixtures element; on a sale, the **whole price** is the proceeds. The examiners reported after M23 (Q4) that candidates deducted fixtures amounts from gains computations, and the N25 (Q5) report stressed that CA-claimed expenditure adjusts the gains computation only where there is a loss. The only interaction is the **loss restriction** for capital allowances (TCGA 1992 s 41): an allowable loss is restricted by the net allowances given.

> **Worked example: the loss restriction (hypothetical figures, TES's office)**
> Suppose TES paid £3,000,000 for the office (no indexation: bought after December 2017) and has had allowances of £400,000 on the fixtures.
>
> | | Sale at £3.6m £ | Sale at £2.5m £ |
> |---|---|---|
> | Proceeds (whole price, including fixtures element) | 3,600,000 | 2,500,000 |
> | Less: cost (whole price) | (3,000,000) | (3,000,000) |
> | Unadjusted gain / (loss) | 600,000 | (500,000) |
> | Restriction: net allowances (£400,000 claimed less £100,000 disposal value fixed by a new s 198 election) | n/a (gain) | 300,000 |
> | **Chargeable gain / (allowable loss)** | **600,000** | **(200,000)** |
>
> The figure for "net allowances" is stated broadly (allowances given less the disposal value brought into the pool).

> **Exam lens: fixtures**
> - **Grade:** "Fixtures" **1 (core)**.
> - **Past appearances:** M23 Q4 (20 marks: property, factory hived down, **s 198 elections**, group roll-over; candidates deducted fixtures amounts from gains computations); N25 Q5 (20 marks: group property, intra-group NGNL transfer, refurbishment with integral features, **s 198 joint election**, SDLT, roll-over; only a minority dealt with CAs and the fixtures election); M26 Q4 (fixtures disposal value in a property question).
> - **Traps:** fixtures values deducted from gains; forgetting the pooling and fixed value requirements on a second-hand building; elected value above the seller's original cost; forgetting that a developer seller means no s 198 election is needed (or possible).
> - **Layout:** the elected figure enters the CA columns (special rate or main pool), never the gains computation (except the loss restriction).

## Long funding leases, where allowances follow the economics

Leasing raises a different question: when one company owns a machine and another uses it, who gets the allowances? Historically the owner, meaning the lessor. Lessors with taxable profits bought plant, claimed the allowances and passed part of the benefit to lessees in lower rentals. The largest deals ended in court.

The best known is ***Barclays Mercantile Business Finance Ltd v Mawson*** [2004] UKHL 51. BMBF paid about £91m for part of a gas pipeline under the Irish Sea and leased it back to Bord Gáis Éireann. The Revenue's case was that the payment produced no real commercial benefit and existed mainly to generate allowances; the House of Lords held that the lessor had incurred the expenditure and was entitled to the allowances. (Facts from secondary reports; the judgment was not opened for this chapter.)

In **FA 2006 (Sch 8, inserting CAA 2001 Part 2 Ch 6A)** Parliament changed the question for **long funding leases**: the allowances move from lessor to **lessee**, on the idea that a lease that really finances the purchase of an asset should be taxed like a loan. The lessor is taxed on its finance return and claims nothing (CTA 2010 Part 9).

| Test | Rule | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Long funding lease | A **funding lease** that is not a **short lease**, not an excluded lease of background plant for a building (s 70R), and not excluded by s 70U (plant leased with land) | CAA 2001 s 70G |
| Funding lease | Meets at least one test at inception: (a) **finance lease test**: under GAAP it is (or would be) a finance lease or loan (s 70N); (b) **lease payments test**: PV of minimum lease payments (at the implicit rate) ≥ **80%** of fair value (s 70O); (c) **useful economic life test**: term > **65%** of remaining useful economic life (s 70P) | s 70J |
| Short lease | Term of **7 years or less**, with anti-avoidance for linked leases and leasebacks | s 70I |
| Lessee's return | For the lessee it is an LFL only if its **tax return for the initial period treats it as one**; not correctable by an error or mistake claim | s 70H |
| Right-of-use lessees | An RoU lease that is an LFL meeting the s 70O or s 70P test is a long funding **finance** lease for the lessee | s 70YI |

**The lessee's position.** The lessee is treated as owning the plant and incurring capital expenditure at the start of the term (s 70A): for a long funding operating lease the amount is set by s 70B, for a long funding finance lease by s 70C (broadly, the present value of the minimum lease payments recognised in the accounts: HMRC BLM42010). In return, the lessee's deduction for rentals under a long funding **finance** lease is capped at the **finance charge** under GAAP (CTA 2010 s 377; s 377A for RoU remeasurements); under a long funding **operating** lease, deductions are reduced by the time-apportioned expected fall in value (s 379).

## Successions, when a business changes hands

When one person succeeds to another's qualifying activity, the plant passes with it (CAA 2001 ss 265–268).

- **General rule (s 265):** the plant is treated as sold at **market value** at the date of succession. The predecessor brings market value into its pool as a disposal value (balancing adjustments in its final period). The successor's expenditure is market value, with **no AIA and no FYA**. Without that bar, a business could be passed around to refresh allowances on old plant.
- **Was there a succession?** A question of fact. Tribunals still use Widgery J's test in ***Kenmir Ltd v Frizzell*** [1968] 1 All ER 414 at 418: "the vital consideration is whether the effect of the transaction was to put the transferee in possession of a going concern…", quoted by the FTT in ***Haymarket Media Group Ltd v HMRC*** [2022] UKFTT 168 (TC) (Judge Heidi Poon and Julian Sims; a VAT transfer of a going concern case on the sale of the Teddington Studios site, appeal dismissed). *Kenmir* was an employment case: use it as the going concern test, not as capital allowances authority. Buying assets is not succeeding to a business.
- **Connected-party election (ss 266–267):** where predecessor and successor are **connected** (s 575 plus the extended tests in s 266(5)) and both are within the charge to UK tax on the activity, they may **jointly elect within 2 years** of the succession for the plant to pass at **tax written down value**: no balancing adjustment for the predecessor; the successor steps into its shoes. Not available to a **dual resident investing company**, or where **s 561** applies.

> **Worked example: market value or the election (hypothetical)**
>
> | Connected succession; plant TWDV £2,000,000; market value £3,000,000 | No election £ | s 266 election £ |
> |---|---|---|
> | Predecessor: disposal value | 3,000,000 | 2,000,000 |
> | Predecessor: balancing charge | 1,000,000 | nil |
> | Successor: qualifying expenditure | 3,000,000 (no AIA/FYA) | 2,000,000 (continues pools) |

**Inside a group: Part 22.** Where a trade passes between companies under common ownership, **CTA 2010 Part 22 Ch 1** can apply automatically; **s 948** then continues allowances as if the successor had always carried on the trade, and the transfer gives no allowances or charges. Chapter 19 owns Part 22. In our invented case, when TEL hives its actuators trade down into Tarnmoor Actuators Ltd (TAL) on **1 February GY6**, the plant passes at TWDV under s 948: no balancing charge, no election needed. (Where the cross-border rule in CAA 2001 s 561 applies, s 948 is disapplied.)

> **Exam lens: successions**
> - **Grade:** "Successions" **1 (core)**.
> - **Past appearances:** no standalone CA succession question M23–M26; M23 Q4 included a factory hived down with the trade (gains and Part 22 context). Expect it embedded in a reorganisation or acquisition question.
> - **Traps:** giving AIA or FYA to a successor; forgetting the 2-year joint election; using s 266 where Part 22 Ch 1 applies automatically; ignoring that a mere asset purchase is not a succession.

## Connected parties, cross-border transfers and the Act's toolbox

**Connected sales of plant (ss 214, 217, 218).** A group company selling plant to a fellow group company is a connected-party transaction (s 575). The buyer gets **no AIA and no FYA** (s 217), and its qualifying expenditure is **capped at the seller's disposal value** (or, if none, the lower of market value and the original cost to the seller or a connected person) (s 218). The aim: no fresh allowances from selling plant to yourself at a high price.

**Non-P&M Parts (ss 567–570).** Where buyer and seller are under common control, or the sole or main benefit of the sale is a tax advantage, the sale is treated as at **market value** (ss 567–568). Where control is the only reason and there is no tax advantage, the parties may elect for the **alternative amount** (broadly the lower of market value and the unrelieved balance) (s 569); barred where the buyer is a **dual resident investing company** (s 570). These sections do not apply to Part 2 (P&M) or where s 561 applies; within the syllabus they matter mainly for R&D allowances (chapter 10). **s 570A** denies a balancing allowance where a tax avoidance scheme has reduced the proceeds.

**Cross-border transfers (ss 561, 561A).** s 561 applies to a transfer within TCGA 1992 s 140A between qualifying companies ("relevant state" = the UK or a member State, s 561(4A)), where the transferee is UK resident or carries on the business through a UK PE: **no allowances or charges**, and the transferee **stands in the transferor's shoes** (CTA 2010 s 948 then disapplied). s 561A covers cross-border mergers (TCGA s 140E). These rules were built for EU law; **how far TCGA s 140A's conditions still operate after Brexit is unclear** (bible flag 25). In an exam, recognise them, state the effect, and note that the conditions turn on the definition of a relevant state.

**Apportionment (s 562)** and **final provisions (ss 571–581)**: the Act's toolbox of definitions, including transfers treated as sales (s 573) and connected persons (s 575). They answer three questions every transaction raises: was there a sale, at what price, and between whom?

> **Exam lens: transfers, miscellaneous and final provisions**
> - **Grades:** "Transfers – s.561, 561A CAA 2001" **1**; "Miscellaneous – s.562–570A" **1**; "Final provisions – s.571–581" **1** (all core in the 2026 grid).
> - **Past appearances:** not examined as standalone questions M23–M26; connected-party sales and apportionment appear inside property and reorganisation questions.
> - **Traps:** AIA/FYA on a connected purchase; a price above the seller's disposal value; forgetting the just and reasonable apportionment when the contract allocates artificially.

## Contributions and grants

Capital allowances relieve the cost a business bears.

| Rule | Detail | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| General rule | Expenditure is treated as **not incurred** by a person to the extent it is met, directly or indirectly, by a **public body** or **another person**. Applies across the Act, including the SBA | CAA 2001 s 532 |
| Insurance and compensation | Outside the general rule | s 535 |
| Non-relievable contributor | Where the contributor is not a public body and cannot get allowances or a deduction for its contribution, the **recipient is treated as incurring the expenditure** | s 536 |
| Contribution allowances | A contributor paying towards another's capital expenditure for its **own** trade or other qualifying activity can claim allowances as if it owned the asset, **if not connected** with the recipient | ss 537–538 |
| SBA version | Contribution allowances adapted for Part 2A | s 538A |

**The group consequence.** Group companies are connected, so a group company that pays towards a fellow member's building gets **no contribution allowance**; because it cannot claim, s 536 leaves the allowances with the recipient. For a group, that is usually the right place for them.

> **Worked example: TES's flood wall (story, GY3)**
> In GY3 TES builds a concrete flood wall protecting a riverside site it lets to TEL. Cost **£500,000**; in qualifying use from **1 January GY3**. The local authority (a public body) pays a grant of **£200,000**.
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Cost of the wall | 500,000 |
> | Less: local authority grant (s 532) | (200,000) |
> | **SBA qualifying expenditure** | **300,000** |
> | **SBA a year (3%), full year in GY3** | **9,000** |
>
> Chapter 28 returns to the grant in the corporate interest restriction.

> **Exam lens: contributions**
> - **Grade:** "Contributions" **1 (core)**. Not seen as a standalone question M23–M26; expect a line in a CA computation (a grant towards plant or a building).
> - **Traps:** claiming on the gross cost when a public grant has been received; giving a contribution allowance to a connected contributor.

## Anti-avoidance and the special tax sites

**Allowance buying (awareness).** A company whose plant has a tax written down value above its balance sheet value holds latent allowances that a buyer with profits might exploit. **FA 2010 (s 26, Sch 4)** inserted **CAA 2001 Part 2 Ch 16A (ss 212A–212S)**, effective from **21 July 2009**. It applies where there is a **qualifying change** in ownership (s 212C) of a company with a **relevant excess of allowances** (TWDV above balance sheet value, ss 212J–212L) and a **limiting condition** is met (s 212LA, FA 2013): excess **£50m or more**; excess **£2m or more** and not insignificant relative to the benefits of the change; or a smaller excess where the change has an **unallowable purpose** (s 212M); or arrangements to keep below the thresholds. FA 2013 also renamed the Chapter "restrictions on allowance buying" and extended it to all qualifying activities. When the rules bite, the excess is restricted so that the change of ownership cannot be used to put it to work for the new owners (detailed mechanics, ss 212N–212S, not opened for this chapter). CTA 2010 Part 9 separately deals with sales of lessor companies. **Pattern to recognise:** tax value above book value plus a change of ownership.

**Freeports and investment zones: special tax sites (awareness).**
- **P&M:** 100% FYA for companies on unused plant for use **primarily** in a special tax site, in a trade, incurred by the site's sunset date (CAA 2001 ss 45O–45R); the plant must remain primarily in use in the site for **5 years** (s 45R).
- **SBA:** an enhanced **10% a year over 10 years** for special tax site qualifying expenditure (s 270AA(2A), (5)).
- **Sunset dates:** GOV.UK guidance gives the enhanced SBA for expenditure up to **30 September 2031** for English freeport tax sites and **30 September 2034** for Scottish Green Freeports, Welsh Freeports and Investment Zones (the building must also be brought into qualifying use by then). The original date was 30 September 2026. **The P&M FYA end date per site was not confirmed from a primary source:** check before use.
- Tarnmoor has no freeport site. In an exam, a scenario that mentions a freeport invites a line on these reliefs.

> **Exam lens: avoidance and freeports**
> - **Grades:** "Avoidance involving allowance buying and other anti-avoidance" **3 (awareness)**; "Freeports" **3 (awareness)**.
> - **Past appearances:** none M23–M26.
> - **Style:** recognition and a brief statement of the rule; no computation expected.

## What to take away

- A building is a bundle of tax assets: land (nothing), structure (SBA), integral features and other fixtures (P&M). A just and reasonable apportionment (s 562) splits the price, whatever the contract says.
- **SBA:** 3% a year of qualifying construction cost for 33⅓ years from the later of first non-residential use and the expenditure; construction contract on or after 29 October 2018; older buildings: only later alterations and extensions. Land, land alteration and plant excluded. Bought unused from a developer: price paid; unused from anyone else: lower of price and construction cost; used: 3% of the original cost continues. No balancing adjustment on sale; the seller adds its SBA to its gains consideration. Demolition ends it. No claim without an allowance statement.
- **TEL's distribution centre:** structure £3.4m, SBA £102,000 a year, **£34,000** for 4 months of GY3; land £1.2m nothing; integral features £0.6m to the special rate pool.
- **Fixtures:** a buyer claims only if the past owner pooled (from April 2014) and the value was fixed (from April 2012), usually by a joint **s 198 election** within 2 years, capped at the seller's original cost. Miss either and the claim is nil for every later owner. The election affects allowances, not gains (except the loss restriction). TES's office fixtures: **£400,000**.
- **LFLs:** a funding lease (finance lease, 80% payments or 65% useful life test) of more than 7 years; the lessee claims on the PV of the payments and deducts only the finance charge. TEL's machine: **£1.5m** to the main pool.
- **Successions:** market value, no AIA/FYA (s 265), unless connected parties elect within 2 years for TWDV (ss 266–267), or Part 22 Ch 1 passes it at TWDV automatically (s 948). Connected plant sales: no AIA/FYA; cost capped at the seller's disposal value.
- **Contributions:** public grants reduce qualifying expenditure; connected contributors get no contribution allowance. TES's flood wall qualifies on **£300,000** (SBA £9,000 a year).
- **Awareness:** allowance buying (Ch 16A) and freeport special tax sites (100% FYA; 10% SBA).

Chapter twenty six: Controlled foreign companies.

A rule on trial.

On the nineteenth of September, twenty twenty four, the Court of Justice of the European Union gave judgment in three joined appeals. The appellants were the United Kingdom, the broadcaster I T V and companies in the London Stock Exchange group. The defendant was the European Commission. The subject was a single exemption in the U K's rules on controlled foreign companies, the exemption for group finance companies.

Five years earlier, on the second of April, twenty nineteen, the Commission had decided that part of that exemption was unlawful State aid. H M R C had been made to collect the money from the groups that had used it. The General Court in Luxembourg had upheld the Commission in June twenty twenty two. Now the Court of Justice set that judgment aside and annulled the Commission's decision. The European dates come from the court's press release and professional reports of it. The British steps that followed are in our own statute book, and we will meet them later in the chapter.

The legal error was a choice of baseline. To call a tax rule State aid, you must show that it departs from the normal rules. The Commission had treated the controlled foreign company rules themselves as the normal rules, and the finance company exemption as a departure from them. The Court of Justice held that the right baseline was the U K's corporation tax system as a whole. Once you look at the whole system, a rule that chooses not to tax some foreign profits is not a gift. It is a decision about where the U K's taxing reach should stop.

That is the question underneath this whole chapter. A U K company owns a subsidiary abroad. The subsidiary is a separate person, resident in another country, taxed there. Its profits are not the parent's profits. So when, if ever, should the United Kingdom tax them? And if it does, on whom, and how much?

This is chapter twenty six, in Part Four, on profits that cross borders. Controlled foreign companies are a core topic on the Advanced Technical paper, and the examiners have set two full twenty mark questions on them in recent sittings. The Living Law, chapter twenty six, met the choice between a branch and a subsidiary abroad, and the exemption for foreign dividends, and only pointed towards these rules. Chapter twenty five of this book took the branch side of that choice. Here we take the subsidiary side to Advanced Technical depth. Throughout, we apply the law for the financial year twenty twenty six, under the Finance Act twenty twenty six.

The rules live in Part nine A of the Taxation, International and Other Provisions, Act twenty ten, which I will call the International Act. A controlled foreign company I will call a C F C, after this first full use. The Corporation Tax Act twenty ten I will call the twenty ten Act.

Why the rules exist.

Start with a choice that every growing group faces. A U K company that sells abroad can do it through a branch or through a subsidiary. Chapter twenty five showed that a branch is part of the U K company, so its profits are U K profits unless the company elects for the branch exemption. A subsidiary is different. It is its own taxpayer. If it is resident abroad, the U K taxes nothing it earns abroad. The U K taxes the parent only on what comes back, and since July two thousand and nine most dividends that come back are exempt.

That is a sensible design for genuine foreign business. A factory in Vallaria, the invented country where our story's main foreign subsidiary lives, should pay Vallarian tax on Vallarian profits. But the same design opens a door. Suppose a U K group moves money, or a valuable patent, or a profitable contract, into a subsidiary in a country with a very low tax rate. The subsidiary has few people and no real reason to exist except the rate. The profits that used to arise in the U K now arise there, and the U K never sees them. That is not a foreign business. It is U K profit wearing a foreign coat.

A controlled foreign company regime is the response. It looks through the coat. Suppose a non-U K company is controlled from the U K, and some of its profits look as though they really belong in the U K. The regime taxes those profits on the U K companies that own it, as if they had earned them.

The U K first introduced such rules in nineteen eighty four. The old regime cast a wide net. Broadly, it caught a controlled company taxed at a low level abroad, and then let it go if it passed one of a set of exclusions. H M R C's manual lists them. There was an acceptable distribution policy, which excused a company that paid most of its profits home as dividends. There was an exempt activities test for genuine trading companies, a de minimis limit, a list of excluded countries, and a motive test. The acceptable distribution policy was abolished from the first of July, two thousand and nine, when the dividend exemption arrived, because paying a dividend home no longer produced U K tax.

Then came Europe. In Cadbury Schweppes versus the Commissioners of Inland Revenue, decided by the Court of Justice on the twelfth of September, two thousand and six, the U K rules were tested against freedom of establishment. The court held that rules like these restrict that freedom, and can be justified only where they target wholly artificial arrangements intended to escape the national tax normally payable. A subsidiary actually established in another member state and carrying on genuine economic activities there could not be taxed this way, even if tax had been one of the reasons for putting it there. The court pointed to objective, checkable factors, such as premises, staff and equipment.

The U K's rules did not say that. In Vodafone Two versus H M R C, the High Court in two thousand and eight held that they could not be read to fit, so they had to be set aside for subsidiaries in the European Union. The Court of Appeal disagreed on the twenty second of May, two thousand and nine. The Chancellor of the High Court sat with Lord Justice Longmore and Lord Justice Goldring. They accepted H M R C's argument that the legislation could be read as containing an extra exception for a company actually established in another member state and carrying on genuine economic activities there. The rules survived, but only with a patch the courts had sewn on.

A patched regime that caught too much and then excused too much was ready for replacement.

The twenty twelve answer.

The Finance Act twenty twelve inserted Part nine A into the International Act. The new regime applies to accounting periods of C F Cs beginning on or after the first of January, twenty thirteen. Its starting idea is the reverse of the old one. Instead of catching everything and excusing some, it aims to tax only profits that have been artificially diverted from the U K, and it defines those profits by rule.

The defining device is the gateway. Picture a sieve held over the U K tax base. A foreign subsidiary's profits are poured in. Most fall away, because they belong where they were earned. Only the profits that belong in the U K drop through. And beside the sieve there are doors. A company that passes through one of the doors walks past the sieve entirely, and nothing it earns is examined.

The precise rule is in the charging provisions. A C F C charge arises for a C F C's accounting period only if two things are true. The C F C has chargeable profits for the period, and none of the entity exemptions applies. Chargeable profits are the C F C's assumed taxable total profits, but only so far as they pass through the gateway. Assumed taxable total profits means the profits computed as if the C F C were a U K resident company, under a set of assumptions the Act spells out. The doors are the five entity exemptions. The sieve is the gateway.

If there is a charge, the Act then runs five steps. First, identify the persons with relevant interests in the C F C, and stop if none is a U K resident company. Second, work out the C F C's creditable tax, broadly its foreign tax on the profits being charged. Third, apportion the chargeable profits and the creditable tax among the relevant interests. Fourth, identify the chargeable companies, the U K resident companies whose share, with their connected and associated persons, is at least twenty five per cent. Fifth, charge each one.

The charge is tax at the main rate on that company's percentage of the chargeable profits, less the same percentage of the creditable tax. It is treated as corporation tax of the chargeable company for its accounting period in which the C F C's accounting period ends. For the financial year twenty twenty six the rate is twenty five per cent.

For an exam answer, and for a real annual review, that gives an order of attack. Is the company a C F C at all? If so, does an entity exemption apply? If one does, stop. If none does, which gateway chapters apply, and how much passes through? Then apportion, test the twenty five per cent threshold, deduct creditable tax, and compute the charge. The examiners have rewarded that order and punished candidates who wandered from it.

One more structural point before the detail. The regime borrows its central test from the international approach to permanent establishments. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development's twenty ten report on attributing profits to permanent establishments asks where the significant people functions are, the people who actually take the decisions about assets and risks. The U K's gateway asks the same question about a C F C. If the people who matter are in the U K, the profits those people generate belong to the U K. That is the regime's theory of artificial diversion in one sentence.

Who is a controlled foreign company.

A C F C is a company that is not resident in the U K and is controlled by a U K resident person or persons. Each word does work. It must be a company. It must be non-resident. And control must sit, wholly or jointly, with persons resident in the U K. They need not be companies, although only companies can be charged.

Control has several meanings, and any one is enough. Legal control is the power, through shares, votes or the articles, to secure that the company's affairs are conducted as you wish. Economic control looks at money. A person controls a company if they would receive more than half of the proceeds if all its shares were sold. The same is true of more than half of its distributed income, or of its assets in a winding up. Lending banks' rights are ignored. Two or more persons who together satisfy either test control the company jointly.

Joint control needs a special rule when one controller is in the U K and the other is not. Picture a fifty fifty joint venture between a U K group and a French group. Without more, it is not obvious that a company controlled jointly by a U K person and a foreign person is controlled from the U K. The forty per cent rule answers that. The company is a C F C if the U K person holds at least forty per cent and the non-U K person holds at least forty per cent but no more than fifty five per cent. So a true joint venture between equals is caught, but a company run by a foreign partner holding sixty per cent is not.

Rights are attributed. A person is treated as having rights they are entitled to acquire in future, rights held by nominees, and, for U K persons, the rights of connected U K persons. Holding through a cousin company does not help.

Then there is accounting control. A U K company controls a foreign company if it is the foreign company's parent under accounting standards. But this applies only where the fifty per cent condition is met. At least half of the C F C's chargeable profits would have to be apportioned to that company and its U K subsidiaries. This catches structures where control lies in contracts or rights rather than shares.

Last, a rule with European fingerprints. The Anti-Tax Avoidance Directive of the European Union required member states to have C F C rules of a certain minimum reach. The Finance Act twenty nineteen added a test for C F C accounting periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty nineteen. A non-U K company is treated as controlled if a U K resident company, alone or with associated enterprises, directly or indirectly has more than a fifty per cent investment in it. Those rules stayed on the statute book after the U K left the European Union.

Two practical points follow. A C F C's residence is the territory where it is liable to tax by reason of domicile, residence or place of management, with tie-breakers where there is more than one. And a C F C has its own accounting periods. One begins when the company becomes a C F C and ends on events such as ceasing to be a C F C. Otherwise the ordinary corporation tax rules for accounting periods apply. The charge then lands in the U K company's accounting period in which the C F C's period ends.

Doors past the sieve.

There are five entity exemptions, in Chapters ten to fourteen of Part nine A. If any one applies for an accounting period, there is no charge for that period. No gateway analysis, no apportionment, nothing. Test them in the statutory order, and stop at the first that works.

The first door is the exempt period. When a company first comes under U K control, typically because a U K group buys it, the group gets twelve months of grace to restructure. H M R C's manual describes the starting conditions. The company exists, is resident outside the U K, is controlled from the U K and has at least one chargeable company, and in the preceding twelve months it had none and the charging condition was not met. An accounting period that ends within the exempt period is exempt, subject to two further conditions. The chargeable company condition limits the benefit to the original owners and those connected with them. The subsequent period condition is the sting. The company must not stop being a C F C before an accounting period beginning after the exempt period, and the C F C charge must not apply to that next period. In other words, the grace is for groups that use the year to put things right. If the next period is chargeable, the grace is withdrawn. Because that is only known later, the Act gives an extended window to amend the return, twelve months after the filing date for the period in which the first post-exempt accounting period ends. H M R C can extend the exempt period on notice given before it ends.

For this example, suppose our invented group, Tarnmoor, which we meet properly later in the chapter, bought a distributor in the invented country of Marrovia on the first of March, with a thirty first of December year end. Its exempt period runs from the first of March to the end of the following February. Its first accounting period ends on the thirty first of December, inside the exempt period, so it is exempt, provided the subsequent period condition is met. In practice that means the group must have put things right by the time the grace runs out. For the next accounting period, which straddles the end of the exempt period, only the profits arising after the exempt period are in the frame. That is a labelled example, not part of our story.

The second door is the excluded territories exemption. A C F C resident in a territory listed in regulations made in twenty twelve is exempt, provided its low-taxed or specially favoured income stays under a threshold. The threshold is the greater of ten per cent of its accounting profits and fifty thousand pounds. There is an intellectual property condition and an anti-avoidance rule. For a short list of major economies, Australia, Canada, France, Germany, Japan and the United States, H M R C's manual says the income and intellectual property conditions need not be met, though the anti-avoidance rule still applies. That manual page dates from twenty sixteen, so check the regulations themselves.

The third door is the low profits exemption, for small companies. A C F C is exempt if its accounting profits, or its assumed taxable total profits, are no more than fifty thousand pounds. It is also exempt if those profits are no more than five hundred thousand pounds and its non-trading income is no more than fifty thousand pounds. All the figures are reduced for an accounting period shorter than twelve months. So a nine month period has limits of three hundred and seventy five thousand pounds and thirty seven thousand five hundred pounds. Arrangements designed to fit inside the limits are ignored, and so are businesses that are really U K services channelled through an intermediary.

The low profit margin and the tax exemption.

The fourth door is the low profit margin exemption. It is meant for companies that do routine, low-margin work abroad, such as a distributor or a service centre. The test is simple to state. A C F C is exempt if its accounting profits are no more than ten per cent of its relevant operating expenditure. But two details decide almost every answer.

First, the profits are measured before interest. Second, relevant operating expenditure is not the whole cost base. It excludes the cost of goods bought by the C F C that are not used in its own territory, and it excludes expenditure that gives rise to income of a related person. A management fee paid to the U K parent is the classic example of the second, because the fee is the parent's income.

The examiners reported, after the May twenty twenty five sitting, that few candidates calculated the low profit margin correctly. So take a labelled example. A distributor C F C has sales of six million pounds. It buys four point two million pounds of goods, and one point five million pounds of them are shipped straight from the factory to customers outside its territory. It also pays staff of seven hundred thousand pounds and a management fee to its U K parent of three hundred thousand pounds. Rent is two hundred thousand pounds, and other costs one hundred and fifty thousand pounds. It also pays ninety thousand pounds of interest. Its profit after interest is three hundred and sixty thousand pounds. Before interest it is four hundred and fifty thousand pounds. Its operating expenditure is five point five five million pounds. Take out the one point five million pounds of goods never used in its territory, and the three hundred thousand pounds of fees that are the parent's income. Relevant operating expenditure is three point seven five million pounds. Ten per cent of that is three hundred and seventy five thousand pounds. Four hundred and fifty thousand pounds is more, so the exemption fails. The careless answer compares three hundred and sixty thousand pounds with ten per cent of the whole cost base, five hundred and fifty five thousand pounds, and wrongly finds the company exempt. The full working is in the reading edition.

The fifth door is the tax exemption, and it is the one large groups use most. A C F C is exempt if the tax it pays locally is at least seventy five per cent of the corresponding U K tax. The corresponding U K tax is corporation tax on its assumed taxable total profits, computed as if it were U K resident. With a U K rate of twenty five per cent, the break-even local rate on an identical base is three quarters of twenty five, which is eighteen point seven five per cent.

The words local tax amount are carefully built, and both sides of the comparison must be adjusted. Local tax is reduced where the local base includes income that is not in the U K computation, and where the U K computation deducts expenses that the local system does not allow. Local tax is also reduced by any repayment or credit given to anyone. A designer rate, a rate the company can effectively choose, does not count at all. A company on a tax holiday has a local tax amount of nil, so it never passes.

A labelled example shows why the adjustments matter. A C F C's assumed taxable total profits are one point two million pounds, so the corresponding U K tax is three hundred thousand pounds and the hurdle is two hundred and twenty five thousand pounds. It pays local tax at eighteen per cent on local profits of one point three million pounds, which is two hundred and thirty four thousand pounds. That looks like seventy eight per cent. But one hundred thousand pounds of the local profits is income that is not in the U K computation, so local tax is reduced by eighteen per cent of that, to two hundred and sixteen thousand pounds. That is seventy two per cent. The company fails.

Notice what the five doors have in common. Each one is a judgement, made in advance by Parliament, that a whole company is unlikely to be a vehicle for diverted U K profit. A new acquisition, a company in a respectable tax system, a small company, a low-margin company, a company that pays proper tax at home. If the company fits one of those descriptions, the law does not look further.

Which gateway chapters apply.

When no door opens, the sieve does its work. The gateway has two stages. Chapter three of Part nine A decides which of the gateway chapters, four to eight, apply to the C F C. Then each applicable chapter decides how much profit passes through. If none applies, nothing passes, and there are no chargeable profits.

Chapter four is the general test, for profits attributable to U K activities. It applies unless the C F C meets any one of four conditions. Condition A is met if the C F C holds no assets and bears no risks under arrangements whose main purpose is to reduce U K tax and which are expected to make it more profitable. Condition B is met if it holds no assets and bears no risks that are managed from the U K. Condition C is met if the C F C itself has the capability to keep its business commercially effective if U K management stopped. Condition D is met if its assumed total profits consist only of non-trading finance profits or property business profits, which are dealt with elsewhere. So a genuine foreign trading company run by local management usually escapes Chapter four at the first look, through Condition A or Condition B.

Where Chapter four does apply, it runs eight steps, slowly. Identify the C F C's assets and risks, leaving out the negligible ones. Identify the significant people functions relevant to them, treating the whole C F C group as if it were one company. Sort those functions into U K and non-U K. If there are no U K functions, stop. Otherwise imagine a U K permanent establishment carrying out the U K functions and attribute to it the assets and risks those functions manage. Leave out any asset or risk where the U K contribution is the minority, measured as half or less. Recompute the C F C's profits without what was attributed to the imaginary permanent establishment. The difference is what passes, subject to three exclusions.

The exclusions are where good planning lives. Profits are excluded where the arrangements create substantial non-tax value. They are excluded where independent companies would have made the same arrangements on the same terms. And they are excluded under the trading profits safe harbour, which has five conditions, all of which must be met. The C F C must have business premises in its territory, occupied with a reasonable degree of permanence. No more than twenty per cent of its trading income may come from U K residents or U K permanent establishments, or ten per cent for banks. No more than twenty per cent of its management expenditure may relate to the U K, with an alternative test asset by asset. It must not have received significant intellectual property from a related U K person in the period or in the previous six years. And no more than twenty per cent of its trading income may come from exporting goods out of the U K, other than to its own territory. An anti-avoidance rule stops groups reorganising to fit the tests.

Notice the shape. The safe harbour turns on facts a group can see and manage, such as where the premises are, who the customers are, and where the managers sit. That is deliberate. The regime wanted a test that a well-run foreign trading company could pass without commissioning a study of people functions every year.

Finance profits.

Money is the easiest thing to move, so the gateway gives it its own chapters. Chapter five deals with non-trading finance profits, such as interest on intra-group loans and on deposits.

It applies when the C F C has such profits, with an important carve-out. Finance profits from funds held for the C F C's own trade or property business are left out, unless the funds are held for something else, such as future dividends, acquisitions or contingencies. And there is a five per cent incidental rule. If the C F C trades or lets property, Chapter five does not apply at all where its non-trading finance profits are no more than five per cent of its trading and property profits, measured before interest and tax. So a trading company with three million pounds of trading profits and one hundred and forty thousand pounds of interest income is safe, because five per cent of three million is one hundred and fifty thousand. A similar rule protects holding companies whose income is mainly exempt dividends.

Where Chapter five applies, profits pass through four routes. The first is profit from U K activities, the significant people functions test again. The second is the one that matters most in practice, capital investment from the U K. Finance profits pass through so far as they are earned on funds derived from capital contributions by U K connected companies, or from profits already charged as C F C profits. Other funds received from U K connected companies count too, unless they were received for goods or services or by way of loan. Put plainly, if a U K parent puts equity into a foreign finance company and the finance company lends it on, the interest it earns is caught. The third route catches arrangements made in place of paying a dividend to a U K company, where tax was a main reason. The fourth catches finance leases to U K connected companies used instead of a purchase.

Chapter six deals with trading finance profits, from a genuine lending trade. They pass only so far as they arise from excess free capital, more equity than an independent company would hold, derived from U K contributions. A group treasury company may elect, within twenty months after the end of the accounting period, to treat its trading finance profits as non-trading, so that it can use the finance company exemptions. Chapter seven deals with captive insurance of U K risks. Chapter eight deals with special capital structures in banks. Both are worth recognising and rarely examined.

The finance company exemptions.

Chapter nine of Part nine A is where the State aid saga began, and it is where Tarnmoor's own C F C lives. It gives a claimable exemption for profits from qualifying loan relationships.

Three conditions open it. Chapter five must apply to the C F C. Its non-trading finance profits must include profits from qualifying loan relationships. And the C F C must meet the business premises condition borrowed from the safe harbour, real premises in its territory used with a reasonable degree of permanence. Then a chargeable company may claim, in its own return.

A qualifying loan relationship is a creditor relationship whose ultimate debtor is a qualifying company, meaning a company connected with the C F C and controlled by the same U K persons. Some debtors are ruled out. The loan does not qualify if the debtor's interest reduces the profits of a U K permanent establishment or a U K property business. Nor does it qualify if the debtor is a U K company without a branch exemption election, or if the interest reduces another C F C's chargeable profits. The policy is plain. The exemption is for financing foreign operations, not for stripping interest out of the U K.

The Finance Act twenty nineteen narrowed this, for C F C accounting periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty nineteen. Only profits that pass through the capital investment route, and not through the U K activities route, can qualify. That was the U K's own response to the Commission's concern, because profits generated by people in the U K are U K profits however they are funded.

There are three exemptions. The full exemption applies to the extent that a loan is funded wholly out of qualifying resources, for example profits from lending to group companies in the debtor's own territory, or local business profits. The seventy five per cent exemption applies to any qualifying loan relationship for which the full exemption is not claimed. Three quarters of the profits are exempt, so one quarter passes. At the main rate, twenty five per cent of twenty five per cent is an effective rate of six point two five per cent before any credit for foreign tax.

The third is the matched interest exemption. It asks whether the group as a whole is really funding the U K with debt. The remaining profits are measured against the worldwide group's aggregate net tax-interest expense, the figure chapter twenty eight computes for the corporate interest restriction. If that figure is nil, the remaining profits are fully exempt. If the U K companies' share of the remaining profits exceeds it, the excess is exempt. H M R C's manual gives an example in which twenty five of remaining profits face nine of net interest expense, so sixteen is exempt. A group whose U K companies pay substantial net interest gets nothing from this exemption.

From chargeable profits to the charge.

Chargeable profits are not yet tax. The charge must be placed on particular U K companies, and here two thresholds must be kept apart. Control decides whether the foreign company is a C F C. The twenty five per cent test decides which U K companies pay. The examiners reported, after the May twenty twenty four sitting, that candidates confused the two.

Start with relevant interests. A U K resident company with an interest in a C F C has a relevant interest, except where it holds that interest indirectly through another U K resident company. So the charge sits at the lowest U K company in the chain. If a U K parent owns a U K holding company which owns the C F C, the holding company is charged, not the parent.

Next, apportionment. Where everyone holds only ordinary shares of one class, the chargeable profits and the creditable tax are apportioned by the percentage of ordinary shares each relevant person holds. Indirect holdings are multiplied through the chain. A U K company that owns eighty per cent of a foreign holding company, which owns seventy per cent of the C F C, has fifty six per cent. Holdings that change in the period are time weighted. Sixty per cent for one hundred and forty six days and then one hundred per cent for two hundred and nineteen days gives eighty four per cent for the year. Where the simple rule cannot work, apportionment is on a just and reasonable basis, and H M R C can substitute its own.

Then the twenty five per cent test. A U K company is a chargeable company only if the percentage apportioned to it, plus the percentages apportioned to persons connected or associated with it, is at least twenty five per cent. Take a labelled example. A finance company in a territory with a five per cent tax rate earns two million pounds of interest from lending to a fellow subsidiary. It is owned eighty per cent by a U K parent, fifteen per cent by an unconnected U K company and five per cent by a foreign investor. The parent claims the seventy five per cent exemption, so chargeable profits are five hundred thousand pounds and creditable tax is five per cent of that, twenty five thousand pounds. The parent is chargeable. Its charge is twenty five per cent of eighty per cent of five hundred thousand pounds, which is one hundred thousand pounds, less eighty per cent of twenty five thousand pounds, which is twenty thousand pounds. It pays eighty thousand pounds. The unconnected U K company holds fifteen per cent and has no connected holders, so it pays nothing. The foreign investor is outside U K tax. Without the claim, the parent would have paid three hundred and twenty thousand pounds. Had the fifteen per cent holder been connected with the parent, the parent's share would count towards its test, and it too would be chargeable on its fifteen per cent.

Creditable tax is the bridge to fairness. It starts with the double tax relief the C F C would get, on the U K assumptions, for foreign tax on the income in its chargeable profits. To that are added any U K income tax deducted from that income, U K tax actually charged on it, and other countries' own C F C charges on it. Amounts repaid are excluded. It is apportioned in the same percentages as the profits.

Three consequences complete the picture. The charge is not reduced by the chargeable company's own losses, management expenses or group relief. A provision that once allowed that, section three seven one U D, was repealed by the Finance Number Two Act twenty fifteen for C F C accounting periods beginning on or after the eighth of July, twenty fifteen. Next, apportioned profits raise the chargeable company's profit-related threshold for surrendering management expenses as group relief, under section one oh five of the twenty ten Act, as chapter thirteen showed. And last, the charge is paid as corporation tax of the chargeable company for its own period, so the full machinery of returns, enquiries, interest and penalties applies to it.

This is also where the commonest misconception falls apart. People say that every low-taxed foreign subsidiary is caught by the C F C rules. It is an understandable belief, because the old regime was built that way and because low tax is what the rules are about. But the modern regime asks three questions before anyone pays. Does an entity exemption apply? Do any profits pass the gateway? And does a U K company hold at least twenty five per cent? A low-taxed subsidiary that trades with real local management, or earns a low margin, or is simply small, pays nothing. A highly taxed one never gets that far.

Tarnmoor's annual review.

Now our invented group. Tarnmoor P L C is a widely held, London listed engineering group, and everything about it, its companies, its people and the countries Vallaria and Marrovia, is invented for teaching. Every year its head of tax, Tom Hesketh, runs a C F C review of each overseas subsidiary before the group's return is prepared. We join the review for Group Year Five, and we run the numbers on this year's rules.

The first company is Tarnmoor Capital, the group's finance company in Marrovia, where corporation tax is nine per cent. Tarnmoor P L C funded it entirely with equity, sixty million pounds before our story began and thirty million pounds more on the first of January in Group Year Five. It has an office and staff in Marrovia, where its board meets and its own team makes the lending decisions. It lends sixty million pounds to Tarnmoor Vallaria and, from that first of January, thirty million pounds to Tarnmoor Ireland, both at six per cent. Its interest income is five point four million pounds, its expenses three hundred thousand pounds, and its profit five point one million pounds.

Tom works the order of attack. It is a C F C, owned one hundred per cent by a U K company. No door opens. It is not newly acquired, Marrovia is not an excluded territory, its profits are far above the low profits limits, and its margin is enormous. Marrovian tax of nine per cent is only thirty six per cent of the U K tax on the same profits, well short of seventy five.

So to the gateway. Chapter four does not apply, because Condition D is met. Its only profits are non-trading finance profits. Chapter five does apply. Nothing passes through the U K activities route, because the people who matter are in Marrovia. But all five point one million pounds is earned on equity from Tarnmoor P L C, so all of it passes through the capital investment route.

Then the claim. Both loans are qualifying loan relationships, because both borrowers are foreign fellow subsidiaries controlled by the same U K parent. Tom checks that neither borrower's interest reduces U K profits. Tarnmoor Ireland left the U K on the thirtieth of June in Group Year Four, before this loan was made, and Tarnmoor Vallaria has no U K permanent establishment to which the borrowing could be attributed. The business premises condition is met. The full exemption is not available, because the loans were funded by U K equity, not by qualifying resources. So Tarnmoor P L C claims the seventy five per cent exemption. Three point eight two five million pounds is exempt, and chargeable profits are one point two seven five million pounds. The matched interest exemption adds nothing. Tarnmoor's aggregate net tax-interest expense, about twenty five million pounds a year, dwarfs the one point two seven five million pounds left to test.

The charge follows. Creditable tax is nine per cent of one point two seven five million pounds, one hundred and fourteen thousand, seven hundred and fifty pounds. Tarnmoor P L C holds one hundred per cent, so its charge is twenty five per cent of one point two seven five million pounds, three hundred and eighteen thousand, seven hundred and fifty pounds, less the credit. The C F C charge is two hundred and four thousand pounds. Without the claim it would have been eight hundred and sixteen thousand pounds. Before Group Year Five, lending only to Tarnmoor Vallaria, it made three point three million pounds a year and the charge was one hundred and thirty two thousand pounds. The one point two seven five million pounds of apportioned profit also raises Tarnmoor P L C's profit-related threshold for Group Year Five, as eight hundred and twenty five thousand pounds did in each earlier year.

The second company is Tarnmoor Vallaria, the group's manufacturing and distribution company. Vallarian tax at twenty per cent, on a base that Tom's team has reconciled to the U K computation, is eighty per cent of the corresponding U K tax. The tax exemption applies. Tom stops there.

The third is Tarnmoor Ireland, which became a C F C when it moved its management to Dublin. The Irish trading rate of twelve and a half per cent is only half the U K rate, so the tax exemption fails. Even a fifteen per cent rate would reach only sixty per cent. Its profits are too large for the low profits exemption, and once its purchases of stock from Tarnmoor Engineering, a related person, are stripped out of its costs, its margin is too high for the low profit margin exemption. Two doors stay unexplored. Whether Ireland is on the excluded territories list, and whether a migrating company can have an exempt period, would each need checking in the legislation. Tom needs neither answer, because the gateway gives nil. Chapter four does not apply, because Condition B is met. Tarnmoor Ireland's Dublin staff manage all of its assets and risks. And Chapter five does not apply to its sixty thousand pounds of deposit interest. That interest is on working capital for its trade, and in any case it is under five per cent of trading profits of about two point four million pounds. Tarnmoor Ireland has no chargeable profits.

The State aid saga.

Return now to the courtroom where the chapter began, because the finance company exemption Tom has just claimed is the rule that was on trial.

The seventy five per cent exemption was a policy choice. On the usual account of the policy, the government wanted the U K to be an attractive home for the headquarters of international groups, and those groups finance their foreign operations through finance companies. Taxing only a quarter of the profits of a finance company that lends to the group's foreign operations was meant to be competitive without inviting abuse.

The European Commission saw it differently. In its decision of the second of April, twenty nineteen, it found that the exemption was partly unlawful State aid. The finding covered the years from twenty thirteen to twenty eighteen, where the finance profits came from significant people functions in the U K. By then the Finance Act twenty nineteen had already narrowed the exemption for later periods. H M R C had to recover the aid, and did so by issuing charging notices and interest charging notices to the groups concerned. The General Court upheld the Commission on the eighth of June, twenty twenty two, in cases brought by the United Kingdom and by I T V.

On appeal, the Court of Justice disagreed. Advocate General Medina had advised in April twenty twenty four that the Commission had chosen the wrong reference framework. The court followed that view on the nineteenth of September, twenty twenty four. Selectivity, the core of a State aid finding, has to be judged against the normal tax system. The C F C rules were not a free-standing system. They were part of the U K's corporation tax, which by design does not tax most profits of foreign subsidiaries at all. Because the Commission had started from the wrong baseline, its finding of selectivity could not stand, and the decision was annulled.

The U K then unwound the recovery. The Controlled Foreign Companies, Reversal of State Aid Recovery, Regulations twenty twenty four came into force on the thirty first of December, twenty twenty four. They require H M R C to cancel the charging notices and interest charging notices and repay. The Finance Act twenty twenty six, section fifty one, adds that interest must be paid on repayments of interest made because those notices were cancelled. It is treated as in force from the second of December, twenty twenty five.

Two lessons outlast the saga. The first is about how a rule's purpose is defined. Whether an exemption is a gift depends on what you compare it with. The second is practical. Tax rules shaped by European law remain in U K statute after Brexit, and some disputes that began in Brussels end in Westminster.

Running the regime.

The C F C charge is self-assessed by each chargeable company in its own corporation tax return. The supplementary pages are C T six hundred B, which H M R C titles controlled foreign companies and foreign permanent establishment exemptions, hybrid and other mismatches. Those pages summarise each C F C and show how the charge is computed. The charge is treated as corporation tax of the chargeable company, so the ordinary rules on returns, enquiries, assessments, interest and penalties all apply to it. H M R C's manual includes tax on controlled foreign companies in the total liability that a very large company pays by quarterly instalments.

Several items need positive action. The Chapter nine exemptions must be claimed in the return. A group treasury company's election to treat trading finance profits as non-trading has a twenty month deadline. An exempt period can be extended only by H M R C's notice given before the period ends, so a group that needs more time must ask early. Where several U K companies hold the same interest and one fails to pay its charge, H M R C can recover the unpaid amount from another of them after three months. Joint appeals are possible where apportionment is in dispute.

For the tax function, that adds up to an annual cycle. Tom Hesketh's team keeps a register of every overseas company, with its control position, its residence, its accounting periods and the exemption or gateway analysis for each year. The review happens before the year end where possible, because substance is easier to fix in advance than to prove afterwards. Board minutes in Marrovia, staff contracts, office leases and credit papers are the evidence that Tarnmoor Capital's people functions are where the group says they are. Acquisitions are checked the day they complete, because the exempt period clock starts then.

Planning in this area is legitimate when it follows real activity. Put real people and decisions where the profits are meant to be. Design finance companies so that their loans qualify, and so that significant people functions are genuinely local. Use the exempt period to restructure an acquired group in good time. What a tax function must not do is manufacture the appearance of foreign management while decisions are really taken in the U K. That is the exact case the gateway is built to catch, and the same facts would raise questions of residence and of disclosure.

Neighbouring regimes.

The C F C rules sit in a crowded street, and an Advanced Technical answer is expected to know the neighbours.

Transfer pricing comes first. A C F C's accounting profits, used in several exemptions, must reflect arm's length pricing, but a difference of fifty thousand pounds or less is ignored. In computing assumed taxable total profits, the C F C is treated as U K resident, which might seem to let it use the new exemption for transactions between two U K companies. The Finance Act twenty twenty six closes that door. For chargeable periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six, the residence assumption is ignored for that exemption, so a C F C cannot use it. Chapter twenty seven teaches transfer pricing.

Next, the interest restriction. The matched interest exemption borrows the corporate interest restriction's measure of net interest, and a finance company's lending to foreign group companies is often the other side of debt pushed into higher-tax countries. Interest that Tarnmoor Vallaria pays to Tarnmoor Capital is outside the U K interest rules altogether, because Tarnmoor Vallaria is not within the charge to U K corporation tax. Chapter twenty eight takes the interest rules in full.

Then hybrids. In Group Year Four, Tarnmoor rejected a proposal called Project Undertow, under which Tarnmoor Finance, the group's U K treasury company, would have issued perpetual notes to Tarnmoor Capital. Chapter twenty nine tells that story. For this chapter, notice one thing. A loan to a U K borrower is not a qualifying loan relationship, so interest flowing from the U K into a C F C finds no shelter in Chapter nine.

Last, the global minimum tax. Tarnmoor's revenue is far above seven hundred and fifty million euros, so the rules of the Finance Number Two Act twenty twenty three apply to it. Those rules allocate a parent's tax under a C F C regime, including the U K C F C charge, down to the C F C when measuring that country's effective tax rate. There is a cap for passive and mobile income. Tarnmoor Capital's nine per cent of Marrovian tax and the two hundred and four thousand pounds of C F C charge together come to about thirteen per cent of its profit. That is still below fifteen per cent, so chapter thirty finds a top-up. Whether a foreign country's domestic minimum tax counts as local tax for the U K tax exemption, or as creditable tax, is not settled in the published guidance. Treat it as an open point.

How the examiner tests it.

C F Cs are core, and the examiners test them hard and often. In May twenty twenty four a twenty mark question asked about five overseas companies. It tested the twenty five per cent threshold, the exemptions, comparisons of tax rates, a company on a tax holiday, and the gateways for the one company no exemption covered. The examiners found the exemptions well handled and the gateways weaker, and they noted that candidates kept discussing further exemptions after finding one that applied. In May twenty twenty five another twenty mark question tested five companies again, with apportionment computations. That one went poorly. Candidates did not apply the shareholding percentage, mishandled creditable tax, did not adjust for gains and dividends, and rarely calculated the low profit margin correctly. The exempt period was well understood. The fix for the middle errors is to remember that the C F C is computed as if it were a U K company. Its chargeable gains are part of its assumed taxable total profits, dividends that would be exempt for a U K company drop out, and U K income tax it has suffered counts as creditable tax.

Smaller parts appear too. In November twenty twenty three, a question on the residence of a group's parent ended with a finance company paying one per cent tax, which had to be taken through the non-trading finance profits gateway. In May twenty twenty six, inside a question about a construction company's first overseas contract, a local subsidiary raised the C F C rules alongside the branch exemption. The examiners reported candidates not mentioning obvious points, such as how C F C legislation works. The favourite combinations are worth knowing. C F Cs arrive with residence, with permanent establishments and the branch exemption, and with the incorporation of a foreign branch, because each of those is a way of deciding where foreign profits are taxed.

The style is technical prose with computations, and no letter format. Marks come at half a mark to one mark per valid point. Lay the analysis out company by company in the order of attack, and stop at the first exemption that works. Keep the two thresholds apart, control and twenty five per cent. Use profit before interest and the trimmed cost base for the low profit margin. Adjust both sides of the tax exemption. And bank the easy marks by stating, in two or three lines, how the charge works and who pays it.

What to take away.

A controlled foreign company is a non-U K resident company controlled by U K resident persons. Control can be legal, economic, joint, or accounting control with the fifty per cent condition. The forty per cent rule catches joint ventures between a U K and a foreign partner of similar size, and the more than fifty per cent investment test came in from twenty nineteen.

The modern regime, in Part nine A of the International Act, applies to C F C accounting periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty thirteen, and aims to tax only profits artificially diverted from the U K.

A charge arises only if the C F C has chargeable profits and no entity exemption applies. Test the exemptions in order and stop at the first that works. The exempt period gives twelve months after first U K control. The excluded territories exemption has a threshold of the greater of ten per cent of accounting profits and fifty thousand pounds. The low profits exemption allows fifty thousand pounds, or five hundred thousand pounds with non-trading income of no more than fifty thousand pounds. The low profit margin exemption compares profit before interest with ten per cent of relevant operating expenditure, after removing goods not used locally and payments that are a related person's income. The tax exemption needs local tax of at least seventy five per cent of the U K tax, which at twenty five per cent means a break-even rate of eighteen point seven five per cent, adjusted on both sides.

If no exemption applies, the gateway decides what passes. Chapter four, U K activities, is switched off by any of Conditions A to D, and otherwise turns on significant people functions in the U K, with a trading profits safe harbour. Chapter five catches non-trading finance profits, subject to the five per cent incidental rule, and its capital investment route catches interest earned on equity from the U K. Chapter nine then lets a finance company with business premises claim full exemption for loans funded from qualifying resources, seventy five per cent exemption otherwise, and the matched interest exemption where the group's net interest expense is small.

Chargeable profits and creditable tax are apportioned by shareholding, through chains and over time. Only a U K company with at least twenty five per cent, counting connected and associated persons, is charged. It pays the main rate on its share of the profits, less its share of the creditable tax, in its accounting period in which the C F C's period ends. No losses or group relief can reduce the charge, and apportioned profits raise the profit-related threshold for surrendering management expenses.

In our invented case, Tarnmoor Capital's profit of five point one million pounds in Group Year Five leaves one point two seven five million pounds chargeable after the seventy five per cent exemption. Creditable tax is one hundred and fourteen thousand, seven hundred and fifty pounds, and Tarnmoor P L C's C F C charge is two hundred and four thousand pounds, against one hundred and thirty two thousand pounds a year before. Tarnmoor Vallaria passes the tax exemption at eighty per cent. Tarnmoor Ireland fails it at fifty per cent but has no chargeable profits.

The question was when the U K should tax the profits of a foreign subsidiary. The answer the law gives is narrow. Only when the subsidiary is controlled from here, escapes every exemption, earns profits that belong here, and is owned to at least twenty five per cent by a U K company.

Chapter twenty seven: Transfer pricing and advance pricing agreements.

In Group Year Five, in a meeting room at Calder's works, two members of Tarnmoor's tax team spread a set of engineering drawings across a table. Opposite them sat Dan Hartley. He had left Calder two years earlier, when his division closed, and he had agreed to come back for an afternoon. Our invented group, Tarnmoor, and everyone in it, Dan included, are invented for teaching. Dan's story began in The Living Law, and it continues here.

The questions they put to him sounded like engineering questions. Who decided how these valves were made? Where did the know-how live, in the drawings or in people's heads? And when Calder sold its process valve business to Tarnmoor Vallaria, the group's subsidiary in the invented country of Vallaria, what actually went across?

They were really tax questions, and very old ones. When one company in a group sells to another, what would a stranger have paid? Calder had sold the business's customer contracts and manufacturing know-how for six million pounds. H M R C had opened an enquiry and was arguing for nine and a half million. The answer would turn on facts like the ones Dan could give.

This is the sixth chapter of Part Four. Transfer pricing and advance pricing agreements are core on the syllabus grid, and the examiners have tested them at five sittings since twenty twenty three. The Living Law signposted transfer pricing but did not teach it, so this chapter starts from first principles and goes to Advanced Technical depth. The prologue's BlackRock case gave a first glimpse. For the financial year twenty twenty six the rules have just been reshaped by the Finance Act twenty twenty six, and this chapter teaches the reshaped law.

A family sale priced for strangers.

Start with the problem. A group is one business. It plans as one, borrows as one and reports to its shareholders as one. But the tax system taxes each company separately, and each country taxes the companies within its reach. So the price at which one group company sells to another decides where the group's profit lands.

Picture a family selling a car to a cousin. The price might be generous, or token, or nothing at all, because the money stays in the family. Nobody minds, unless a third party has a share in what each relative earns. In a multinational group that third party is every tax authority. Underprice a sale from a United Kingdom company to a sister in a lower tax country, and profit leaves the United Kingdom without anything real having moved.

The answer the world settled on is the arm's length principle. Each transaction between related parties is priced as if the parties were independent, dealing in comparable circumstances, each able to walk away. Article nine of the model treaty of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, the O E C D, states it for treaties. Its second paragraph lets the other country make a corresponding adjustment so the same profit is not taxed twice. The O E C D's Transfer Pricing Guidelines explain how to apply it.

The precise rule in the United Kingdom is in Part four of the Taxation, International and Other Provisions, Act twenty ten, which I will call the International Act. It does not ask why the group chose its price. It asks what strangers would have agreed, and taxes the United Kingdom company on that basis if the real price leaves it better off for tax.

That leads to the chapter's misconception. Many people think transfer pricing only matters when a tax haven is involved. It is easy to see why. The famous scandals involve brass plate companies on islands. But the rule applies between any related parties, in any countries, whenever the United Kingdom side gets a tax advantage from the actual terms. A sale from a United Kingdom company at twenty five per cent to a sister in a country taxing at twenty per cent is enough. So is a sale to a sister in a country taxing at thirty per cent, if the price shrinks the United Kingdom profit. The rule protects the United Kingdom's share. It does not care where the other profit goes.

The figures show how much is at stake. Advisers summarising H M R C's transfer pricing statistics, published in March twenty twenty six, report a transfer pricing yield of about three point three nine billion pounds for twenty twenty four to twenty five. Much of that came from groups whose counterparties were in ordinary tax systems, not havens.

There is also a policy debate here, and it runs through this book. The arm's length principle treats an integrated group as a set of strangers. Critics say that is a fiction, because a group exists precisely to do things strangers cannot. Defenders say it is the only test that two tax authorities can both apply, and that the alternatives, such as dividing profit by formula, would need a global agreement nobody has. The law, for now, stands with the defenders. Chapter thirty shows how the global minimum tax adds a floor without replacing the principle.

From abroad, to home, and back again.

The modern rules have a short, odd history. For years, United Kingdom transfer pricing applied only where one party was outside the United Kingdom. From chargeable periods beginning on or after the first of April, two thousand and four, the Finance Act two thousand and four extended it, together with thin capitalisation, to transactions between two United Kingdom companies. Chapter two told that story. The usual explanation is European Union law. The Court of Justice had held, in Lankhorst-Hohorst in two thousand and two, that national thin capitalisation rules could breach freedom of establishment. Applying the rules at home as well as abroad removed the discrimination. That is the common account, not something the statute says.

The United Kingdom's own earlier rules were then litigated. In the Test Claimants in the Thin Cap Group Litigation, the Court of Justice gave judgment on the thirteenth of March, two thousand and seven. It held that the United Kingdom's thin capitalisation rules restricted freedom of establishment where they targeted lending within groups. The Court of Appeal gave its own decision in the litigation in twenty eleven.

So for two decades a loan from one United Kingdom company to another had to be priced at arm's length, even though both paid United Kingdom tax. Usually that achieved nothing. If the borrower was disallowed some interest, the lender could claim to be taxed on less, and the two adjustments cancelled out. The Corporate Tax Roadmap of October twenty twenty four said that removing United Kingdom to United Kingdom transfer pricing would reduce the compliance burden.

The Finance Act twenty twenty six did it, with section one six four A of the International Act, the United Kingdom to United Kingdom exemption. It applies for chargeable periods commencing on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six. The same Act reshaped the rest of Part four. It modernised the participation tests and rewrote the rules on guarantees. It told readers to interpret Part four consistently with Article nine of the O E C D Model as approved on the eighteenth of November, twenty twenty five, and the twenty twenty two Guidelines as amended from time to time. It removed the old requirement for H M R C's Commissioners to sanction transfer pricing determinations. And it replaced diverted profits tax with a charge on unassessed transfer pricing profits inside corporation tax, which is chapter thirty's subject.

Commencement needs care, and the exam rewards it. Take a real calendar example, not our story. A company with a calendar year to the thirty first of December, twenty twenty six, is under the new rules for the whole year. Now take a company whose year runs from the first of April, twenty twenty five, to the thirty first of March, twenty twenty six. It is under the old rules for the whole of that year, and the new rules start with its next period. In our invented story, every period is simply treated as falling under the twenty twenty six law.

The basic rule.

Now slow down, because the basic rule is where marks are won. Section one hundred and forty seven of the International Act has five building blocks.

First, there must be provision made or imposed between two persons, the affected persons, by a transaction or a series of transactions. Provision means the terms of the dealing, such as the price, the interest rate, the amount lent, the duration, who bears which risk. A series can include arrangements and understandings, and not every party need be part of every step.

Second, the participation condition must be met. One party must participate in the management, control or capital of the other, or the same persons must participate in both. The next section takes that apart.

Third, the actual provision must differ from the arm's length provision, the terms independent enterprises would have made. And section one hundred and fifty one adds a sharp edge. The arm's length provision can be no provision at all, where strangers would never have dealt.

Fourth, the actual provision must confer a potential advantage in relation to United Kingdom taxation. The statute gives two effects. Effect A is smaller profits. Effect B is larger losses. In deciding whether there is an advantage you ignore the corporate interest restriction and one of the rules that turns interest into a distribution, so you cannot argue that some other rule would have taken the deduction anyway.

Fifth, if all that is true, the advantaged person's profits and losses are computed as if the arm's length provision had been made instead. If both are advantaged, both are adjusted.

Notice what the rule never does. It never reduces United Kingdom profits. It is a one-way street. If a United Kingdom company overcharges a foreign sister, the United Kingdom simply collects more tax, and any relief is for the other country. The disadvantaged party's relief, where it is in the United Kingdom, comes by claim, which a later section covers.

Three boundaries matter in a computation. Part four does not touch capital allowances or chargeable gains. Those regimes have their own market value rules, so a sale of plant or land to a related company is valued under the capital allowances or gains rules, not by transfer pricing. Exchange gains and losses on certain financial instruments, such as those matched or within the hedging rules, are taken outside the adjustment by a section the twenty twenty six Act inserted. And the rule applies for income tax as well as corporation tax, though this paper cares about companies.

Self-assessment is the next point people miss. A company must compute its own profits on the arm's length basis in its return. H M R C does not have to find the problem first. That is why transfer pricing documentation exists, and why a wrong price can carry a penalty.

Now return to the prologue. BlackRock HoldCo Five versus H M R C, decided by the Court of Appeal in twenty twenty four, involved about four billion dollars of loans from one group company to another. H M R C argued that no independent lender would have lent that much on those terms. The First-tier Tribunal found that an independent lender would have lent, provided the borrower gave protective covenants, and that the covenants would have been given. The Court of Appeal restored that finding, so on transfer pricing the taxpayer won. It lost on the unallowable purpose rule instead, as chapter twelve explained.

Commentators have read one passage of the judgment as a hint that H M R C argued from the wrong side of the table. Transfer pricing asks what independent enterprises would have done, and the borrower is an enterprise too. Would an independent borrower in the same position have wanted the debt at all? That argument was not run, and the case did not decide it. It is a live question for future disputes, and a good point to make in an answer. Test both sides of the deal.

The prologue's lesson stands. Transfer pricing is a valuer's question. It asks what the deal would have looked like between strangers. It never asks why the group did it. That is the unallowable purpose rule's job, and a loan can pass one test and fail the other.

Who participates.

The participation condition is the gateway, and the statute has two versions of it.

For an ordinary transaction, condition B, participation is tested at the time the provision is made or imposed. One party directly or indirectly participates in the management, control or capital of the other, or the same person or persons participate in both. Direct participation means control, in the sense of section eleven twenty four of the Corporation Tax Act twenty ten, which is the power to secure that the company's affairs are conducted as you wish, through shares, votes or the constitution. Indirect participation reaches further, including holders who would control if their rights were attributed, and, in a joint venture where two parties each hold at least forty per cent, each of those two parties.

For a financing transaction, condition A, the test is the same but it is met if participation exists at the time or within the following six months. That catches the lender who advances money just before taking control of the borrower, which is exactly the shape of the May twenty twenty three question about a United States company that might take sixty per cent of a borrower.

The twenty twenty six Act added four refinements. A transfer pricing notice under section one four eight A lets H M R C treat the condition as met in a particular case. That is where the parties are not participating under the technical rules but are, looking at all the circumstances, participating in the sense Article nine intends. A new rule on acting together replaces the old one for financing cases. It treats investors with parallel equity interests as participating, so a private equity club deal cannot escape by splitting the shares. A rule on common management treats two companies run by the same people, with a mechanism aligning their shareholders' interests, as controlling each other. That catches stapled structures. And a targeted anti-avoidance rule disregards arrangements with a main purpose of failing the participation condition.

H M R C's guidance says the existing participation conditions remain unchanged. The new rules are additions around their edges.

One link back to chapter one is worth making. Every regime in this book has its own idea of a group. Transfer pricing's rests on control and common control, direct or indirect, rather than on the seventy five per cent chains that group relief and gains groups need. A company can be outside a group relief group, outside a gains group and still inside transfer pricing.

In our invented case participation is never in doubt. Tarnmoor plc owns every relevant company outright, so every dealing between group companies meets it. The real questions are whether an exemption applies, and what the arm's length price is.

Who is let off.

Part four has three exemptions. The first is short. A company that has been dormant since the thirty first of March, two thousand and four, is outside it.

The second is the small and medium-sized enterprise exemption, which I will call the S M E exemption. Its definition comes from a European Commission Recommendation of two thousand and three, kept in United Kingdom law with some changes. A small enterprise has fewer than fifty staff and either turnover or a balance sheet total of no more than ten million euros. A medium-sized enterprise has fewer than two hundred and fifty staff and either turnover of no more than fifty million euros or a balance sheet total of no more than forty three million euros. The figures are measured across the enterprise and its linked enterprises, which are those under common control, counted in full, plus a share of partner enterprises, where the holding is between twenty five and fifty per cent. The United Kingdom version uses the actual period's data, annualised, and drops the European rule that a change of status needs two consecutive years. The thresholds are printed in your tax tables. The twenty twenty six reforms left the exemption alone.

Watch the difference from research and development. The research relief rules double the staff and money ceilings. Transfer pricing does not. Fewer than two hundred and fifty staff is the line here.

The exemption has exceptions. A small or medium-sized enterprise can elect out, irrevocably. The exemption never applies where the other party is resident in a non-qualifying territory, broadly a country without a treaty containing a non-discrimination article. For a small enterprise, H M R C can give a transfer pricing notice only where the dealing feeds patent box profits. For a medium-sized enterprise, H M R C can give a transfer pricing notice requiring an arm's length computation, but only after opening an enquiry, and the company can appeal only on the ground that it is not medium-sized. It then has ninety days to amend its return.

Take a quick example, invented for illustration. A United Kingdom group has two hundred and twenty staff, turnover of sixty million euros and a balance sheet of forty million euros. Turnover is too high for the medium limit, but the balance sheet passes, and either test will do. So the group is medium-sized and exempt, unless H M R C gives a notice or a counterparty is in a non-qualifying territory.

The exam's favourite twist is an S M E that joins a large group. The November twenty twenty four paper had a United States multinational buy a United Kingdom S M E. The marking guide gave six and a half marks for seeing that transfer pricing now applied, because the target's linked enterprises now include the whole group, and for setting out the Master File and Local File it would need.

Our invented case has the same moment. Brackenwell Sensors, with forty five staff, was a small enterprise on its own. From the day Tarnmoor bought it, its linked enterprises included the whole group of about six thousand people, and the exemption fell away. Calder, with about three hundred staff, never had it.

The third exemption is new, and it is the subject of the next section.

The United Kingdom to United Kingdom exemption at work.

Section one six four A switches off the arm's length adjustment for qualifying United Kingdom to United Kingdom provision. The conditions are cumulative. Both affected persons must be companies resident in the United Kingdom. The provision must be relevant to both, with the profits chargeable to corporation tax. Both must be charged at the same rate of corporation tax, so a small profits rate company dealing with a main rate company fails. Both must use the same reference currency. And a list of disqualifications must not apply. There must be no one-sided derivative of a particular kind, no branch exemption adjustments, and no patent box provision, meaning provision that affects a patent box company's relevant intellectual property profits. Neither party may be an excluded company. The excluded list includes banking companies, ring fence oil companies, tonnage tax companies, life insurers' basic business, investment trusts and other funds, real estate investment trusts, securitisation companies and qualifying asset holding companies.

Two switches can turn the exemption off. The company can elect out, irrevocably, for a provision or a period. And H M R C can give a transfer pricing notice where it is expedient to prevent a loss of tax, judged across both parties, normally after opening an enquiry. The appeal is on the ground that there is no such loss. H M R C's guidance explains the logic. Where both parties are taxed in the United Kingdom at the same rate, compensating adjustments usually make the pricing neutral, so testing it only costs compliance time.

One more exclusion links to chapter twenty six. A controlled foreign company cannot use the exemption when its profits are computed for the controlled foreign company charge, even though those profits are computed as if it were a United Kingdom company.

Now the story. Chapter two followed Tom Hesketh's research memo in the first weeks of Group Year One. Tarnmoor Finance, the treasury company, lends four hundred and thirty million pounds to four United Kingdom group companies at six per cent. Later it lends eight million pounds to Brackenwell. All of that is within the exemption, so no arm's length testing is needed. Tarnmoor Finance takes no deposits and carries on no banking business, so, on our invented facts, it is not a banking company. Tom's file records why, because the label treasury company proves nothing on its own.

Chapter two left one point open, the loan to Tarnmoor plc itself. The parent has large management expenses and usually no taxable profits. Is it charged at the same rate as the lender? The published commentary describes the condition as a comparison of rates, with the small profits rate against the main rate as its example. None of it says how the condition reads for a company with no taxable profits at all. So treat this as unsettled. Tom took the prudent course. He relied on the exemption, but he kept evidence that six per cent is an arm's length rate for the parent, using the same market evidence the group uses for its cross-border loan. If the exemption failed, nothing would change.

Two other dealings sit outside the exemption. From Group Year Four Calder is in the patent box. Any dealing between Calder and Tarnmoor Engineering that feeds Calder's patent box profits, such as engineering services supplied to the Ashlar programme, is patent box provision, and must be priced at arm's length. And the interest-free loan of twenty million pounds from Tarnmoor plc to Tarnmoor Ireland was within the exemption only while Tarnmoor Ireland was resident in the United Kingdom. On the thirtieth of June, Group Year Four, it migrated to Ireland, as chapter twenty two described. From then on the loan is a cross-border dealing, and a later section prices it.

The exemption switches off one rule, not the whole code. The unallowable purpose rule and the corporate interest restriction still apply to every one of these loans.

Five ways to find a stranger's price.

Once a dealing is inside Part four, the company needs a price. The Guidelines, which the statute now tells us to follow as amended from time to time, describe five methods. H M R C's manual summarises them in the same terms.

Three are traditional transaction methods. The comparable uncontrolled price method compares the price charged with prices between independent parties for the same thing in comparable circumstances. Suppose, for illustration only, that Calder sold a valve to a sister for nine hundred pounds and to strangers for a thousand, on the same terms and volumes. The comparison would speak for itself. It is the most direct method, and the most fragile, because small differences in terms, volume or market destroy comparability.

The resale price method starts with the price at which a distributor resells to an independent customer and deducts a gross margin that covers the distributor's functions, assets and risks. It suits marketing and distribution. In an invented example, a distributor resells a product for one thousand pounds, and comparable independent distributors earn a gross margin of twenty five per cent. The arm's length purchase price is seven hundred and fifty pounds.

The cost plus method takes the supplier's costs and adds an arm's length mark-up. It suits manufacturing of semi-finished goods, long-term supply and services. A contract manufacturer with costs of four hundred pounds a unit and a mark-up of twenty per cent sells at four hundred and eighty. The cost base must include all relevant costs, including things like share option charges.

Two are transactional profit methods. The transactional net margin method compares a net profit indicator of the tested party, such as its operating margin or its mark-up on total costs, with that of comparable independent companies. Take a distributor with sales of ten million pounds and operating expenses of one point five million. If an arm's length operating margin is three per cent, its operating profit should be three hundred thousand pounds, so its purchases from the group can be no more than eight point two million.

The profit split method divides the combined profit of the parties. It suits highly integrated operations, or cases where both sides make unique and valuable contributions, so that neither can be benchmarked alone. In a residual split, each party first gets a routine return, and the remainder is shared by a key such as relative research spending. Suppose combined profit is ten million pounds, routine returns are three million and two million, and the residual five million is split sixty to forty. The parties end with six million and four million. The full working is in the reading edition.

Which method? The old Guidelines had a strict hierarchy, with profit methods as a last resort. Since twenty ten the test is the most appropriate method for the case, with a natural preference for the traditional methods, and for the comparable uncontrolled price, where they are equally reliable. The choice follows from the facts, and the facts come from a functional analysis.

The November twenty twenty three paper made this a twenty mark question. It asked for methods for a manufacturer, a distributor and a research centre. The examiners reported that the theory was good and the application weak, because candidates did not analyse functions and risks. Match the method to the entity. A routine manufacturer suggests cost plus. A distributor suggests resale price or the net margin method. A contract research centre suggests cost plus, but only after checking who controls the research risk, because a centre that controls its own risks is not a contract researcher.

Dixons and the point of sale.

The first United Kingdom case on all this is still the best illustration of why functions matter. D S G Retail versus H M R C was decided by the Special Commissioners, John Avery Jones and Charles Hellier, and released on the thirty first of March, two thousand and nine. The tribunal described it as the first transfer pricing case to have come before it.

D S G Retail was the Dixons retail group. Its shops sold extended warranties on electrical goods to customers at the till. The warranties were insured, and the insurance ended up with a captive insurer in the Isle of Man, Dixons Insurance Services Limited, a group company. From May nineteen eighty six to April nineteen ninety seven an independent insurer, Cornhill, acted as the fronting insurer and passed the risk on to the captive. The question was whether the retailer had been paid enough for what it gave the captive.

The group put forward other insurance arrangements as comparable uncontrolled prices. The tribunal rejected them. The reason was what it called the point of sale advantage. The retailer owned the moment of sale, when a customer buying an electrical item could be offered a warranty. No independent insurer had that access, so deals that lacked it were not comparable. The tribunal found that, under the old rules, the retailer had provided business facilities to the captive below an arm's length price. It pointed to a profit split based on a return on the captive's capital, leaving the exact figure to be worked out.

Hold on to the method of thought. Who performed the functions? The retailer, through its staff and shops. Who owned the valuable asset? The retailer, through its customer relationship at the till. Who bore the risk? The captive, through its insurance capital, for which it deserved a return, but no more. Functions, assets and risks decide the price. That is exactly what the twenty twenty three candidates left out.

The case also shows how a transfer pricing dispute is really fought. It is not a contest of formulae. It is a contest of facts about what each party did and what each party could have done without the other. The comparables failed because they lacked one fact, access to the customer at the moment of sale. Keep that in mind when we return to Calder, because Calder's dispute turned on the same kind of fact, where the know-how really lived.

Services, stewardship and five per cent.

Head offices do a great deal for their subsidiaries, and the question is what may be charged for. Two ideas do the work.

The first is the distinction between services and stewardship. A service is something an independent company would pay for, or do for itself, such as payroll, information technology support or purchasing. Stewardship, or shareholder activity, is what a parent does because it is the owner, such as holding board meetings, meeting the stock market's listing rules, preparing group accounts, raising capital for the group, overseeing its investments. The Guidelines treat shareholder activities as not chargeable to the subsidiaries. The November twenty twenty three examiners noted that most candidates did not know the stewardship point.

The second is the simplified approach for low value-adding services. H M R C's manual describes these as supportive services, not part of the group's core business, using no unique and valuable intangibles and involving no significant risk. Under the simplified approach the group pools the costs of such services, allocates them to the companies that benefit using sensible keys, such as headcount for payroll, and adds a mark-up of five per cent. Pass-through costs, paid on to third parties, are excluded from the mark-up. The approach still needs documentation, and it is not a benchmark for services outside the definition.

Now the story, from Group Year One. Tarnmoor plc provides services to Tarnmoor Vallaria. Chapter thirteen listed them as group purchasing, engineering standards and information technology support, costing two million pounds. The tax team classed them as low value-adding services. The purchasing was of indirect supplies such as software licences, travel and insurance, not the raw materials Tarnmoor Vallaria uses to make valves, which would be core to its business. The engineering standards work maintained common manuals and audits. None of it used unique intangibles or carried significant risk.

Tarnmoor plc had charged one point six million pounds, a figure carried over from an old budget. At arm's length the charge is cost plus five per cent, two point one million pounds. The provision is between a United Kingdom company and a Vallarian one, so the United Kingdom to United Kingdom exemption is irrelevant, and Tarnmoor is not an S M E. Tarnmoor plc is advantaged, because its income is smaller than it should be. So it self-assesses an upward adjustment of five hundred thousand pounds, worth one hundred and twenty five thousand pounds of tax at twenty five per cent.

Here is the subtle part. Tarnmoor plc pays no tax itself. Its management expenses exceed its income, and it surrenders the excess to Tarnmoor Engineering as group relief. But chapter thirteen showed that the surrender is capped at the excess over its gross profits plus the controlled foreign company profits apportioned to it. The adjustment raises its gross profits by five hundred thousand pounds, so it can surrender five hundred thousand pounds less, and Tarnmoor Engineering pays one hundred and twenty five thousand pounds more. The tax is collected one company along.

The rest of Tarnmoor plc's head office, its board, listing, investor relations, group finance and audit, is stewardship. Tarnmoor does not recharge it, and the Guidelines say it should not. Those costs stay as Tarnmoor plc's own management expenses.

Notice the second benefit of pricing the recharge properly. Chapter thirteen explained that H M R C's view, after its twenty twenty five letter campaign, is that a holding company's costs of serving a subsidiary's business are not its own management expenses unless they are recharged at arm's length. Getting the transfer price right protects the deduction as well as the income.

Tarnmoor Vallaria gets no relief in the United Kingdom for paying more. Whether Vallaria lets it deduct the higher charge is a Vallarian matter. If Vallaria refused, the treaty's mutual agreement procedure would be the route, as a later section explains.

Lending to relatives.

Financing is where transfer pricing meets the corporate interest restriction, and where the money is largest. H M R C's manual puts it simply. Thin capitalisation means looking at every aspect of lending and borrowing from a transfer pricing angle, covering the amount of the debt, the interest rate, the duration, the currency and any guarantees.

Two questions follow. Would an independent lender have lent this amount to this borrower, on its own, given its earnings and assets? And at what rate? If a subsidiary has borrowed more than it could have borrowed alone, the interest on the excess is disallowed. If the rate is too high for the amount it could have borrowed, the excess rate is disallowed too.

Take an inbound example, invented for illustration. A United Kingdom subsidiary borrows one hundred million pounds from its United States parent at nine per cent, paying nine million pounds of interest. An analysis shows it could have borrowed only sixty million pounds alone, at seven per cent. Arm's length interest is four point two million pounds, so four point eight million is disallowed by transfer pricing. Only then does the corporate interest restriction look at what is left. On these figures it takes a further six hundred thousand pounds. Transfer pricing always comes first. Chapter twenty eight shows the second step.

Guarantees were rewritten in twenty twenty six. Where an amount would not have been lent at all but for a guarantee from a participator, such as a parent, the guarantee provision is never treated as arm's length. So the borrower cannot rely on the parent's credit to justify the debt. A United Kingdom company with the right relationship can instead elect to be treated as the guarantor of the excessive borrowing. That links to claims that treat the guarantor as if it had borrowed, so the interest can be relieved there rather than lost. The election is irrevocable and has a time limit of four years after the first period concerned. Implicit support, the incidental benefit a company gets simply from belonging to a strong group, is not a guarantee, but it is taken into account in pricing, which usually means a better rating and a lower rate.

The commencement is staggered. For periods ending on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six, the new guarantee rules apply to borrowing taken on from that date. They apply to all borrowing for periods commencing on or after the first of January, twenty twenty eight, unless the company elects to bring them in earlier.

In our invented case Tarnmoor Finance lends one hundred and twenty million pounds to Tarnmoor Vallaria at six per cent, seven point two million pounds a year. Here Tarnmoor Finance is the party that could be advantaged, because a rate below arm's length would shrink its United Kingdom profit. Tom's team tested the rate against the yields on comparable bonds issued by similarly rated industrial borrowers, allowing for Tarnmoor Vallaria's stand-alone credit standing and its implicit support from the group. No guarantee was involved. Six per cent sat within the range, so no adjustment was needed.

The interest-free loan to Tarnmoor Ireland is the opposite story. From the first of July, Group Year Four, the borrower is Irish. An independent lender would not lend twenty million pounds for nothing. At an arm's length rate of six per cent, Tarnmoor plc must bring in six hundred thousand pounds for the second half of Group Year Four and one point two million pounds a year from Group Year Five. Tarnmoor plc again pays no tax directly. The adjustment reduces the non-trading deficit it surrenders, so the group pays a quarter of each amount in tax through Tarnmoor Engineering, one hundred and fifty thousand pounds and then three hundred thousand pounds a year. Tarnmoor Ireland gets no United Kingdom relief, because it is no longer within United Kingdom tax on the business the loan funds. Its relief is a question for Ireland under the treaty.

Selling the know-how.

Now back to the meeting room. A business restructuring, in transfer pricing language, is a cross-border reorganisation of functions, assets and risks within a group. Moving a product line, converting a full manufacturer into a contract manufacturer, or centralising intellectual property abroad are the classic cases. The question is what an independent party would have been paid for giving up what it gave up.

Two rules frame it. First, the twenty twenty six Act changed how the intangibles regime values these transfers. Between related parties, the intangibles rules normally substitute market value. For a cross-border transfer that is subject to transfer pricing, made on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six, they now use the arm's length price instead, so one standard governs. Second, the Guidelines' chapter on business restructurings, as commentators reproduce it, asks what options were realistically available to each party. An independent company would not accept a deal that left it worse off than its next best option. A mere loss of future profit potential does not automatically need compensation. But a transfer of something of value, such as rights, intangibles or a going concern, does.

In our invented case, on the first of October, Group Year Three, Calder sold its process valve customer contracts and manufacturing know-how to Tarnmoor Vallaria for six million pounds, supported by a valuation report. It sold the related plant for one point four million pounds, which the capital allowances rules dealt with, because transfer pricing does not reach them. Chapter eleven showed the six million as an intangibles realisation credit, taxed at twenty five per cent, one point five million pounds.

H M R C opened its enquiry into Calder's return on the first of June, Group Year Five, inside the window for a company in a large group. It argued for nine and a half million pounds. Its reasons, in our story, were two. The valuation had counted only the remaining terms of the existing customer contracts, while an independent buyer would also pay for the renewals it could expect. And an independent seller in Calder's position had a realistic alternative, carrying on the business itself, so it would not have sold for less than that alternative was worth.

The group's answer came from the functional analysis, and that is where Dan's afternoon mattered. The interviews, with Dan among the engineers, established three things. Development of the process valve designs and the manufacturing methods had been controlled in the United Kingdom, by Calder's engineers and their managers. Tarnmoor Vallaria had performed none of it before the sale. And only part of the know-how was in documents. In our invented story Dan put it plainly. The drawings tell you what to make. They do not tell you how to make it run. The rest sat in the heads of the team, and none of them moved to Vallaria. Tarnmoor Vallaria had to rebuild that knowledge at its own cost.

The first finding supported H M R C. Calder was the developer, so Calder deserved the value of the know-how. The second and third supported the group. What actually crossed the border was less than a going concern, and Calder's division was closing anyway, which shaped its realistic options.

In Group Year Six the parties settled at eight million pounds. Calder's profits for its nine-month period increased by two million pounds, and its tax by five hundred thousand pounds, so the transfer bore two million pounds of tax in total. Interest ran on the extra tax from the dates it should have been paid, which for a very large company are its instalment dates in Group Year Three, at the instalment rate until the normal due date and at the late payment rate after that. The settlement also reopened the group's interest restriction return for Group Year Three, as chapter twenty eight explains. There was no penalty. Calder had kept transfer pricing records, including a Local File entry and the valuation, and it had taken reasonable care. The presumption of carelessness that applies where records are missing, which a later section explains, never arose.

The planning lesson is plain. A restructuring is the transaction H M R C reviews most closely, because it moves future profit for good. The defence is evidence assembled at the time, showing who did what, what transferred, what each party could otherwise have done. Calder's file was good enough to avoid a penalty, but not good enough to avoid an adjustment.

Making both sides whole.

A transfer pricing adjustment taxes the same profit twice unless someone gives relief on the other side. The law has three tools.

The first is the compensating adjustment, for dealings inside the United Kingdom. Where only one party is advantaged and the other, the disadvantaged person, is within United Kingdom tax on the profits concerned, the disadvantaged person can claim to be computed on the arm's length basis too. The advantaged person must have returned its own profits on that basis, and the claim generally has a time limit of two years. Since twenty twenty six this matters less, because most United Kingdom to United Kingdom dealings are exempt. It still matters where a condition fails, for example where the parties pay different rates.

The second is the balancing payment. An adjustment changes tax, not cash. The disadvantaged person may pay the advantaged person to put the money where the profit has been taxed. The statute lets such a payment be ignored for tax, and not treated as a distribution, up to the amount of the compensating adjustment. But one of its conditions is that the payer is within United Kingdom tax on the profits concerned. H M R C's manual adds that there is no obligation to make a balancing payment. So when Tarnmoor Vallaria might have paid Calder the extra two million, the United Kingdom shelter would not have applied, because the payer is Vallarian. In our story the group made no payment.

The third tool, for cross-border cases, is the mutual agreement procedure. Article nine, paragraph two, of a treaty following the O E C D Model provides for the other state to make a corresponding adjustment where it agrees with the first state's adjustment, with the competent authorities consulting where needed. Article twenty five lets the taxpayer present its case to its competent authority, under the Model's wording within three years from the first notification of the action giving rise to the double taxation. Where the United Kingdom must give relief, section one hundred and twenty four of the International Act requires H M R C to give effect to a mutual agreement solution despite any other enactment. Consequential claims can be made within twelve months of the solution being notified. H M R C sets out its practice in a statement of practice issued in twenty eighteen.

In our story Tarnmoor Vallaria asked the Vallarian competent authority for relief after the settlement. In Group Year Seven Vallaria made a corresponding adjustment, treating Tarnmoor Vallaria as having paid eight million pounds for the know-how. How that works in Vallarian law is outside our scope. Advisers summarising H M R C's statistics report that mutual agreement cases concluded in twenty twenty four to twenty five took just under twenty five months on average.

Agreeing the price in advance.

A dispute settled after five years is expensive. For a recurring flow of real value, a group can instead agree the method with H M R C before the periods it covers. That is an advance pricing agreement.

Part five of the International Act defines one as a written agreement between a business and H M R C, made following an application, that settles how certain questions will be dealt with for the periods it covers. Those questions include transfer pricing between associates and the attribution of profits to a permanent establishment. While the agreement is in force, the Tax Acts apply in accordance with it. It ceases if it is revoked under its terms, if a significant provision is breached, or if a condition on which it rests is not met. It is void if it was obtained by fraudulent or negligent misinformation. It can be modified, and it can cover earlier periods, though not before the twenty seventh of July, nineteen ninety nine.

A unilateral agreement binds only the United Kingdom. A bilateral agreement, negotiated by H M R C with the other country's competent authority, binds both, and is the form H M R C prefers. H M R C's manual says an application should ideally be made before the first period it covers, and that the same information should go to both authorities at the same time. It adds that agreements usually carry critical assumptions and an annual compliance report. It aims to complete an agreement within thirty months of a formal application, and says bilateral agreements can take thirty six months or longer. Advisers reading H M R C's twenty twenty three guidance report that an open enquiry into related matters will generally hold an application back.

Our invented group used one. From Group Year Four Calder licenses the Ashlar patents to Tarnmoor Vallaria for a royalty of six per cent of Ashlar sales, about one point two million pounds a year. The royalty matters three ways. It sets Calder's taxable income. It drives Calder's patent box profits, so a later adjustment would ripple into the patent box deduction. And Vallaria withholds tax on it, so the two countries must agree on the figure. Tom's team prepared a bilateral application. Work could not finish while H M R C's enquiry into the know-how sale was open, and once that settled, H M R C and the Vallarian authority agreed the method. The bilateral agreement covers the royalty from Group Year Six.

Two practical points follow. The transfer pricing records rules exclude only agreements made on or before the thirty first of March, twenty twenty three, so Calder still documents the royalty in its Local File. And the proposed new transaction schedule, which the next section covers, would exempt transactions under an agreement in force. An agreement does not remove the work. It removes the argument.

Keeping the file.

Transfer pricing is self-assessed, so the law requires evidence. Three layers apply to a group of Tarnmoor's size.

The first is the transfer pricing records regulations of twenty twenty three. They apply for accounting periods beginning on or after the first of April, twenty twenty three. United Kingdom members of a multinational group above the country-by-country reporting threshold, seven hundred and fifty million euros of consolidated revenue, must keep and preserve a Master File and a Local File in the form the Guidelines describe. The Master File gives the group picture, with its structure, businesses, intangibles, financing and transfer pricing policies. The Local File gives each company's own material transactions, with the functional analysis and the method chosen. They are kept, not filed, and produced when H M R C asks. Transactions between two United Kingdom resident companies are left out of the Local File, unless they involve the patent box or a ring fence trade.

The records carry teeth. Since July twenty twenty three, Schedule twenty four to the Finance Act two thousand and seven has contained a presumption. An inaccuracy involving a transfer pricing computation is presumed careless if the company failed to keep the required records, unless it shows it took reasonable care. Careless inaccuracies attract penalties of up to thirty per cent of the potential lost revenue, deliberate ones seventy per cent, and deliberate and concealed ones one hundred per cent, reduced for disclosure. Failing to keep records at all can also cost up to three thousand pounds for each accounting period.

The second layer is the country-by-country report, under regulations of twenty sixteen. It applies to a group with consolidated revenue of at least seven hundred and fifty million euros, measured, as the rules are usually summarised, on the previous period. The group files a report showing, broadly, for each country its revenue, profit, tax paid, employees and assets. A United Kingdom parent files it with H M R C within twelve months after the end of the period, and H M R C exchanges it with other countries. Advisers summarise the penalties as three hundred pounds for a failure, sixty pounds a day if it continues, and up to three thousand pounds for an inaccurate report. Tarnmoor's revenue was about one thousand three hundred and fifty seven million euros in Group Year One, at the story's assumed exchange rate. That puts it well over the line, so Tarnmoor plc files the report, and every United Kingdom company keeps its Master File and Local File.

The third layer is coming next. The twenty twenty six Act gave H M R C power to require an international controlled transactions schedule, a standard annual dataset of cross-border related party transactions. H M R C consulted on draft regulations from the sixteenth of June to the thirty first of July, twenty twenty six, intending them to apply to accounting periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty seven. The regulations have not been made, so this is not yet law, and it is not examinable on the twenty twenty six Act.

For the tax function the file is a calendar. Each year it refreshes the Master File, updates each Local File for new transactions, re-tests the United Kingdom to United Kingdom exemption, files the country-by-country report, and checks whether any dealing is moving value abroad. A restructuring gets its own file before it happens, with the valuation, the functional analysis and the options each party had.

One more link belongs to chapter thirty. A company that fails to self-assess an arm's length adjustment may face the new charge on unassessed transfer pricing profits, at a higher rate, if its conditions are met. A correct return is the best protection.

How the examiner tests it.

Transfer pricing is core on the twenty twenty six grid. It has appeared five times since May twenty twenty three. The November twenty twenty three paper gave twenty marks to methods, low value services and stewardship. May twenty twenty three tested participation and thin capitalisation with the interest restriction, and the examiners reported time wasted on diverted profits tax, which did not apply to loans. May twenty twenty five put an interest-free loan and an undercharged management fee inside a computation, and the marks came from spotting and explaining them. November twenty twenty four tested the loss of S M E status on an acquisition, with the Master File and Local File. May twenty twenty six fed transfer pricing adjustments into an interest restriction computation. In the same sitting another question excluded transfer pricing, and some candidates wrote about it anyway.

So the style is technical prose and computations, in questions of ten, fifteen or twenty marks, with half a mark to a mark per point. State each condition, apply it to the facts, compute the adjustment, and say who can claim relief. Analyse functions, assets and risks before choosing a method. Remember that an interest adjustment comes before the interest restriction. And when a question excludes transfer pricing, leave it out.

The traps repeat. Candidates forget to test the S M E exemption, or test it on the subsidiary alone instead of with its linked enterprises. They use the research and development ceilings instead of the transfer pricing ones. They try to reduce United Kingdom profits under the rule, which it never does. They give the overseas counterparty a United Kingdom compensating adjustment it cannot have. And for twenty twenty seven sittings there are new traps. They apply the United Kingdom to United Kingdom exemption to a cross-border loan, to a controlled foreign company or to patent box dealings. Or they forget that the exemption switches off transfer pricing alone, and leaves the unallowable purpose rule and the interest restriction in place.

Do not over-write. In the November twenty twenty four question, the examiners noted that some candidates covered transfer pricing in great depth and missed easier marks elsewhere. A clear paragraph on each condition, a short computation and a line on documentation usually earns most of what is available.

What to take away.

Transfer pricing exists because a group is one business taxed as many companies. The arm's length principle prices each related party dealing as if strangers had agreed it. Section one hundred and forty seven of the International Act applies when there is provision between two affected persons, the participation condition is met, the provision differs from arm's length, and it gives a potential United Kingdom tax advantage. The arm's length provision can be no provision at all. The rule only ever increases United Kingdom profits. It ignores capital allowances and gains.

Participation means control or common control, tested at the time, or within six months for financing. The twenty twenty six Act added notices, acting together, common management and an anti-avoidance rule.

Small and medium-sized enterprises are exempt, meaning fewer than two hundred and fifty staff, and turnover up to fifty million euros or a balance sheet up to forty three million euros. Joining a large group ends that. Section one six four A applies for chargeable periods commencing on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six. It exempts most dealings between United Kingdom companies paying the same rate, in the same currency, with no patent box provision and no excluded company. Controlled foreign companies cannot use it.

There are five methods, comparable uncontrolled price, resale price, cost plus, transactional net margin and profit split, chosen by the most appropriate method after a functional analysis. D S G Retail shows why functions, assets and risks decide the answer. Stewardship is not charged. Low value services are charged at cost plus five per cent.

In our invented story, the recharge to Vallaria rose from one point six million to two point one million pounds, costing one hundred and twenty five thousand pounds of tax. The Vallarian loan at six per cent was accepted. The interest-free Irish loan brought in six hundred thousand pounds, then one point two million a year. Calder's know-how sale was re-priced from six million to eight million pounds, costing five hundred thousand pounds plus interest, with no penalty, and Vallaria gave relief through the mutual agreement procedure. A bilateral advance pricing agreement now covers the Ashlar royalty.

Relief comes by compensating adjustment at home, by mutual agreement abroad, and by agreement in advance where the flow recurs. Records are the defence, meaning the Master File, the Local File and the country-by-country report, with careless behaviour presumed where records are missing.

Transfer pricing decides how much interest a company may pay. The next chapter asks how much of what is left the United Kingdom will allow. That is chapter twenty eight, the corporate interest restriction.

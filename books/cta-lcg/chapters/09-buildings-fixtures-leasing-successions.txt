Chapter nine: Buildings, fixtures, leasing and successions.

On the first of September in Group Year Three, Tarnmoor Engineering takes the keys to a new distribution centre. Our invented group, Tarnmoor, is a listed engineering group, and everything about it in this book is made up for teaching. The building is brand new, bought from the developer who built it, for five point two million pounds. To the tax system it is at least three things, and possibly four.

The ground it stands on gets no allowance at all. The heating, the lighting and the electrical systems are plant, and they join a pool. The concrete, steel and roof are a structure, and they earn a slow, straight line allowance that will outlive most of the people in the room.

This chapter is about the assets that sit between land and machinery, and about the moments when allowances move from one owner to another. It is the second capital allowances chapter in Part Two of this book. Chapter eight dealt with plant and machinery in the pools. Here we take the structures and buildings allowance at Advanced Technical depth, then fixtures, long funding leases, successions, contributions and the rules that stop allowances being bought and sold. Almost every topic here is core on the Advanced Technical syllabus for Taxation of Larger Companies and Groups. Two small corners, allowance buying and the special tax sites in freeports, are awareness only. The Living Law, chapter twelve, taught the basics of plant, pools and the buildings allowance. We build on it.

A word on names. The Capital Allowances Act two thousand and one I will call the Capital Allowances Act. The Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act nineteen ninety two is the Gains Act. The Corporation Tax Act twenty ten is the twenty ten Act. All the law in this chapter is the law for the financial year twenty twenty six, as it stands after the Finance Act twenty twenty six. That Act left the buildings allowance, fixtures and long funding leases alone, but its fourteen per cent main pool rate matters here, because fixtures and leased plant often land in that pool.

One building, four tax assets.

Start with the price. When a company buys a building, the contract usually gives one figure. The tax rules need that figure split, because each piece follows a different regime. The Capital Allowances Act says that where property is sold together, the price is apportioned on a just and reasonable basis, whatever the parties choose to call each part. That is section five hundred and sixty two. A label in the contract does not bind H M R C if it is not just and reasonable.

The first piece is the land. Land is never plant, and the structures and buildings allowance expressly excludes the cost of acquiring land or rights over it. H M R C's manual adds that the share of legal fees and stamp duty land tax that relates to the land is excluded too. So the land piece earns nothing until the property is sold, when it becomes part of the base cost in the gains computation.

The second piece is the structure itself. Walls, floors, roof, foundations, car parks, roads and bridges. This is what the structures and buildings allowance relieves, at three per cent a year.

The third piece is fixtures that count as plant. Some are integral features, a list in the Act that includes electrical and lighting systems, cold water systems, space and water heating, powered ventilation and air cooling. These go to the special rate pool at six per cent. Others are ordinary plant fixed into the building, such as a sprinkler system or racking bolted to the floor, which may go to the main pool.

The fourth piece is everything else. Some costs are neither land nor structure nor plant. Landscaping that does not create a structure, land remediation and land reclamation are in this category. They earn nothing.

People assume that if they bought a building, they bought allowances. Often they bought allowances only on the parts the law recognises, only if the paperwork is right, and only at the rate the law sets for each piece. A building is a bundle of tax assets, each with its own rules, and the bundle is only as good as its weakest document.

For Tarnmoor's distribution centre, the developer's price of five point two million pounds is split in our invented case like this. Land, one point two million pounds. Integral features, nought point six million pounds. The structure, three point four million pounds. The tax team has a surveyor's report to show the split is just and reasonable.

Why buildings got an allowance again.

For years before twenty eighteen, most new commercial buildings in the United Kingdom got no capital allowance at all. A company that built a factory could claim for the machines inside it and for some fixtures, but the shell earned nothing. The cost was relieved only when the building was sold, and only against a gain. The Budget of the twenty ninth of October, twenty eighteen, filled that gap with a new allowance for structures and buildings.

The law says the allowance is available where the construction contract was entered into on or after the twenty ninth of October, twenty eighteen. That date is a hard line. If any contract for the physical construction was signed before it, the building does not qualify at all. The rate started at two per cent a year. It rose to three per cent from the first of April, twenty twenty, for companies. At three per cent, the allowance runs for thirty three and a third years.

Three ideas sit behind the design. First, it is a straight line allowance on the original construction cost, not a pool. There is no balancing allowance when the building is sold and no balancing charge. Second, the allowance belongs to the building, not to the owner. When the building is sold, the buyer simply carries on claiming the same annual amount for whatever is left of the thirty three and a third years. Third, the relief is clawed back through the gains computation, not through a balancing charge. When an owner sells, the allowances it claimed are added to its sale proceeds.

The building must be in non-residential use. Dwellings and residential use are outside the allowance, which is why a block of flats cannot qualify. A period of disuse after the building has been in qualifying use does not stop the allowance. A switch to residential use does.

The allowance period starts on the later of two dates. One is the date the building is first brought into non-residential use. The other is the date the qualifying expenditure is incurred.

The allowance does not reach older buildings, but it does reach new work on them. If a company extends or renovates a building constructed in, say, twenty ten, the old building earns nothing, while the capital cost of the extension or renovation can qualify on its own, provided that work was contracted for after the start date. The examiners tested exactly this in May twenty twenty six, with an office built in twenty ten. Many candidates gave the allowance on the whole office. Only the alterations qualified.

Tarnmoor shows the same point. In Group Year Two, Tarnmoor Engineering's capital allowances include a structures and buildings allowance on a one point nine six million pound extension to an older factory. The extension was contracted for after the start date and in use for all of Group Year Two. Three per cent of one point nine six million pounds is fifty eight thousand, eight hundred pounds. The factory it is attached to earns nothing.

What qualifies, and what does not.

The heart of the allowance is qualifying expenditure. For a building a company constructs itself, that is the capital cost of construction. Site preparation counts, and H M R C's manual says this includes demolishing an old building to clear the site. The cost of the land does not. Nor does the cost of altering land, such as reclamation, remediation or landscaping that does not create a structure. Nor does anything that qualifies as plant. Nor does the cost of obtaining planning permission.

The harder cases arise when a company buys a building rather than building it. The law has three rules, and the examiner likes them.

If a company buys a new, unused building from a developer, who built it in the course of a development trade, the qualifying expenditure is the price paid for the relevant interest, after taking out the land and anything that is not structure. That is the rule that governs Tarnmoor's distribution centre. It is generous, because the developer's profit margin is part of the price, and the buyer gets relief on it. H M R C's guidance adds a limit. If a buyer pays more than market value, the allowance is restricted to market value.

If a company buys an unused building from someone who is not a developer, the qualifying expenditure is the lower of the price paid and the original construction cost. A buyer in that position needs the seller's construction figures.

If a company buys a building that has already been used, it does not get relief on the price at all. It steps into the seller's position. It claims three per cent of the original construction cost for the rest of the period, and only if it has the allowance statement, which we come to shortly.

That third rule surprises people. Suppose, for this example, a warehouse cost two million pounds to build and a buyer pays three million pounds for it a few years later, of which eight hundred thousand pounds is land. The buyer does not claim three per cent of two point two million pounds. It claims three per cent of the original two million pounds, sixty thousand pounds a year, for the remaining years. The price is irrelevant.

Demolition ends the allowance. H M R C's manual says that where the whole building is demolished, it stops qualifying, any unclaimed allowance is lost, and allowances already claimed are added to the consideration in the gains computation. Demolishing part of a building is different, because restoration works then count as new capital expenditure in their own right.

Time apportionment needs care. The allowance is an annual amount, reduced proportionately where the period is shorter than a year or the building qualifies for only part of it. Exam questions are worked to the nearest month. In November twenty twenty five, faced with a new company's short first period, some candidates cut the allowance by ninths instead of twelfths, and the examiners noticed. The annual figure is always the starting point, and a month is always a twelfth of it.

Tarnmoor's distribution centre.

Now the numbers for the story. The distribution centre is first used on the first of September in Group Year Three, the same day Tarnmoor Engineering buys it and pays the developer. So the allowance period starts on that date. Tarnmoor's accounting year ends on the thirty first of December, which gives four months of use in Group Year Three.

The structure is three point four million pounds. Three per cent of that is one hundred and two thousand pounds a year. Four twelfths of one hundred and two thousand pounds is thirty four thousand pounds. That is the allowance for Group Year Three. From Group Year Four onwards, Tarnmoor Engineering claims one hundred and two thousand pounds every year until the thirty three and a third years run out, unless it sells the building, demolishes it or stops using it for a qualifying purpose. Over the full period, the allowance adds up to exactly three point four million pounds.

The land, one point two million pounds, earns nothing.

The integral features, nought point six million pounds, are special rate expenditure. The question is whether they also earn the fifty per cent first-year allowance that chapter eight described for new special rate plant. That allowance needs the plant to be unused and not second-hand. Fixtures in a new building bought straight from the developer arguably meet that test, because nobody has used them, but I found no H M R C statement that settles the point for fixtures in a building bought from a developer. In our invented case, the tax team logs the question for review, and the computation takes the cautious route of a writing down allowance of six per cent, thirty six thousand pounds in the first year. If the first-year allowance is available, the figure would be three hundred thousand pounds, with the other three hundred thousand pounds going to the pool.

Notice what is not needed here. The developer held the building as trading stock. It never incurred capital expenditure on the fixtures and was never entitled to allowances on them. That matters, because the fixtures rules we meet later in this chapter only demand a pooling history and an agreed value where a past owner was entitled to claim. Buying new from a developer avoids those requirements altogether. The split of the price is governed only by the just and reasonable rule.

There is a gains thread too. Chapter sixteen, on company gains from property, tells how Tarnmoor Estates sells a depot for six million pounds and rolls part of its gain into this very distribution centre, leaving Tarnmoor Engineering with a base cost of three point seven two two five million pounds. When Tarnmoor Engineering one day sells the building, every pound of structures and buildings allowance it has claimed will be added to its sale proceeds. So the allowance is not a permanent gift. It is a deduction now against trading profits at twenty five per cent, matched by a larger chargeable gain later, which may itself be rolled over, sheltered by losses or never realised at all. That timing advantage is the real value of the allowance to a group that holds its buildings for decades.

The allowance statement and the life of a building.

Because the allowance travels with the building, each new owner needs to know three facts it cannot easily discover for itself. When was the first construction contract signed? How much was the qualifying expenditure? When was the building first used for a non-residential purpose? The law answers this with the allowance statement.

The law says no one can claim the allowance unless they have an allowance statement. For the person who incurred the expenditure, that means making one. For a later buyer, it means getting a copy from a past owner. The statement must identify the building and state the date of the earliest contract for construction, the amount of qualifying expenditure and the date the building was first brought into non-residential use. Since a change in twenty twenty two, it must also give the date of any later qualifying expenditure incurred after first use. Where a building sits in a freeport or investment zone tax site, the statement must show that special amount separately.

Think of the statement as the building's birth certificate. Lose it, and a later owner cannot prove the allowance life that is left.

This is where the buildings allowance becomes due diligence. When a group buys a used building, the buyer's lawyers ask for the allowance statement, warranties that no construction contract predates the twenty ninth of October, twenty eighteen, and the seller's records of claims. When a group buys a company that owns buildings, the same questions apply inside the target. When a group sells a building, it should pass the statement to the buyer, because a buyer who cannot claim will pay less.

Now follow a building through its life. If the owner sells, the allowance for the year of sale is apportioned between seller and buyer, and the buyer carries on at the same annual amount. The seller adds its claims to its sale proceeds for gains. If the building is demolished, the allowance stops forever.

The gains rule deserves a worked example because it runs against instinct. Suppose, for this example, a company built a warehouse for two million pounds on land it bought for eight hundred thousand pounds. Over five years it claims sixty thousand pounds a year, three hundred thousand pounds in all. It then sells for three million pounds. The base cost is two point eight million pounds. The allowances are not deducted from cost. Instead they are added to proceeds, so the gain is three million pounds plus three hundred thousand pounds, less two point eight million pounds, which is five hundred thousand pounds. The full working is in the reading edition. The government's own guidance puts it simply, telling sellers to add the total allowance claimed to their disposal receipts.

That is why the structures and buildings allowance behaves so differently from plant allowances. A plant pool claws back excess relief through a balancing charge taxed as income. A building claws it back through the gain, which may be rolled over or covered by capital losses.

Fixtures, and who owns what is fixed.

Now leave the shell of the building and look at what is fixed inside it. A fixture, in the Capital Allowances Act, is plant or machinery so installed or fixed in or to a building or other land that it becomes part of that building or land in law. Lifts, heating, air conditioning, wiring and sanitary fittings are classic examples.

Fixtures raise a problem that ordinary plant does not. When a machine is sold, it moves with the buyer. A fixture cannot. It belongs in law to whoever owns the land, and the land may be owned by a freeholder, let to a tenant, sublet, mortgaged and sold, all while the same boiler sits in the basement. Without special rules, several people could claim for one boiler, or none could.

The Capital Allowances Act deals with this in its chapter on fixtures, which runs from section one hundred and seventy two. The ownership rules decide who is treated as owning a fixture for allowance purposes. Broadly, it is the person with an interest in the land who incurred the expenditure. Other rules in the same chapter decide when someone stops being treated as owner, for example when it sells its interest in the land, and what disposal value it must bring into its pool when that happens. On a sale of the building, the disposal value for the seller is normally the part of the sale price that is attributable to the fixtures.

There is an old cap that still matters. If a past owner claimed allowances on a fixture and brought a disposal value into account, the new owner's qualifying expenditure cannot exceed that disposal value, plus any installation costs. That is section one hundred and eighty five. It stops a fixture growing in value for tax as it passes from hand to hand.

Here is the image to carry. Fixtures are like luggage on a journey with connecting flights. If the luggage is checked through properly at each change, it arrives with every passenger who comes after. If one owner fails to check it through, it is lost, not just for the next owner but for every later owner too. The precise rule follows in the next section. It has two parts, called the pooling requirement and the fixed value requirement, and between them they explain why fixtures are one of the commonest sources of lost allowances in property deals.

For a group, two practical points follow. First, fixtures sit in the special rate pool if they are integral features, and in the main pool otherwise. A landlord company such as Tarnmoor Estates can claim on fixtures in buildings it lets, because letting commercial property is a qualifying activity. Second, because fixtures are plant, they can attract the annual investment allowance even when second-hand. The full expensing allowance and the fifty per cent allowance cannot be claimed on second-hand fixtures, because those allowances need the plant to be unused and not second-hand.

The pooling and fixed value requirements.

Before twenty twelve, the fixtures rules allowed a familiar abuse. A seller could claim allowances on the fixtures for years, sell the building without bringing a meaningful disposal value into account, and leave the buyer to claim again on its own valuation of the same fixtures. Allowances could be claimed twice on one boiler.

Parliament closed this in two steps. From April twenty twelve, the fixed value requirement made seller and buyer agree the value, or have it fixed by the tribunal. From April twenty fourteen for companies, the pooling requirement made a buyer's claim depend on the seller having pooled its expenditure. The law now says that where a past owner was entitled to claim allowances on a fixture, a buyer's new expenditure on it is treated as nil unless the requirements are met. That is sections one hundred and eighty seven A and one hundred and eighty seven B.

The pooling requirement is met if the past owner allocated the fixture expenditure to a pool, or claimed a first-year allowance on it, in a chargeable period beginning on or before the day it ceased to own the fixture. In plain terms, the seller must have put the fixtures into its capital allowances computation before it sold. It need not have claimed much, and it need not have claimed in that year. But it must have pooled.

The fixed value requirement applies where the past owner must bring in a disposal value on the sale. It is met if, within two years of the transfer, the seller and buyer make a joint election fixing the value, or one of them applies to the tribunal to fix it. There is a further disposal value statement requirement in some cases where the past owner ceased to own the fixture in other ways, which needs a written statement within two years.

The consequence of failure is severe and permanent. If the requirements are not met, the buyer's expenditure on the fixtures is nil, and so is the expenditure of every later buyer whose title passes through it. The luggage is lost.

The law puts the burden of proof on the current owner. A buyer must be able to show that the requirements were met, which in practice means getting copies of the seller's computations and the signed election.

Now the misconception this chapter promised. A property buyer can always claim allowances on the fixtures it buys. That is wrong. The buyer can claim only if the seller pooled, and only if the value was fixed by election or by the tribunal within two years. If the seller never claimed, the buyer may claim nothing, unless the seller was never entitled to claim at all, as with a developer holding the building as trading stock, in which case the requirements do not apply. A buyer who wants allowances must make them a condition of the deal before contracts are signed. After completion, the seller has no reason to help.

The section one nine eight election.

The tool for meeting the fixed value requirement is the joint election in section one hundred and ninety eight, which practitioners call the section one nine eight election. Its twin, section one nine nine, does the same job when a lease is granted rather than a freehold sold.

The election is made jointly by seller and buyer. It fixes how much of the sale price is attributable to the fixtures. It cannot exceed the seller's original qualifying expenditure on them, or the actual price. Whatever is not allocated to the fixtures is treated as the price of the rest of the property. The election is irrevocable, and it must be made within two years of the sale.

The negotiation has a natural tension. The seller wants a low figure, because the elected amount is its disposal value. A low figure means a smaller reduction in its pool and no balancing charge. The buyer wants a high figure, because the elected amount is its qualifying expenditure. Each pound moved between them is a pound of allowances moved from one side to the other. A common outcome is a figure that leaves the seller with no balancing charge, with the price adjusted to compensate.

In our invented case, Tarnmoor Estates buys an office building in Group Year Two. The seller had spent seven hundred thousand pounds on the fixtures years before. The two companies sign a section one nine eight election fixing the fixtures at four hundred thousand pounds. That is within the cap, because it is less than the seller's seven hundred thousand pounds. The fixtures are all integral features, so they are special rate expenditure. The group allocates four hundred thousand pounds of its single annual investment allowance to Tarnmoor Estates, so the whole four hundred thousand pounds is relieved at once. Without the allowance, the fixtures would have gone into the special rate pool, and the first writing down allowance would have been six per cent, only twenty four thousand pounds.

Now the gains trap, which the examiners have flagged. The election does not change the gains computation. Tarnmoor Estates' base cost for gains is the whole price it paid for the office, including the part allocated to fixtures. When it sells, the whole sale price is the proceeds, including the part allocated to fixtures. The examiners, after the May twenty twenty three sitting, reported that candidates deducted fixtures amounts from gains computations. You do not.

The allowances interact with gains in only one way. If the sale produces a loss, the loss is restricted by the net allowances given. Suppose, for this example, the office cost three million pounds, and Tarnmoor Estates has had four hundred thousand pounds of allowances on the fixtures. If it sells for three point six million pounds, the gain is six hundred thousand pounds, with no adjustment for the allowances. If it sells for two point five million pounds, and a new election fixes the fixtures at one hundred thousand pounds, the unadjusted loss is five hundred thousand pounds. Broadly, the net allowances are three hundred thousand pounds, so the allowable loss is two hundred thousand pounds. The full working is in the reading edition.

Long funding leases, where allowances follow the economics.

Leasing raises a different question. When one company owns a machine and another uses it, who should get the allowances? For most of the history of capital allowances, the answer was the owner, which meant the lessor. Lessors with taxable profits bought plant, claimed the allowances and passed part of the benefit to lessees in lower rentals. The largest deals ended in court.

The best known is Barclays Mercantile Business Finance versus Mawson, decided by the House of Lords in two thousand and four. Barclays Mercantile paid about ninety one million pounds for part of a gas pipeline under the Irish Sea and leased it back to the Irish gas board, Bord Gais Eireann. The Revenue's case was that the payment produced no real commercial benefit and existed mainly to generate allowances. The House of Lords held that the lessor had incurred the expenditure and was entitled to the allowances.

In the Finance Act two thousand and six, Parliament changed the question. For long funding leases, it moved the allowances from the lessor to the lessee. The idea is that a lease which is really a way of financing the purchase of an asset should be taxed like a loan. The lessee, who bears the economics of ownership, claims the allowances. The lessor is taxed on a finance return and claims none.

The definitions do the work. A long funding lease is a funding lease that is not a short lease. A funding lease is one that meets at least one of three tests at its inception. The first is the finance lease test, which asks whether the accounts treat it, or would treat it, as a finance lease or a loan. The second is the lease payments test, met where the present value of the minimum lease payments is eighty per cent or more of the asset's fair value. The third is the useful economic life test, met where the lease term is more than sixty five per cent of the asset's remaining useful economic life. A short lease is one with a term of seven years or less, with anti-avoidance for linked leases and leasebacks. Leases of background plant for a building and certain plant leased with land are excluded.

For the lessee, there is a further condition. The lease is a long funding lease only if the lessee's tax return for the period in which the lease begins treats it as one. That choice cannot later be reversed by an error or mistake claim.

When the conditions are met, the law says the lessee is treated as owning the plant and as incurring capital expenditure on it at the start of the lease term. For a long funding finance lease, the amount is, broadly, the present value of the minimum lease payments recognised in the accounts. In return, the lessee's deduction for the rentals is limited to the finance charge shown in its accounts, under section three hundred and seventy seven of the twenty ten Act. The capital element is relieved through allowances instead. Since the leasing standards brought most leases onto lessees' balance sheets, the law also treats a right-of-use lease that meets the lease payments or useful life test as a long funding finance lease for the lessee.

Tarnmoor's machine on a lease.

In our invented case, from the first of January in Group Year Three, Tarnmoor Engineering takes a large, refurbished computer-controlled machining centre on a ten year finance lease from an unconnected leasing company. The present value of the minimum lease payments is one point five million pounds. The rentals are about two hundred and four thousand pounds a year, paid in arrears, at an implicit interest rate of six per cent.

Run the tests. The accounts treat the lease as a finance lease, so it is a funding lease. Its term is ten years, more than seven, so it is not a short lease. It is a long funding lease, and Tarnmoor Engineering's return for Group Year Three treats it as one. Tarnmoor Engineering, the lessee, claims the allowances. The leasing company claims none.

The machine was used before, so it is second-hand. The full expensing allowance and the forty per cent first-year allowance both need unused plant, so neither is available. The one point five million pounds joins the main pool. At fourteen per cent, it adds two hundred and ten thousand pounds to Group Year Three's writing down allowance.

The rentals are not deducted in full. In the first year, the finance charge in the accounts is ninety thousand pounds, six per cent of one point five million pounds. That is the deduction. The capital element of the first rental, about one hundred and fourteen thousand pounds, is not deducted, because the allowances relieve the capital. The depreciation of the machine in the accounts is added back, as it would be for any owned asset.

Now the examiner's angle. In November twenty twenty five, a computation included machinery on a long funding lease. The suggested answer put it in the main pool. The examiners reported that a common error was treating it as special rate expenditure. Long funding lease plant goes in whichever pool its own nature dictates, which for ordinary machinery is the main pool.

There is an open question worth knowing. If a lessee under a long funding lease takes new, unused plant, can it claim full expensing on its deemed expenditure? The Act contains an express bar for transfer and long funding leaseback arrangements, where a company sells plant and leases it back. I found no express bar for an ordinary long funding lease of new plant, and some commentators say the lessee can claim first-year allowances if the other conditions are met. The November twenty twenty five suggested answer gave main pool writing down allowances, without discussing the point. This point is not settled in the sources I could check. In the exam, follow the question's facts, state your assumption, and move on. The story avoids the question by making Tarnmoor's machine second-hand.

Successions, when a business changes hands.

Now follow whole businesses. When one person succeeds to another's trade, or to another qualifying activity, the plant passes with it. The Capital Allowances Act deals with the allowances in sections two hundred and sixty five to two hundred and sixty eight.

The general rule is that the plant is treated as sold by the predecessor to the successor at market value on the date of succession. The predecessor brings market value into its pool as a disposal value, which may give a balancing charge or a balancing allowance if the trade ceases for it. The successor treats market value as its expenditure. It gets no annual investment allowance and no first-year allowance on it. Without that bar, a business could be passed around to refresh allowances on old plant.

Whether a succession has happened at all is a question of fact. Tribunals still use a test from an employment case decided in nineteen sixty eight, Kenmir Limited versus Frizzell. In the words of Mr Justice Widgery, quoted by the First-tier Tribunal in Haymarket Media Group versus H M R C in twenty twenty two, the vital consideration is whether the effect of the transaction was to put the transferee in possession of a going concern. Haymarket was a value added tax case about the sale of a television studio site, not a capital allowances case, but the going concern question is the same. Buying the assets is not the same as succeeding to the business.

For connected parties, market value is often the wrong answer. If the predecessor and successor are connected, and both are within the charge to United Kingdom tax on the activity, they may jointly elect, within two years of the succession, for the plant to pass at its tax written down value. That is the connected-party succession election in sections two hundred and sixty six and two hundred and sixty seven. There is no balancing adjustment for the predecessor, and the successor steps into its shoes, taking over its pools and its history. The election is not available to a dual resident investing company, or where the cross-border transfer rule in section five hundred and sixty one applies.

Suppose, for this example, connected companies transfer a trade whose plant has a tax written down value of two million pounds and a market value of three million pounds. Without the election, the predecessor has a balancing charge of one million pounds, and the successor starts with three million pounds of expenditure but no first-year allowance. With the election, neither side has a balancing adjustment, and the successor continues from two million pounds.

Inside a group, a stronger rule often takes over. Where a trade passes between companies under common ownership, Part twenty two, Chapter one, of the twenty ten Act can apply automatically. Section nine hundred and forty eight then says allowances continue as if the successor had always carried on the trade, and the transfer itself gives no allowances or charges. Chapter nineteen owns Part twenty two. In our invented case, when Tarnmoor Engineering hives its actuators trade down into Tarnmoor Actuators in Group Year Six, the plant passes at tax written down value under that rule, with no balancing charge and no election needed.

Connected parties, cross-border transfers and the Act's toolbox.

Not every transfer is a succession. A group company may simply sell plant to a fellow group company. The Capital Allowances Act treats buyer and seller as connected, using the connected persons test in its final provisions, and section two hundred and fourteen brings in the connected-party rules. The buyer gets no annual investment allowance and no first-year allowance, under section two hundred and seventeen. Its qualifying expenditure is capped at the seller's disposal value, or, if the seller had none, the lower of market value and the original cost to the seller or a connected person, under section two hundred and eighteen. The aim is to stop a group generating fresh allowances by selling plant to itself at a high price.

For assets outside plant and machinery, sections five hundred and sixty seven to five hundred and seventy do a similar job. Where the buyer and seller are under common control, or the sole or main benefit of the sale is a tax advantage, the sale is treated as made at market value. If control is the only reason and there is no tax advantage, the parties may elect for an alternative amount, broadly the lower of market value and the unrelieved balance. That election is barred where the buyer is a dual resident investing company. Within the syllabus they matter mainly for research and development allowances, which chapter ten covers. Section five hundred and seventy A denies a balancing allowance where a tax avoidance scheme has reduced the proceeds.

Then there are the cross-border transfer rules in sections five hundred and sixty one and five hundred and sixty one A. They follow the Gains Act's rules for transferring a business between companies of different states, and for cross-border mergers. Where they apply, there are no allowances or charges on the transfer, and the transferee stands in the transferor's shoes, provided it is resident in the United Kingdom or carries on the business through a permanent establishment here. These rules were built for the European Union. How far they still reach after Brexit is not clear from the sources opened for this book, and their scope is flagged as unsettled. In an exam, recognise them, state the effect and say that the conditions turn on the definition of a relevant state.

Finally, the Act's toolbox, its final provisions in sections five hundred and seventy one to five hundred and eighty one. These hold the definitions the rest of the Act leans on, including the rule that treats certain transfers as sales, in section five hundred and seventy three, and the meaning of connected persons, in section five hundred and seventy five. Along with section five hundred and sixty two on apportionment, they are core on the syllabus because they answer three questions every transaction raises. Was there a sale? At what price? Between whom?

Contributions and grants.

Capital allowances relieve the cost a business bears. So when someone else pays, the law asks who really bore the cost. The general rule, in section five hundred and thirty two, is that expenditure is treated as not incurred by a person to the extent that it is met, directly or indirectly, by a public body or by another person. The rule applies across the Act, including the structures and buildings allowance.

There are exceptions. Insurance money and compensation do not count as a contribution. More important for groups, where the contributor is not a public body and cannot get allowances or a deduction for its contribution, the recipient is treated as having incurred the expenditure after all, under section five hundred and thirty six. The relief is not lost. It stays with the company that owns the asset.

The contributor may get relief of its own. A contributor that pays towards another person's capital expenditure for the purposes of its own trade or other qualifying activity can claim contribution allowances, as if it owned the asset, provided the two are not connected. That is sections five hundred and thirty seven and five hundred and thirty eight, with a version for the buildings allowance in section five hundred and thirty eight A. Notice the group consequence. Two companies in the same group are connected, so a group company that pays towards a fellow member's building cannot claim contribution allowances. The recipient keeps the allowances instead, because the contributor could not claim them. For a group, that is usually the right place for them anyway.

In our invented case, in Group Year Three, Tarnmoor Estates builds a concrete flood wall to protect a riverside site it lets to Tarnmoor Engineering. The wall costs five hundred thousand pounds and is in use from the first of January in Group Year Three. The local authority pays a grant of two hundred thousand pounds towards it. A local authority is a public body, so the general rule applies. Tarnmoor Estates' qualifying expenditure is three hundred thousand pounds, not five hundred thousand pounds. The wall is a structure, so it earns the structures and buildings allowance at three per cent, nine thousand pounds a year. Chapter twenty eight comes back to the grant, because its accounting treatment touches the corporate interest restriction.

Anti-avoidance and the special tax sites.

Two awareness topics close the technical part of the chapter.

The first is allowance buying. When a company has more tax written down value in its plant than the plant is worth in its balance sheet, it holds latent allowances. A buyer with profits could acquire the company and enjoy those allowances. The Finance Act twenty ten inserted Chapter sixteen A into Part two of the Capital Allowances Act to stop this, with effect from the twenty first of July, two thousand and nine. It applies where there is a qualifying change in the ownership of a company that has a relevant excess of allowances, meaning tax written down value above balance sheet value. Amendments in twenty thirteen added thresholds. The rules bite where the excess is fifty million pounds or more, where it is two million pounds or more and not insignificant compared with the benefits of the change, or, for a smaller excess, where the change has an unallowable purpose. When they bite, the excess allowances are restricted, so that a change of ownership cannot be used to put them to work for the new owners. The twenty ten Act also has separate rules on the sale of lessor companies. At awareness level, recognise the pattern. Tax value above book value, plus a change of ownership, means check the allowance buying rules.

The second is the special tax sites. Freeports in England, Scotland and Wales, and investment zones, contain designated tax sites. A company investing in new plant for use primarily in a special tax site can claim a one hundred per cent first-year allowance, and the plant must stay primarily in use in the site for five years. A building in a special tax site earns an enhanced structures and buildings allowance of ten per cent a year over ten years, instead of three per cent over thirty three and a third. These reliefs end on sunset dates. According to the government's guidance, the enhanced buildings allowance applies to expenditure incurred up to the thirtieth of September, twenty thirty one, for English freeport sites, and the thirtieth of September, twenty thirty four, for Scottish green freeports, Welsh freeports and investment zones. The plant allowance follows site-specific dates, which should be checked for each site before use. Tarnmoor has no site in a freeport. In an exam, a scenario that mentions a freeport is inviting you to mention these reliefs.

The tax function's checklist, and the examiner's.

Every property or plant transaction in a group raises the same short list of questions, and most have a deadline.

On buying a building, split the price on a just and reasonable basis. Separate the land, the structure, the integral features, other plant and any non-qualifying costs. Ask whether the seller is a developer, a non-developer or a past user, because that decides the structures and buildings allowance. Obtain the allowance statement and warranties on the construction contract date. Make the section one nine eight election within two years, and check that the seller pooled. Write all of this into the sale contract, because the seller's cooperation ends at completion.

On selling a building, remember the gains add-back for the buildings allowance and the disposal value for fixtures. Agree the fixtures figure with the buyer, and pass on the allowance statement.

On leasing, classify every lease of plant at inception. A ten year finance lease is likely to be a long funding lease, and the return must treat it as one. For an exam answer, the machine goes in the pool its nature decides.

On reorganisations, decide whether a transfer is a succession, a Part twenty two transfer or a simple connected sale. Use the connected-party succession election within two years where it helps. When plant moves between group companies, expect no first-year allowance and a cost capped at the seller's disposal value.

On grants and contributions, net public grants off the qualifying cost, and remember that a group company paying for a fellow member's asset gets no allowance itself.

The examiner tests these topics often, usually embedded in a larger question. In May twenty twenty six, a fifteen mark question on capital allowances included a warehouse bought from a developer and an office built in twenty ten with later alterations. In May twenty twenty five, a computation included the buildings allowance on second-hand buildings with conversion costs, and the examiners reported that qualifying expenditure was weak. In November twenty twenty five, a new company's buildings allowance had to be time apportioned by twelfths. In May twenty twenty three and November twenty twenty five, property questions included fixtures elections. The examiners reported candidates deducting fixtures values from gains, and few dealing with the election at all. In November twenty twenty five, long funding lease machinery belonged in the main pool. The answers are technical prose with computations in the conventional columns. They are never letters or reports.

What to take away.

A building is a bundle of tax assets. Land earns nothing. The structure earns the structures and buildings allowance. Integral features and other fixtures are plant. A just and reasonable apportionment splits the price, whatever the contract says.

The structures and buildings allowance is three per cent a year of qualifying construction cost, for thirty three and a third years, starting from the later of first non-residential use and the date of the expenditure. The construction contract must date from the twenty ninth of October, twenty eighteen, or later. For an older building, only later alterations and extensions qualify. Land, land alterations and plant are excluded. A building bought unused from a developer qualifies on the price paid. One bought unused from anyone else qualifies on the lower of price and construction cost. One bought used carries on at three per cent of the original cost. There is no balancing adjustment on sale. The seller adds the allowances it claimed to its sale proceeds for gains. Demolition ends the allowance. No claim can be made without an allowance statement.

For Tarnmoor's distribution centre, the structure of three point four million pounds earns one hundred and two thousand pounds a year, and thirty four thousand pounds for the four months of Group Year Three. The land of one point two million pounds earns nothing. The integral features of nought point six million pounds go to the special rate pool.

Fixtures follow the building. A buyer can claim only if the past owner pooled its expenditure and the value was fixed, usually by a joint section one nine eight election within two years. The elected figure cannot exceed the seller's original cost. Miss either requirement and the claim is nil for every later owner. The election affects allowances, not the gains computation, except that a loss is restricted by net allowances. Tarnmoor Estates' office fixtures were fixed at four hundred thousand pounds, all covered by the annual investment allowance.

A long funding lease is a funding lease of more than seven years. It meets the finance lease test, the eighty per cent payments test or the sixty five per cent useful life test. The lessee claims the allowances on the present value of the payments, and its rental deduction is limited to the finance charge. Tarnmoor Engineering's machine adds one point five million pounds to the main pool.

On a succession, plant passes at market value with no first-year allowance, unless connected parties elect within two years for tax written down value, or Part twenty two passes it at tax written down value automatically. Connected sales of plant give no annual investment or first-year allowance, and the cost is capped at the seller's disposal value.

Public grants reduce qualifying expenditure. Connected contributors get no contribution allowance. Tarnmoor Estates' flood wall qualifies on three hundred thousand pounds.

Allowance buying and the freeport reliefs are awareness topics. Recognise them and state the rule.

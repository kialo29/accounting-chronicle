# Part Four: Across Borders.

# Chapter 22: Where a company lives, and how it leaves.

On 1 March GY4 a letter left Leeds for HMRC. It came from Tarnmoor Ireland Ltd (TIL), a company in our invented group, Tarnmoor. Tarnmoor, its companies, its people and its numbers are invented for teaching, as they are throughout this book. Ireland is real. The letter said three things. TIL intended to stop being resident in the UK. It would do so at the end of 30 June. And here was a statement of the tax it owed, with a proposal for how that tax would be secured before it went.

TIL was incorporated in Ireland. Its customers were Irish. Yet for years it had paid UK corporation tax on every euro it earned, because its directors met in Leeds. Now the board had decided to move its meetings, and its real decisions, to Dublin. A company cannot simply walk out of the UK tax net. It must tell HMRC first, account for what it owes, and pay tax on gains it has never realised.

This chapter asks two questions. Where does a company live for tax purposes? And what happens when it leaves? It opens Part Four, on profits that cross borders. On the LCG grid, **company residence** (with the chargeable profits of non-UK resident companies) and **non-resident and dual resident companies** (gains) are **core (grade 1)**; **migration of a company (post 1 January 2020 only)** is **non-core (grade 2)**. The chapter builds on *The Living Law* (TKS), chapter 20 (company residence and *De Beers*) and chapter 26 (the treaty tie-breaker, including Article 4 of the Multilateral Instrument for the UK and Ireland). Here we take both to Advanced Technical depth, then follow TIL out of the door.

| Corporation Tax Act 2009 (CTA 2009) | the 2009 Act | ss 14, 18 (residence); s 10 (AP ends); ss 41, 162 (deemed cessation, stock); s 333 (loan relationships); s 609 (derivatives); s 859 (intangibles) |

## Two tests, one question.

Residence decides how much of a company's income the UK claims. A UK resident company is charged on all its profits wherever they arise, subject to reliefs such as the branch exemption (chapter 25) and foreign tax credit (chapter 24). A non-resident company is charged only on narrow categories of UK profit, such as a trade carried on through a UK permanent establishment or UK property income (CTA 2009 s 5; chapter 23 owns that list). Residence is the first switch in every international answer.

There are two tests, and a company need pass only one.

1. **Incorporation (statute).** A company incorporated in the UK is UK resident (CTA 2009 s 14). It does not matter where its directors live, where its board meets or where it trades: a company registered at Companies House carries its residence with it like a birth certificate.
2. **Central management and control (case law).** A company incorporated abroad is UK resident if its central management and control (CMC) is exercised here. The House of Lords settled the test in *De Beers Consolidated Mines Ltd v Howe* [1906] AC 455, where a South African-registered diamond company with South African mines was UK resident because the directors who made its real decisions met in London (TKS chapter 20).

The two tests work as a ratchet. Incorporation keeps a UK company resident whatever happens to its board. CMC can make a foreign company resident too, and a foreign company can drift in and out of residence as its decision making moves. Only one route takes residence away from a UK-incorporated company, and it runs through a treaty (s 18, below).

> **Misconception: "a UK company can move its tax residence abroad by moving its board."** It cannot. Moving the board of a UK-incorporated company leaves it UK resident under s 14. At most it becomes dual resident. Whether it then becomes non-resident depends on a treaty tie-breaker (and, under the Multilateral Instrument, on the two tax authorities agreeing): CTA 2009 s 18. A board meeting in Paris does not change a birth certificate issued by Companies House.

TIL shows the other side of the ratchet. It was incorporated in Ireland, so s 14 never applied to it. It was UK resident only because its CMC was in Leeds. Move the control to Dublin, genuinely and completely, and it stops being UK resident as a matter of domestic law.

## Where the mind of a company lives.

CMC sounds precise and is not. The courts have always treated it as a question of fact. In *De Beers*, Lord Loreburn LC said that "the real business is carried on where the central management and control actually abides" (at 458, as quoted in *Wood v Holden*).

Think of a company as having a body and a mind. The body is its factories, offices, staff and customers; the mind is the small group of people who decide its strategy, approve major contracts, raise finance, declare dividends and appoint managers. Residence follows the mind. The test looks for the **highest level of control**, not day-to-day management: the factory manager in Cork who runs production is managing; the board in Leeds that decides whether to build the factory, how to fund it and whether to sell it is exercising CMC.

In most companies the highest level of control is exercised by the board, acting as a board. So the usual question is where the board meets and decides; but that is a starting point, not an answer. **HMRC's view** is set out in Statement of Practice 1/90, reproduced at INTM120200: the location of board meetings, "although important in the normal case", is not necessarily conclusive; the place of directors' meetings "is significant only insofar as those meetings constitute the medium through which central management and control is exercised".

Two warnings follow. A board which meets abroad but merely signs decisions made elsewhere is not exercising control there: the meeting is theatre. And a board can exercise control without meeting at all, through written resolutions, calls and informal decisions; if the directors who really decide all live and work in the UK, the company may be UK resident even if its formal meetings are held offshore.

HMRC's guidance also recognises modern board practice. It gives examples where it would **not usually review** residence, including a UK-based director who habitually joins overseas meetings by electronic link (INTM120150), and its pandemic guidance accepts that a company does not necessarily become UK resident because a few board meetings are held, or some decisions taken, here for a short period (INTM120185). These are statements of HMRC practice, not law: they say where HMRC will look, not what a court would find.

**In our invented case,** before GY4 TIL was UK resident on the plainest facts. Its board was made up of Tarnmoor executives based in Leeds; it met in Leeds; it approved the Irish budgets, the purchase of a UK warehouse (GY1) and the acquisition of an Irish distributor's business (GY2). Its residence was no accident: it kept TIL inside the UK group for group relief and the gains rules (chapter 1).

## Usurped, or merely influenced.

Most foreign subsidiaries of UK groups have boards abroad that listen closely to the parent. The cases that matter most to a group tax function ask: when a parent tells a subsidiary's board what it wants, where is the subsidiary's mind?

**Usurped.** In *Unit Construction Co Ltd v Bullock* [1960] AC 351 (HL), a UK company's African subsidiaries had constitutions requiring their boards to meet, and their business to be managed, outside the UK. In practice the local boards stood back and the parent's board in London took the real decisions. The subsidiaries were UK resident. Lord Radcliffe treated the location of control as a matter of fact, of "actuality"; it did not matter that the parent's conduct was contrary to the subsidiaries' constitutions. Residence follows what happens, not what the documents say should happen.

**Influenced.** *Wood v Holden* [2006] EWCA Civ 26 (Chadwick LJ giving the leading judgment; Moore-Bick LJ and Sir Christopher Staughton). Note first: Mr Wood was the businessman behind Ron Wood Greetings Card Holdings Ltd, **not the musician**. In 1996 he and his wife sold their shares for £23.7m through a chain of companies ending with a Dutch company, Eulalia Holding BV, whose sole managing director was a Dutch trust company. The planning depended on Eulalia being resident outside the UK. HMRC argued that Eulalia's director simply did what Mr Wood and his advisers wanted, so no real decisions were taken in the Netherlands. The Special Commissioners agreed; Park J reversed them; the Court of Appeal upheld Park J. Eulalia was Dutch resident.

The distinction is the one every group tax function lives by:

| Board that is... | Result | Authority |
|---|---|---|
| Bypassed: stands aside while someone else decides | Usurped: the company lives where the usurper decides | *Unit Construction* |
| Advised, proposed to and influenced, but itself considers and takes the decision | Keeps CMC | *Wood v Holden* |
| Formally decides, but implements an uncommercial decision really taken by the parent | Parent's location | *Development Securities* (below) |

A client who always follows a solicitor's advice still makes the decision; only a client who hands over the pen has stopped deciding. The legal rule is narrower: the court asks whether the board actually considered and took the decisions that matter, at the highest level of the company's affairs, and where. Influence is not control. But *Wood v Holden* is reassuring only up to a point: a board that never asks a question will not necessarily be respected.

## When the parent really decides.

*HMRC v Development Securities plc* [2020] EWCA Civ 1705 (David Richards, Newey and Nugee LJJ) is the modern counterweight. Development Securities, a UK property group, used a 2004 scheme devised by a large accountancy firm to make use of indexation allowance on assets standing at a loss. New Jersey subsidiaries with Jersey-resident directors were to buy assets from the group under options at prices well above market value, and later become UK resident. The scheme depended on their being Jersey resident when they bought.

| Tribunal | Finding |
|---|---|
| FTT | Jersey boards had not acted improperly and had checked legality, but had not engaged with the uncommercial substance; they implemented the parent's decision: **UK resident** |
| UT | The directors had applied their minds and had not abdicated their responsibilities: **Jersey resident** |
| Court of Appeal | UT's criticisms of the FTT not well-founded; FTT decision restored: **UK resident** when the options were exercised |

The case did not overrule *Wood v Holden*. Both ask by whom, and where, the real decision was taken; the answers differed on the facts. Eulalia's director took a decision that was, for Eulalia, an ordinary one. The Jersey directors approved transactions against their own companies' interests that made sense only for the group. A board that signs something uncommercial for its own company because the parent wants it is hard to describe as the company's mind.

The reasoning was not unanimous: one member of the court expressed "very considerable reservations" about the FTT's reasoning while agreeing with the outcome, and commentators still debate how much active engagement is enough. Two lessons are clear: a subsidiary board asked to do something uncommercial will be scrutinised hard; and evidence that the board understood, questioned and could have refused a proposal is far more persuasive than evidence that it met in the right place.

**In our invented case,** TIL's migration turned the lesson around. To leave the UK, its control had to move to Dublin in fact, not just on paper. The board was reconstituted with effect from the end of 30 June GY4: a majority of Irish-resident directors with industry experience joined, the Leeds executives stepped down, and meetings moved to Dublin. The board was given authority over budgets, contracts and financing, and used it. Tom Hesketh, the group head of tax, wrote the new governance protocol; its first line read (in our invented story) "decisions are made in the room, not before it".

> **Going further: governance for overseas subsidiaries.** The same lesson runs the other way for subsidiaries a group wants to keep abroad (for Tarnmoor, TVS in Vallaria and TCM in Marrovia, both invented). A defensible file shows: directors resident locally with the skills to decide; board packs circulated in time to be read; minutes that record discussion, questions and alternatives, not just resolutions; decisions taken at the meeting, not pre-agreed by e-mail from the parent; a delegation framework that lets the local board say no; and an annual residence review by the tax function. The group may propose and influence (*Wood v Holden*); it must not decide for them (*Unit Construction*, *Development Securities*). A subsidiary properly resident abroad still meets the CFC rules (chapter 26).

## Two countries, one company.

Residence tests are national, and nothing stops two countries claiming the same company. A company incorporated in one country and managed in another will often be resident in both: dual residence.

Treaties solve the problem in two steps (TKS chapter 26):

1. **Treaty residence.** Article 4(1) of a treaty based on the OECD Model treats as resident a person "liable to tax therein by reason of their domicile, residence, place of management or other criterion of a similar nature", excluding persons liable only on local-source income (HMRC's description, INTM153050).
2. **Tie-breaker** for a person resident in both:
   - **Older treaties:** resident only where its **place of effective management** (POEM) is situated. POEM is a treaty concept, not the same words as CMC, though on most facts the two point to the same place. HMRC: "Typically the tie-breaker awards residence to the country in which the effective management of the company is located" (INTM154050).
   - **MLI Article 4** (the Multilateral Instrument, in force for the UK from 1 October 2018): the competent authorities "shall endeavour to determine by mutual agreement" the company's residence, having regard to its place of effective management, place of incorporation and other relevant factors; without agreement, no treaty relief except as the authorities agree. The UK and Ireland both adopted it; for UK corporation tax it has effect from **1 April 2020** (HMRC's synthesised UK–Ireland text).

**Treaty non-residence in domestic law.** CTA 2009 s 18: a company that would be UK resident but is regarded by a treaty as resident only in the other territory is treated as **not UK resident for all purposes of the Taxes Acts** (from 30 November 1993; formerly FA 1994 s 249). It is the only route by which a UK-incorporated company can become non-resident, and under MLI Article 4 it now depends on an agreement between tax authorities, not on a rule a company can engineer by moving its meetings.

**Timing.** *HMRC v Smallwood* [2010] EWCA Civ 778 concerned a marketed "round the world" trust scheme: UK trustees were replaced by a Mauritian trustee, a gain was realised, and UK trustees were reappointed within the same tax year. The majority held that the tie-breaker could not be applied as a "snapshot" at the moment of disposal; viewed in the context of the whole scheme, POEM was in the UK and the UK could tax. Smallwood involved trustees, not a company, but the lesson carries over: a tie-breaker is not a stopwatch to be started and stopped around a transaction.

> **Exam lens: residence and dual residence.**
> - **Grade:** company residence and chargeable profits of non-UK resident companies **1 (core)**; non-resident and dual resident companies (gains) **1 (core)**. Check the 2027 grid when published (no change expected: the 2028 grid keeps residence in the key principles).
> - **Past appearances:** **N23 Q1** (20 marks, 9/3/8): residence of an Anglo-Belgian parent before and after board changes (incorporation, CMC, **supplied treaty tie-breaker** using POEM), CT administration and a CFC finance company; generally good, but some candidates used the OECD Model instead of the **supplied article**, and some strayed into DPT and CIR. **N24 Q6** (10 marks): a non-UK company setting up in the UK by branch or subsidiary: PE, CMC, UK residence, when a non-resident is chargeable; very well answered when tied to the scenario.
> - **Style:** technical prose; apply each test in turn (incorporation, CMC, treaty, s 18) to the facts at each date; 0.5–1 mark per point.
> - **Traps:** saying a UK company becomes non-resident by moving its board; using the Model's wording when an extract is supplied; treating board location as conclusive; forgetting s 18 makes treaty non-residence apply for **all** tax purposes.

**In our invented case,** TIL was dual resident before migrating: UK resident by CMC in Leeds; Ireland also treated it as resident; and the two competent authorities had agreed, before the story begins, that it was UK resident for treaty purposes. (This book does not discuss Irish domestic law.) When control moved to Dublin, TIL ceased to be UK resident under domestic law, and the authorities agreed it was Irish resident from the migration date.

## What dual residence costs.

A company that stays UK resident while also resident abroad (because the tie-breaker favours the UK, or no treaty applies) is inside the UK charge, but Parliament is wary of it: a company taxed in two countries can sometimes deduct one expense in both, or use one loss twice. The rules target the **dual resident investing company** (DRIC), broadly a dual resident company that is not a trading company, or whose trade consists mainly of holding shares, making loans or similar group-finance activities.

| Rule | Effect | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Group relief | A dual resident company cannot surrender losses if it meets the DRIC conditions | CTA 2010 s 109 |
| No gain, no loss | A DRIC cannot receive assets at no gain, no loss within a gains group | TCGA 1992 s 171(2)(d) |
| Group roll-over | Excluded where a DRIC would apply the proceeds | TCGA 1992 s 175(2) |
| Capital allowances | Connected-party succession elections (transfer at TWDV) and s 570 elections barred where a DRIC is involved | CAA 2001 s 266(1)(c), s 570(2)(b) |
| Branch exemption | While dual resident, a company cannot exempt PE profits in the territory in which it is treaty resident (HMRC's view) | INTM287010; chapter 25 |

The examiners tested the DRIC group relief bar in **N24 Q2** (20 marks, group and consortium relief), and reported that it was missed.

**In our invented case,** TIL escaped all of this. It was a trading company (distributing valves and parts), so not a DRIC. While UK resident it could surrender and claim group relief (chapter 1) and receive assets at no gain, no loss. Its dual residence was a fact to manage: the tax function kept the competent authority agreement on file, reviewed board minutes each year, and made sure no Irish meeting quietly took a decision that belonged in Leeds.

**SAO.** The senior accounting officer rules turn on UK incorporation. TIL (Irish-incorporated) was never inside them, even while UK resident; its migration changes nothing on that front (chapter 4).

## Leaving, and why leaving costs money.

When a company stops being UK resident, three things happen at once: its accounting period ends; the UK loses its claim to tax its future foreign profits and gains; and it may carry assets that grew in value while it was resident, untaxed because unrealised.

**The AP ends** (CTA 2009 s 10(1)(g); chapter 3). TIL's AP that began on 1 January GY4 ended on 30 June GY4. Its return for those six months is the migration return, and every exit charge falls into it.

**Why exit charges.** A resident company is taxed on worldwide gains when realised; if it could leave just before selling, gains built up during residence would escape. So departure itself is treated as a realisation. Picture a hotel guest who has been helping themselves from the minibar all week: the hotel presents the bill at checkout, not after the guest has finished the bottles at home. The rule: the company is treated as disposing of its assets at market value immediately before it leaves, and reacquiring them at that value.

**The old group postponement is gone.** TCGA s 187 let a company leaving as a 75% subsidiary of a UK-resident company jointly elect to postpone the gain on its foreign trading assets. It was **repealed by FA 2019 Sch 8 para 9**, where s 185 applies on a company ceasing to be UK resident **on or after 1 January 2020** (para 9(5)). For migrations since then, the gains charge can only be **paid** over time, under a payment plan, where the company qualifies.

**Where today's rules come from.** In *National Grid Indus BV* (C-371/10), decided by the Court of Justice in 2011, a Dutch company moved its place of effective management to the UK and the Netherlands taxed its unrealised gains at once. The Court held that a member state may fix the tax on gains accrued within its jurisdiction, but that immediate recovery was disproportionate; offering deferral, possibly with interest, would be acceptable [secondary sources; the judgment was not opened]. The UK introduced exit charge payment plans (FA 2013 Sch 49, inserting TMA Sch 3ZB) with effect from **11 December 2012** (CG13430). The EU Anti Tax Avoidance Directive then set a common model, and FA 2019 Sch 8 (made while the UK was still bound) turned the plans into fixed instalments over five years. Rules shaped by EU freedoms remain on the UK statute book in EU form: a post-Brexit oddity with a practical consequence (below).

## The gains exit charge, and the land that waits.

**TCGA 1992 s 185.** When a company ceases to be UK resident it is deemed to dispose of **all its assets** at market value immediately before the relevant time and to reacquire them at that value (s 185(2); CG42370). Every chargeable asset is caught, wherever situated, unless excepted. Indexation runs only to December 2017 for assets held before then; losses on the deemed disposals are allowable in the ordinary way.

| Rule | Detail | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Excepted assets | Assets situated in the UK and used or held for a trade carried on through a UK PE after migration (they stay within the UK charge: chapter 23) | s 185(4) |
| Roll-over blocked | No roll-over from an asset disposed of before migration into an asset acquired after it, unless the new asset is a UK PE asset | s 185 |
| Ceasing to exist | Is not ceasing to be resident: the charge is about departure, not death | s 185 |
| Old postponement | s 187 repealed for migrations on or after 1 January 2020 | FA 2019 Sch 8 para 9 |

**UK land: s 187B.** Since **6 April 2019** non-resident companies have been chargeable on gains on UK land and certain indirect interests in it (TCGA s 2B, Sch 1A; chapter 23). So there is no need to tax UK land at departure: the UK can tax it when it is sold. **TCGA s 187B** (substituted by FA 2019 Sch 1 para 67): where an interest in UK land is deemed disposed of under s 185(2), the gain or loss **does not accrue at migration**; on a later disposal of the whole or part of the interest, the postponed amount is treated as accruing then, **in addition to** the gain or loss on the actual disposal.

- **Automatic:** no claim.
- **Election out:** the company may elect to exclude a deemed disposal from s 187B **within 2 years** after the day of the deemed disposal (s 187B(5)–(6)). Reasons: a **loss** on the land that the company wants in its migration period against other gains, or capital losses available now to absorb the gain.
- **Two layers on a later sale:** base cost after migration = market value at migration (the s 185 reacquisition), so the actual gain measures post-departure growth and the postponed amount measures pre-departure growth. Together they equal the gain over original cost: nothing lost, nothing taxed twice.

> **Worked example 22.1: TIL's English warehouse (invented)**
>
> TIL bought the warehouse in GY1 for £3,000,000 and let it to an unconnected logistics company. Market value at migration (30 June GY4): £3,800,000.
>
> | £ | |
> |---|---|
> | Deemed proceeds (s 185(2)) | 3,800,000 |
> | Cost | (3,000,000) |
> | Indexation (acquired after December 2017) | nil |
> | Deemed gain | 800,000 |
> | Accruing at migration (s 187B) | **nil** (postponed) |
>
> **Illustration only (not a story fact): suppose TIL later sells the warehouse for £4,500,000.**
>
> | £ | |
> |---|---|
> | Proceeds | 4,500,000 |
> | Base cost (market value at migration) | (3,800,000) |
> | Actual gain | 700,000 |
> | Postponed gain accruing on the same disposal (s 187B) | 800,000 |
> | **Total chargeable** | **1,500,000** |
> | Check: proceeds less original cost (4,500,000 − 3,000,000) | 1,500,000 |
>
> Had TIL elected out within two years, the £800,000 would have been taxed in the migration AP: CT at 25% of **£200,000**, eventually payable either way.

## The other exits.

Gains are only one kind of value a departing company carries. Each income code has its own exit, and an AT answer lists them all. **N25 Q3** expected trading stock, machinery, derivatives, land and intangibles.

**The trade: deemed cessation.** When a company ceases to be within the charge to CT in respect of a trade, it is treated as permanently ceasing to carry it on (CTA 2009 s 41(2)). A company that migrates and keeps no UK PE is in exactly that position: its trade carries on in the real world, but for UK tax it has stopped.

| Item | Exit rule | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Trading stock | Valued under the cessation rules: unless sold to a person who carries on (or will carry on) a UK trade and can deduct the cost, brought in at the amount it would have realised on an open-market sale at cessation | CTA 2009 ss 41, 162 |
| Plant and machinery | End of the qualifying activity is a disposal event (CAA s 61(1)(f), applied by s 577(2) where a company ceases to be within the charge, per HMRC OT42550); plant kept is brought in at market value; balancing charge or allowance in the final chargeable period; **no WDA** in that period | CAA 2001 ss 55, 61, 577 |
| Intangible fixed assets | Deemed realisation and reacquisition at **market value** immediately before ceasing to be resident; credit = MV − TWDV | CTA 2009 s 859 |
| Loan relationships | Deemed assignment and reacquisition at **fair value** immediately before ceasing to be resident; excludes items held for a UK PE, a UK land dealing or development trade, a UK property business or other UK property income | CTA 2009 s 333 |
| Derivative contracts | As loan relationships, at fair value | CTA 2009 s 609 |
| Chargeable assets (including shares) | s 185 deemed disposal; for shares, test the SSE first (chapter 18): at least 10% needed | TCGA 1992 s 185; Sch 7AC |

> **Trap (N25 Q3).** The derivatives exit (s 609) is a fair value measurement on departure. It has nothing to do with the Disregard Regulations (chapter 12); the examiners reported that some candidates wrongly discussed them.

The pattern is simple. Every exit turns value accrued under UK jurisdiction into taxable profit, at market or fair value, on the last day of residence. A good answer walks through the balance sheet asset by asset, asking which code taxes each one and whether an exception keeps it in the UK.

> **Exam lens: migration exits.**
> - **Grade:** migration of company (post 1 January 2020 only) **2 (non-core)**: working knowledge plus the main computation. The gains exit (s 185) also sits within "non-resident and dual resident companies" **1 (core)**.
> - **Past appearance:** **N25 Q3** (15 marks): residence tests; exit charges on deemed disposal, trading stock, machinery, derivatives, land and intangibles; **UK land automatically postponed** (the land in the question had been acquired after 5 April 2019); payment plans; notice and statement to HMRC and penalties. Common errors: **missing the automatic land postponement**, wrongly discussing the Disregard Regulations, and weak administration and payment plan detail.
> - **Layout:** a schedule of assets (book value, market value, exit amount, code, plan-eligible?), then the CT for the migration AP, then CT1 − CT2.
> - **Traps:** citing s 187 postponement (repealed); taxing UK land at migration; forgetting stock and plant (the deemed cessation); forgetting that the AP ends on migration; offering a payment plan to a UK-incorporated company.

For a six-month AP ending on the last day of a month, HMRC's manual (CTM92815; chapter 3) puts instalments on **14 March** and **14 June GY4**. The sources checked for this book do not say how the instalment regime treats tax that is then deferred under a Sch 3ZB plan; a tax function would agree the position with HMRC as part of the s 109B arrangements.

Look at where the tax comes from. Only £325,000 relates to the ordinary trading profit and the balancing charge; £525,000 is tax on value never turned into cash. The stock is still on the shelves, the customer list is still in use, and the shares are unsold. That cash problem is the reason payment plans exist.

## Settling up before you go.

A company that has left the UK is hard to chase: its assets, directors and bank accounts may all be abroad. So TMA 1970 ss 109B–109F require a migrating company to settle its affairs **before** it leaves.

**Conditions A–D (s 109B), all to be met before the company ceases to be UK resident:**

| Condition | Requirement |
|---|---|
| A | Notice to HMRC of the company's intention to cease to be UK resident |
| B | The notice specifies the **migration time** |
| C | A **statement** of the tax payable for periods beginning before the migration time, with details of the **arrangements proposed for securing payment** (which may include a proposed CT exit charge payment plan) |
| D | Arrangements for securing payment **made and approved** by HMRC (disapplied to the extent payment is secured by a Sch 3ZB plan) |

Disputes over the amount in the statement go to the tribunal, whose decision is final; an approval is **void** if information was not fully disclosed.

**"Tax" (s 109F)** includes CT, PAYE and CIS deductions the company should have made, income tax deductible under ITA 2007 ss 946 and 966, and amounts under s 77C, with interest on all of them.

**Penalties and recovery:**

| Provision | Effect |
|---|---|
| s 109C | Company migrating without meeting A–D: penalty up to the **unpaid tax** for pre-migration periods |
| s 109D | Any person who knowingly takes part (directors; controlling companies and their directors) liable to a penalty of the same maximum; directors presumed party unless they show otherwise; professional advisers acting only as advisers excluded |
| s 109E | Tax unpaid **6 months** after it is payable (under the plan, if any): HMRC may, within a **3-year** time limit, serve notice on companies in the migrating company's **51% group** in the 12 months before migration and on **controlling directors**, who must pay within **30 days**; they may recover from the migrating company; the payment is not deductible |

So migration is a group event: TPLC and TEL would be first in line if TIL failed to pay.

> |---|---|
> | 1 March GY4 | s 109B notice: intention to cease UK residence; migration time the end of 30 June GY4; statement of tax for periods to that date; proposed security: a Sch 3ZB plan for the qualifying CT, and the rest paid on the ordinary timetable with a TPLC guarantee for anything outstanding |
> | May GY4 | HMRC approves the arrangements (condition D) |

## Paying over six years.

**TMA 1970 Sch 3ZB** (inserted by FA 2013 Sch 49; amended by FA 2019 Sch 8 Part 1 for APs ending on or after 1 January 2020).

| Feature | Rule | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Eligible company | Has freedom of establishment under **TFEU Article 49** or **EEA Agreement Article 31**; ceases to be UK resident and becomes resident in a **relevant state** (an EU member state, or an EEA state with recovery assistance equivalent to Directive 2010/24/EU); carries on business there; not treaty-resident outside it | para 1 |
| Qualifying tax | **CT1 − CT2**: CT for the migration AP less CT computed ignoring the exit charge provisions (TCGA s 185; CTA 2009 s 162 via s 41(2)(b); s 333; s 609; s 859). Capital allowances are not on the list, so tax on a balancing charge is outside | para 2 |
| Application | Within **9 months** after the end of the migration AP, with the required details; security may be required where there is a serious risk to collection; void for non-disclosure | paras 1(4), 8 |
| Payment | **Six equal instalments**: the first on the day after the end of the 9 months following the migration AP; the others on the next **5 anniversaries** | para 11 |
| Interest | Runs as if there were no plan; late payment penalties only for missing plan dates | — |
| Acceleration | Whole balance due at the next instalment date on insolvency, administration, liquidation (or foreign equivalents), ceasing to be resident in a relevant state, or 12 months' non-payment; tax on a particular asset accelerated on its disposal (partly on a part disposal) | paras 12–14 |
| Realisation method | Removed for APs ending on or after 1 January 2020 (FA 2019 Sch 8 para 6(6)); CTM34133–CTM34134 still describe it and are out of date | — |

**Who can use it after Brexit?** **HMRC's view** (CTM34132) is that eligible companies are those **incorporated in an EU or EEA member state** that become resident in another such state. A UK-incorporated company no longer has Article 49 TFEU rights, so in practice it cannot use a plan (the book's reading of para 1 with CTM34132). The UK's own companies are shut out of a relief that still exists in UK law. legislation.gov.uk shows unapplied effects on Sch 3ZB; check the current text before relying on the detail.

> **Worked example 22.4: TIL's payment plan (invented)**
>
> TIL is incorporated in Ireland (an EU member state) and becomes resident in Ireland, where it carries on its business: an eligible company.
>
> | £ | |
> |---|---|
> | CT1 | 850,000 |
> | CT2 | (325,000) |
> | **Qualifying CT** | **525,000** |
> | Six equal instalments (525,000 ÷ 6) | **87,500** each |
> | Due dates | 1 April GY5, GY6, GY7, GY8, GY9 and GY10 |
> | Outside the plan: CT on the balancing charge (100,000 × 25%) | 25,000 |
>
> Interest runs on each instalment as if there were no plan. The plan defers the cash, not the cost of money.

> **Exam lens: notice, security and payment plans.**
> - **Grade:** within migration **2 (non-core)**.
> - **N25 Q3** rewarded: notice before migration (conditions A–D), the statement and security, penalties on company and directors, recovery from group companies, and the plan (eligibility, CT1 − CT2, 9 months, six equal annual instalments, interest, acceleration). The examiners found administration and plan detail weak.
> - **Traps:** treating the plan as a postponement of the charge (it defers payment; interest runs); applying it to a UK-incorporated company or a migration outside the EU/EEA; putting capital allowances balancing charges into CT1 − CT2; describing the realisation method; forgetting personal exposure of directors.

## After the move, and what the examiner looks for.

From 1 July GY4 TIL is a non-resident company. A new AP begins at once, because it remains within the charge to CT, but only on its UK property business (the warehouse rents) and, in time, on the warehouse gain including the postponed £800,000 (CTA 2009 s 5; TCGA s 2B; chapter 23).

| Regime | Effect of migration | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Group relief | Without a UK PE, TIL can no longer surrender or claim | 15 |
| Gains group | TIL stays a member (non-resident companies count); no gain, no loss transfers only for assets within the UK charge | 17 |
| Transfer pricing | The £20m interest-free loan from TPLC no longer meets the UK-to-UK exemption (TIOPA 2010 s 164A): full Part 4 | 2, 27 |
| CFC rules | TIL becomes a CFC | 26 |
| SAO | No change (never in: Irish-incorporated) | 4 |

> **Going further: the tax function's migration project.**
> 1. **Months ahead:** test whether control will really move; rebuild the board; document the decision-making protocol.
> 2. **Valuation:** value every asset at the migration time (stock, intangibles, shares, plant, land, loans, derivatives); market values drive every exit.
> 3. **Model:** CT1, CT2, the plan, interest, and whether to elect out of s 187B within 2 years.
> 4. **Notice:** give s 109B notice, statement and proposals early enough for approval before the migration time; disclose fully (approval is void otherwise).
> 5. **Exposure:** brief directors on s 109D and the group on s 109E.
> 6. **Timing:** migrating when values are low reduces the exit charge; losses or reliefs available in the migration AP can absorb it.
> 7. **After:** register the new AP; set up transfer pricing documentation and the CFC review; update the group relief and gains group maps.
>
> **Ethics.** Residence can be argued either way on borderline facts. An adviser should not help a board "paper" a move of control that has not happened (*Development Securities*), and should make sure the s 109B statement discloses fully.

**How the examiner tests it.** Residence questions (N23 Q1; N24 Q6) reward applying incorporation, CMC, the supplied tie-breaker and s 18 to the facts at each date. Migration questions (N25 Q3) reward a full exit schedule, the land postponement and detailed administration. The trap runs both ways: in **M26 Q5** (a UK company's first overseas construction contract) many candidates wrongly discussed residence or migration of the UK company in a question about a foreign PE, and earned nothing for it. Answer the requirement asked.

## What to take away.

- **Residence:** UK incorporation (CTA 2009 s 14) or CMC in the UK. CMC is the highest level of control, usually the board acting as a board; a question of fact.
- **Boards:** influenced but deciding keeps control (*Wood v Holden* [2006]); bypassed loses it (*Unit Construction* [1960]); rubber-stamping uncommercial decisions really taken by the parent loses it (*Development Securities* [2020]).
- **Treaties:** a UK company cannot leave by moving its board; it is non-resident only if a treaty tie-breaker makes it resident elsewhere (s 18). Older treaties: POEM; MLI Article 4 (including UK–Ireland, from 1 April 2020 for UK CT): competent authority agreement. DRICs cannot surrender group relief or receive no gain, no loss transfers.
- **Exit:** the AP ends; TCGA s 185 deems disposal and reacquisition at market value of all assets except UK PE assets; s 187 postponement repealed (migrations from 1 January 2020); UK land postponed automatically under s 187B unless the company elects out within 2 years; stock (ss 41, 162), plant (balancing adjustments), intangibles (s 859), loans (s 333) and derivatives (s 609) all exit at market or fair value.
- **Before leaving:** notice, migration time, statement and approved security (TMA s 109B); penalties up to the unpaid tax (ss 109C–109D); recovery from 51% group companies and controlling directors (s 109E).
- **Plans:** an eligible company moving to an EU/EEA state pays CT1 − CT2 in six equal annual instalments with interest, applying within 9 months; in practice not available to UK-incorporated companies.
- **TIL:** TTP £3.4m; CT £850,000; plan £525,000 = 6 × £87,500 from 1 April GY5; warehouse gain £800,000 postponed.

So where does a company live? Where its mind is, unless it was born here. And how does it leave? Only by paying for what it takes with it.

TIL now has a UK foothold as a non-resident. Chapter 23, on inbound companies, permanent establishments and withholding, asks what the UK taxes when a foreign company does business here.

# Chapter ten: Research and development.

*In our invented case*, it is a grey morning in early April of GY2, and Graham Pike, the finance director of Calder Valve Engineering Ltd (CVE), is reading a checklist from the group tax team. Calder is the invented valve maker from *The Living Law* (TKS), now owned by our invented group, Tarnmoor. Its first year inside the group has just ended on 31 March GY2. Calder has spent £2.0m developing a valve that can sense its own wear. Item four on the checklist asks a simple question: has Calder made a research and development claim in the last three years? Graham checks the files. Calder has never claimed at all.

That one fact sets a clock running. If Calder does not tell HMRC that it intends to claim within six months of the end of that period of account, the claim is invalid. Not reduced: invalid. A credit worth £400,000 would be lost for want of a short online form.

This chapter is about the relief that pays companies to invent, and the machinery around it, which is now as much about proof and procedure as about rates. It sits in Part Two (measuring profit) because the relief works inside the trading computation. All three syllabus rows it covers are **core (grade 1)**: the research and development expenditure credit, R&D intensive companies, and R&D allowances. *The Living Law*, chapter 21, introduced the merged scheme and ERIS at TKS level. Here we go deeper: the payment steps, group surrender, the size tests that catch a start-up the day a large group buys it, the overseas restriction, and the claim notification trap that Graham has just found.

The law is **FY2026** law under FA 2026. The main rules are in **CTA 2009 Part 13** (Chapter 1A, the merged RDEC, ss 1042A ff; Chapter 2, ERIS, ss 1044 ff; and the general provisions later in the Part) and the allowances in **CAA 2001 Part 6** (ss 437–451).

## Why the state pays for ideas.

When a company discovers something, it cannot keep all of the value. Competitors learn from its products, its engineers move on, and other firms build on what it has shown is possible. Economists call this a **positive externality**: the company bears the whole cost but captures only part of the benefit, so left alone it under-invests.

GOV.UK's guidance on the merged scheme gives the policy aim as "to support and incentivise R&D, helping overcome a market failure (positive externality) which causes underinvestment in R&D". The relief is a subsidy delivered through the tax system, and a large one. HMRC's *Research and Development Tax Credits Statistics* (September 2026) give a **provisional £8.2bn** of relief claimed for 2024–25, on about **40,325 claims**, down about **17%** in a year, driven by a fall of about 19% in SME claims (large company claims rose about 4%).

**Does it work?** An HMRC working paper (2015) estimated that each £1 of tax forgone stimulated **£1.53 to £2.35** of extra R&D spending. HMRC's evaluation of RDEC (published November 2020) put the figure for that scheme at **£2.40 to £2.70** per £1 of relief. Both count only extra spending by existing claimants (the "intensive margin"), not wider spillovers.

**The other side is abuse.** During the years of easy claims, a market grew up of advisers who promised refunds for routine work and took a percentage. HMRC revised its estimate of error and fraud for 2020–21 to **16.7%** of the cost of the reliefs (about **£1.13bn**), with **24.4%** in the old SME scheme. Later HMRC publications give lower figures for later years on different bases (for example 9.9% for 2022–23 in the HMRC Annual Report and Accounts 2024–25), which do not fully reconcile. Those numbers explain almost every procedural rule in this chapter: advance notification, the additional information form, the overseas restriction and the PAYE cap.

**The debate (L7).** One side says R&D relief is among the most effective things the tax system does, and that complexity and compliance burdens deter the honest small firms who need it most. The other says a relief paid in cash to loss-making companies will attract fraud unless it is fenced, and that the fences are the price of keeping the relief. The law today reflects the second view tempered by the first. As a larger-company adviser you will meet both the group that under-claims because nobody joined up the engineers and the tax team, and the boutique adviser who offers to find a refund that is not there.

## What counts as research and development.

The tax definition starts from GAAP (the definition is aligned with ITA 2007 s 1006 and the regulations made under it) and is shaped by the government's *Guidelines on the Meaning of Research and Development for Tax Purposes*, which the definition adopts. The heart of the test is that a project **seeks an advance in science or technology** through the **resolution of scientific or technological uncertainty**. The Guidelines ask whether a **competent professional** in the field would regard the advance as one, rather than as routine. Work that applies known techniques, however clever or valuable commercially, is not R&D for tax.

**The cases.** Both recent decisions are First-tier Tribunal decisions and bind no one:

- *Get Onbord Ltd v HMRC* [2024] UKFTT 617 (TC): an AI tool for "know your client" checks. HMRC refused the claim, saying the project did not advance overall knowledge or capability. The **taxpayer won**. The tribunal accepted the company's evidence and said that once a taxpayer has shown enough to suggest an overall advance, HMRC must produce some material to show the advance was only routine (a point about the evidential burden, as reported by ICAEW).
- *Tills Plus Ltd v HMRC* [2024] UKFTT 614 (TC): a software project found to combine existing technology and products rather than to develop something new. The tribunal found no evidence of an advance achieved by resolving scientific or technological uncertainty, and **HMRC won**. Commentators noted that an expert report for the company lacked evidence and reasoning.

The lesson: the test is the same, and **evidence decides it**. A claim stands or falls on contemporaneous records showing what was uncertain, why a competent engineer could not look the answer up, and what the team tried.

**Project Ashlar (invented).** Calder's smart process valve carries sensors inside the valve body to measure vibration and seal wear, so that a refinery can replace a valve before it fails. Nobody knew whether sensors and wiring could survive the pressure and heat, or whether a signal could pass reliably through thick steel. Dan Hartley (TKS; invented), a chartered engineer, spends **60%** of his time on Ashlar in Calder's first group year (AP 1 April GY1 to 31 March GY2). His design notes and failed prototypes are exactly the evidence a tribunal looks for.

**Misconception (minor):** R&D need not happen in a laboratory. A workshop where a team cracks open a failed prototype qualifies if the project seeks a genuine advance; the shiniest laboratory does not qualify for routine testing of a known product.

## The merged expenditure credit.

For many years the UK ran two schemes: an enhanced deduction (with a payable credit for loss makers) for SMEs, and an above-the-line expenditure credit (RDEC) for larger companies. At the **Autumn Statement of 22 November 2023** the government announced a merger. **FA 2024 Sch 1** enacted the merged scheme and a separate enhanced relief for loss-making R&D-intensive SMEs, from an appointed day set by regulations (SI 2024/286): **accounting periods beginning on or after 1 April 2024**.

| Feature | Merged scheme RDEC (CTA 2009 Part 13 Ch 1A) |
|---|---|
| Rate | **20%** of qualifying expenditure (s 1042G); 49% for ring fence trades (outside LCG) |
| Who | Any trading company, any size (including SMEs that are not R&D intensive) |
| Tax treatment | Taxable trading receipt of the AP (above the line) |
| Net value | 20% × (1 − 25%) = **15%** at the main rate; 20% × (1 − 19%) = **16.2%** at the small profits rate |
| Use | Seven payment steps (ss 1042I ff): own CT first, cash last |

Three features set the credit apart from the old enhanced deduction.

**First, the credit is taxable.** It is a trading receipt of the period. The company deducts its R&D spending in full as normal trading expenses and then adds the credit back as income. That is why it appears above the line, like a grant, and why it increases accounting profit before tax.

**Second, the net value is lower than the headline.** For a company paying 25%, a 20% credit is worth 20% less a quarter of itself: 15% of the spending. For a company taxed at 19%, the net value is 16.2%. On £1m of qualifying expenditure that is £150,000 or £162,000. Compare the ERIS ceiling below (£269,700 on £1m), which is why the size tests matter so much to a start-up.

**Third, the credit is used through fixed payment steps** that pay the company's own CT first and produce cash only at the end. Those steps are where the examiners have caught candidates, so we take them slowly below.

> **Worked example 10.1: Calder's first claim (AP 1 April GY1 to 31 March GY2) (invented)**
>
> | £ | |
> |---|---:|
> | Tax-EBITDA (before CAs and before RDEC) | 4,400,000 |
> | Less capital allowances (incl. R&D allowance £500,000; WE 10.4) | (1,800,000) |
> | Adjusted trading profit before RDEC (after deducting £2,000,000 R&D costs as normal expenses) | 2,600,000 |
> | Add RDEC: 20% × £2,000,000 (taxable trading receipt) | 400,000 |
> | **Taxable total profits** | **3,000,000** |
> | CT at 25% | 750,000 |
> | Step 1: RDEC discharges CT of the period | (400,000) |
> | **CT payable** | **350,000** |
>
> Net benefit to the group: £400,000 − (25% × £400,000) = **£300,000**, i.e. 15% of qualifying expenditure. Calder is large, not very large, in this AP (its associates are counted on 31 March GY1, when it had none: chapter 3), so it pays four instalments of £187,500 in months 7, 10, 13 and 16; the credit does not reduce them (CIRD89870).

## Qualifying expenditure.

The credit is 20% of qualifying expenditure, and the law lists the categories (CTA 2009 ss 1123 ff as applied by Ch 1A). A cost outside them does not count, however closely it relates to the project.

| Category | Rule | Calder AP to 31 March GY2 (£) |
|---|---|---:|
| Staffing costs | Salaries, employer's NIC and employer pension contributions of staff directly and actively engaged in R&D; partly engaged staff apportioned on a just and reasonable basis | Dan Hartley: 60% × £120,000 = **72,000**; other engineers and technicians 1,428,000; total **1,500,000** |
| Externally provided workers (EPWs) | Unconnected provider: **65%** of the payment; connected (or electing) providers: by reference to the provider's own relevant costs | — |
| Contractor payments (contracted-out R&D) | Unconnected contractor: **65%** of the relevant (UK or s 1138A) portion (CIRD138000); connected or jointly elected (s 1135): different measure | Brackenwell (unconnected throughout the AP): 65% × £400,000 = **260,000** |
| Consumable items | Materials used up or transformed in the R&D (prototype castings, seals, sensors) | **180,000** |
| Software, data licences and cloud computing | So far as used directly in the R&D (data and cloud from APs beginning on or after 1 April 2023) | **60,000** |
| **Qualifying expenditure** | | **2,000,000** |
| **RDEC at 20%** | | **400,000** |

Dan Hartley's employment costs (salary, employer's NIC and employer pension contributions) are about £120,000 a year; 60% of his time goes on Ashlar, so £72,000 qualifies. Calder subcontracted part of the sensor design to Brackenwell Sensors Ltd, an invented start-up, for £400,000. Brackenwell was unconnected throughout Calder's AP (Tarnmoor bought it on 1 July GY2, after the AP ended), so 65% qualifies. Had Calder paid Brackenwell after 1 July GY2, the two would have been connected and the connected-party measure, by reference to Brackenwell's own relevant costs, would have applied instead.

The categories are also a test of judgement. A cost is either in a category or out. The adviser's job is to resist stretching a category to cover something that feels like research but is not, and to make sure genuine costs, such as cloud processing of test data, are not left out because nobody told the tax team they existed.

**Payment condition.** Every category has one: nothing can be claimed until it has been paid (CIRD132000).

**What is out.** Repairs to the test bay (the N25 Q1 examiners criticised candidates who included repairs); rent, rates and general overheads; **capital expenditure** (Calder's £500,000 test rig gets an R&D allowance instead: below); overseas contractor costs outside s 1138A.

## Who claims, and research done abroad.

**Contracted-out R&D.** Under the merged scheme the company that **decides to undertake or initiate** the R&D claims it (CIRD161000). A contractor cannot claim for R&D contracted to it where the customer intended or contemplated the R&D when the contract was made. So Calder claims 65% of what it paid Brackenwell for the Ashlar sensor design, and Brackenwell cannot claim for that work. This ends the old problem of both parties claiming the same project under different schemes.

**The overseas restriction (CTA 2009 s 1138A).** For APs beginning on or after 1 April 2024, contractor payments for R&D undertaken **outside the UK**, and EPW costs for workers not subject to UK PAYE and NIC, are excluded from **both** schemes unless:

1. conditions necessary for the R&D (geographical, environmental, social, legal or regulatory) are **not present in the UK**;
2. they **are present** where the R&D is undertaken; and
3. it would be **wholly unreasonable** to replicate them in the UK.

The **cost and availability of workers are disregarded** (s 1138A). The unconnected-contractor 65% applies only to the UK (or qualifying overseas) portion: HMRC's draft guidance illustrates a contractor with half its R&D in the UK giving 50% × 65% = **32.5%** of the payment.

*For example*, a payment of £200,000 to an unconnected contractor that performs half of the work in the UK and half abroad (with no s 1138A exception) gives qualifying expenditure of 50% × 65% × £200,000 = **£65,000**.

*For this example only*, suppose Calder paid a laboratory in Vallaria (an invented country) to run the pressure tests because it was cheaper: excluded, because cheaper labour is exactly what the law disregards. If the tests needed a natural geological condition found only in Vallaria, the exception might apply. M25 Q2 tested overseas subcontracting.

**FA 2026 s 34.** Northern Ireland companies claiming ERIS may escape the overseas restriction (a Windsor Framework relaxation, alongside the de minimis State aid limit in s 1112J). FA 2026 s 34 amends s 1138A(1)(b) to begin "for the purposes of relief under Chapter 2", confirming that the relaxation is **ERIS-only**, with effect for claims made on or after **30 October 2024** (retrospective).

## Seven steps to cash.

The credit is not simply paid out. It flows through seven steps (CTA 2009 ss 1042I ff; CIRD112100). Picture a waterfall: the credit first fills the company's own tax pool, loses a notional-tax splash, meets a weir set by payroll taxes, and only then flows to other periods, group companies, other taxes and finally the bank.

**Before step 1:** any step 2 amount brought forward from an earlier AP discharges the current AP's CT.

| Step | What happens | Key points |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Discharge the company's CT liability for the AP of the claim | Calder stops here: £400,000 < £750,000 |
| 2 | **Notional tax deduction** on any amount left | Rate: **25%** if profits are chargeable at the main rate (**including marginal relief companies**); otherwise **19%** (small profits and loss-making companies). The deducted "step 2 amount" is **not lost**: it may be **surrendered** to a group member or **carried forward** to discharge the company's CT of later periods (s 1042L(3)) |
| 3 | **PAYE cap** | Payable amount limited to **£20,000 + 300%** of the company's relevant PAYE and NIC liabilities for the AP (s 1112B(2); CIRD140000); excess carried forward as credit of the next AP. **Exempt** (HMRC guidance) where the company's staff create or manage IP that it can exploit and connected-party EPW and subcontractor spend is no more than **15%** of qualifying expenditure |
| 4 | Discharge CT of any other AP | |
| 5 | **Surrender** to another group member | Used by the recipient to discharge its CT |
| 6 | Discharge other liabilities to HMRC (VAT, PAYE, contract settlements) | |
| 7 | **Payment** | Conditions apply (for example the company must be a going concern, and HMRC need not pay while the return is under enquiry: s 1112H as summarised in CIRD112100) |

**Step by step, in words.** Step 1 uses the credit against the company's own CT for the period of the claim. Calder stops here, because its £400,000 credit is less than its £750,000 liability. Only if something is left does step 2 apply: the remainder is reduced by notional tax at 25% (main rate profits, including marginal relief companies) or 19% (everyone else, including loss makers). The lower rate for loss makers is the main design concession in the merged scheme (CIRD112100). Step 3 is the payroll weir: cash cannot exceed £20,000 plus three times the company's relevant PAYE and NIC, which targets shell companies with no real payroll, where much of the fraud sat. Anything above the cap is not lost but carried forward as credit of the next AP. Steps 4 to 6 use what remains against the company's CT for other periods, then surrender it to group members, then set it against other HMRC liabilities. Only at step 7 is cash paid.

> **Worked example 10.5: all seven steps (labelled hypothetical, not part of the Tarnmoor story)**
>
> *Suppose* a standalone UK trading company with no associated companies and no group has qualifying expenditure of £1,500,000, adjusted trading profit before RDEC of £100,000, and relevant PAYE and NIC of £40,000. The PAYE cap exemption does not apply.
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---:|
> | RDEC: 20% × £1,500,000 | 300,000 |
> | TTP: £100,000 + £300,000 | 400,000 |
> | CT at 25% (TTP above £250,000) | 100,000 |
> | Step 1: discharge CT of the AP | (100,000) |
> | Remaining | 200,000 |
> | Step 2: notional tax at 25% (profits chargeable at the main rate) = step 2 amount, carried forward against future CT | (50,000) |
> | Remaining | 150,000 |
> | Step 3: PAYE cap £20,000 + 300% × £40,000 = £140,000; excess carried forward as credit of the next AP | (10,000) |
> | Steps 4 to 6: no other CT, no group, no other liabilities | — |
> | **Step 7: payable** | **140,000** |
>
> Had TTP fallen in the marginal relief band (£50,001 to £250,000 with no associates), step 2 would still use **25%**: marginal relief companies are "chargeable at the main rate" for this purpose (CIRD112100).

**Why notional tax?** The credit is taxable. A loss maker pays no tax on it this year, but the credit reduces its loss and so its future relief. Step 2 stops it taking cash today for the tax element.

**Mnemonic:** own tax, notional tax, payroll cap, other periods, group, other taxes, cash.

> **Exam lens: RDEC (grade 1 core)**
> - **Past appearances:** M24 Q4 (10 marks: linked enterprises and the RDEC steps; "poor"; candidates confused step 1, the CT set-off, with step 2, the net value restriction); M25 Q2 (merged scheme inside a CT computation with overseas subcontracting and cloud/software costs; marked leniently because of the rule change); N25 Q1 (merged scheme inside a full computation; repairs wrongly included).
> - **Style:** usually embedded in a 15–20 mark computation; "Calculate, with explanations". 0.5–1 mark per point: the credit as a trading receipt, the 20% rate, each excluded cost, the step 1 set-off, the step 2 rate with its reason.
> - **Traps:** treating RDEC as a deduction rather than a taxable credit; 25% versus 19% notional tax (marginal relief companies use 25%); including capital items, repairs or overseas contractor costs; 65% for unconnected contractors and EPWs; forgetting claim notification for a first claim.
> - **Layout:** add the RDEC in the CT pro forma as trading income; deduct it from the CT liability below the line; show steps in order.

## Surrender inside the group.

Step 5 is where a large group finds value. A loss-making subsidiary can take cash, net of notional tax and subject to the PAYE cap; or it can surrender the credit to a taxpaying group company, which uses it to pay its own CT. Inside a group with a large tax bill, surrender turns a deferred, restricted asset into an immediate saving, and the step 2 amount, which would otherwise sit idle in a company that will make losses for years, can travel too.

*In our invented case*, Tom Hesketh, the group head of tax, compares the options for Brackenwell's GY2 credit. Cash would bring in £486,000 now, with the £114,000 step 2 amount carried forward against tax Brackenwell may not pay for years. Surrender to Tarnmoor Engineering, whose GY2 CT is £2,226,250, uses the whole £600,000 at once. He surrenders both amounts. The surrendered credit discharges TEL's liability when Brackenwell's claim is made; TEL's instalments are still based on its full liability (our reading of HMRC's guidance at CIRD89870, which deals with a company's own credit).

> **Worked example 10.2: Brackenwell's merged credit, GY2 (year to 31 December GY2) (invented)**
>
> Tarnmoor plc buys Brackenwell Sensors Ltd (BSL) on **1 July GY2**. Its founder Dr Asha Varma stays as CTO. BSL is linked to Tarnmoor at the end of GY2, so (HMRC's view: next section) it is large for the **whole** year and claims the merged RDEC on qualifying expenditure of **£3,000,000**.
>
> | Step | £ |
> |---|---:|
> | RDEC: 20% × £3,000,000 | 600,000 |
> | Step 1: BSL's own CT for GY2 (loss-making) | nil |
> | Step 2: notional tax at **19%** (loss maker) | (114,000) |
> | Remaining after step 2 | **486,000** |
> | Step 3: PAYE cap £20,000 + 300% × £900,000 relevant PAYE/NIC = £2,720,000: not restrictive | — |
> | Step 4: no other CT liabilities | — |
> | Step 5: surrendered to Tarnmoor Engineering Ltd (TEL) | (486,000) |
> | Step 2 amount also surrendered to TEL (s 1042L) | 114,000 |
> | **Total credit discharging TEL's GY2 CT of £2,226,250** | **600,000** |
>
> **Group economics:** the credit is taxable in BSL, so BSL's loss (and the group relief it surrenders to TEL) is £600,000 smaller, costing 25% × £600,000 = £150,000. Net benefit **£450,000** = 15% of £3,000,000. The steps move cash; the rate decides value.
>
> **Payment for the surrender.** TEL pays BSL £600,000 under a written agreement. Under **CTA 2009 s 1042N(5)–(6)** (inserted by **FA 2026 s 31**, for payments made on or after **26 November 2025**), a payment for surrendered RDEC that does not exceed the credit surrendered is **ignored** in computing both companies' profits and is **not a distribution**. Any excess is outside the protection.

## Research and development intensive companies.

ERIS (enhanced R&D intensive support, CTA 2009 Part 13 Ch 2) is for one kind of company only: a **loss-making R&D-intensive SME**.

**Size (CIRD91400–92000).** The R&D SME definition in CTA 2009 Part 13 is the European Commission Recommendation 2003/361/EC with the ceilings **doubled**:

| Test | Recommendation ceiling | R&D relief ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| Staff headcount | under 250 | **under 500** |
| Turnover | €50m | **€100m** |
| Balance sheet total | €43m | **€86m** |

Staff **and** either the turnover or the balance sheet test. **Linked enterprises** (broadly control, usually more than 50% of voting rights) are aggregated **in full**; **partner enterprises** (broadly 25% to 50%) are aggregated **in proportion** (CIRD91500, CIRD91700). Sterling figures are converted to euros at the balance sheet date rate. The 2026 CTA tax tables reproduce the SME definition, including notes for R&D (check the 2027 tables).

**Intensity.** Relevant R&D expenditure at least **30%** of total relevant expenditure, with connected companies' figures aggregated; **one year's grace** if the test was met in the previous 12-month AP with a valid claim.

**The relief.**
- Extra deduction **86%** of qualifying expenditure (186% in total).
- **Surrenderable loss** = the lower of the **unrelieved trading loss** (after current-period sideways relief and group relief; losses brought forward or carried back ignored) and **186% of the related qualifying expenditure** (CIRD122000).
- Payable credit **14.5%** of the loss surrendered; **not taxable**; the loss surrendered is extinguished.
- Maximum value 186% × 14.5% = **26.97%** of qualifying expenditure in cash.
- The **PAYE cap** and its exemption apply. NI companies: extra relief limited to de minimis State aid (s 1112J, substituted by FA 2025 s 29).

The two reliefs differ in kind, not just in rate. RDEC is a taxable credit that any trading company can use. ERIS is an enhanced deduction that creates or increases a loss, part of which can then be swapped for a non-taxable cash credit. A company that surrenders its loss for ERIS cash gives up that loss for carry-forward or group relief; a company with taxable profits gets the extra deduction but no cash. ERIS is therefore aimed at one profile: the young, cash-hungry, loss-making developer whose research is most of what it does, which is exactly what Brackenwell was before the acquisition.

> **Worked example 10.3: Brackenwell before acquisition (year to 31 December GY1) (invented)**
>
> BSL: independent; 45 staff; owned by its founders and venture investors (no partner or linked enterprise on the invented facts).
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---:|
> | Intensity: £3,500,000 ÷ £5,000,000 | **70%** (≥ 30%) |
> | Trading loss before enhancement (after deducting £3,500,000 qualifying expenditure) | (4,200,000) |
> | Extra deduction: 86% × £3,500,000 | (3,010,000) |
> | **Trading loss after enhancement** | **(7,210,000)** |
> | Cap: 186% × £3,500,000 | 6,510,000 |
> | Unrelieved loss (no other profits, no group) | 7,210,000 |
> | **Surrenderable loss (lower)** | **6,510,000** |
> | **Payable credit: 14.5% × £6,510,000** | **943,950** |
> | PAYE cap: £20,000 + 300% × £900,000 = £2,720,000: not restrictive | — |
> | **Loss carried forward** (£7,210,000 − £6,510,000) | **700,000** |
>
> Effective rate £943,950 ÷ £3,500,000 = 26.97%. The pending credit is a deal point: the SPA should say whether the price includes it and who bears any HMRC reduction (chapter 20).

## Linked enterprises and the cliff edge of acquisition.

**The misconception:** "a subsidiary of a large group can claim the SME-style intensive relief if it is small itself". It cannot. Size is tested across **linked enterprises**. The moment Tarnmoor owns BSL, the group's staff (about 6,000) and revenue (over £1bn) are added to BSL's: BSL is no longer an SME and can claim only the merged RDEC. M24 Q4 turned on exactly this (a parent and its R&D subsidiary were linked; most candidates missed it).

**Timing (HMRC's view).** The Recommendation normally lets a company keep its status until it crosses the thresholds in **two consecutive** years. **HMRC's view** (CIRD92000) is that the cushion does not apply on a **takeover** by a large group: the company is treated as **large for the whole AP** in which it is acquired, including the months before completion. Hence BSL's GY2 claim is a merged RDEC claim on all £3.0m, including January to June.

This is HMRC guidance, not statute, but it is how claims are processed. Advisers sometimes suggest ending the target's AP at completion so that the pre-acquisition months form a separate AP that can still be tested as an SME. *In our invented case*, nobody thought of it in time: put R&D status on the due diligence list before signing.

**Partner enterprises in reverse.** A small company part-owned (25%–50%) by an investor must include a proportion of the investor's figures. The Recommendation has special rules for some investor types (for example certain venture capital investors); check them in CIRD92800 (text of the Recommendation) on real facts. *In our invented case* BSL's venture investors did not make it a partner or linked enterprise of any larger group.

> **Going further: R&D in the Brackenwell deal (invented)**
> - **Pending credit.** BSL's GY1 ERIS credit of £943,950 related to a period when it was independent. If the claim is made after completion, the cash arrives in a Tarnmoor company; the SPA should say whether the price already reflects it, and who bears the loss if HMRC enquires into the claim and reduces it (an indemnity or a price adjustment: chapter 20).
> - **Status of the acquisition year.** On HMRC's whole-period view, BSL's January to June GY2 spending gets the merged RDEC, not ERIS. Ending BSL's AP on 30 June GY2 was the planning point that was missed.
> - **Payment step 7 conditions.** A target that is not a going concern, or has PAYE or VAT arrears, may find its cash credit delayed; due diligence should check both.
> - **Losses.** The ERIS credit extinguishes the losses surrendered, and the remaining £0.7m joins BSL's other pre-acquisition losses, which chapter 14 shows are restricted after the change in ownership.
> - **Claim history.** HMRC enquiries into past R&D claims are common; a target with aggressive historic claims (or contingent-fee advisers) is a warranty and indemnity issue.

> **Exam lens: ERIS and linked enterprises (grade 1 core in 2026; not examined from 2028)**
> - **Past appearances:** M24 Q4 (linked enterprises; poor).
> - **Traps:** testing the subsidiary alone; forgetting partner enterprises; using the transfer pricing SME thresholds (250 staff, €50m/€43m) instead of the doubled R&D ceilings (500, €100m/€86m); applying 14.5% to the whole enhanced loss rather than the lower of the unrelieved loss and 186% of qualifying expenditure; treating the ERIS credit as taxable.
> - **2028:** the reformed syllabus drops R&D-intensive SMEs from LCG; this does not affect the 2027 sittings.

## Telling HMRC first.

Back to Graham Pike and his clock. Two procedural requirements now guard every claim, and both were responses to the error and fraud figures.

**Claim notification (CTA 2009 s 1142A).** For APs beginning on or after 1 April 2023, a company must notify HMRC in advance if:
- it is claiming for the **first time**; or
- it has not made a claim in the **3 years ending with the last day of the claim notification period** (also where HMRC removed an earlier claim, and ignoring amendment-claims for pre-April 2023 APs made on or after 1 April 2023).

The **claim notification period** runs from the **first day of the period of account** to **6 months after the end of the period of account** (GOV.UK guidance). Without a notification, a claim made after that period ends is **invalid**. Content: SI 2023/813 (Content of Claim Notifications, Additional Information Requirements and Miscellaneous Amendments) Regulations; delivered electronically under HMRC's directions, naming the company officer responsible.

| Company | Period of account | Previous claim in 3 years? | Notification deadline |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calder | 1 April GY1 – 31 March GY2 | No (never claimed: invented assumption) | **30 September GY2** (filed April GY2) |
| BSL | Year to 31 December GY2 | Yes (ERIS for GY1) | Not required |

**Traps.** The window runs from the **period of account**, not the accounting period: where a long period of account is split into two APs, the six months run from the end of the period of account. A company whose last claim was over three years ago is a first-time claimant again.

**Additional information form (FA 1998 Sch 18 para 83EA).** Mandatory for claims made on or after **8 August 2023**, submitted **before** the claim in the return, giving projects, costs by category and people involved. HMRC may **remove** a claim made without complying with a requirement (para 83EB).

**Time limit (FA 1998 Sch 18 para 83E).** A claim may be made, amended or withdrawn within **2 years** from the end of the period of account (periods up to 18 months; 42 months from the start for longer periods). HMRC has a discretion to accept late claims (para 83E(5); SP 5/01).

> **Going further: the group R&D calendar**
> Tom Hesketh's team keeps a register of every company doing R&D: period of account, last claim date, notification deadline, AIF status, return date. Joiners, newly formed subsidiaries and companies that pause R&D are the usual casualties. A missed notification is not a technicality HMRC can overlook: the law says the claim cannot be made.

## Research and development allowances.

Capital expenditure never enters RDEC or ERIS. **CAA 2001 Part 6** gives an **R&D allowance of 100%** (s 441) for capital expenditure on R&D related to the trade, undertaken by the trader or on its behalf (s 437). The meaning of R&D follows the same GAAP-based definition and Guidelines. **Land does not qualify.** The allowance is given for the chargeable period in which the expenditure is incurred (or, if before trading, the period in which the trade starts); the company may claim less than 100%. A later disposal brings in a disposal value and a balancing charge.

> **Worked example 10.4: Calder's test rig (invented)**
>
> | £ | AP to 31 March GY2 |
> |---|---:|
> | Test rig (capital, used for Ashlar R&D): R&D allowance 100% | 500,000 |
> | Plant and machinery allowances (chapter 8) | 1,300,000 |
> | **Calder's capital allowances (WE 10.1)** | **1,800,000** |
>
> The rig is not qualifying expenditure for RDEC.

**Why choose it over full expensing?** For a company, new plant may already get full expensing at 100% (chapter 8). The R&D allowance also covers **buildings** used for R&D, which full expensing never does. Against that, a disposal triggers a balancing charge. N25 Q4 (a new subsidiary) expected R&D allowances; "few mentioned" them. A new company may incur R&D capital spending before it trades: the allowance then falls in the period the trade begins, which affects the timing of tax and instalments.

**Why the allowance exists.** Without it, research buildings and equipment would be relieved slowly or not at all, and the incentive would favour R&D done with people and materials over R&D that needs heavy kit. The allowance gives the whole cost at once, matching the credit's treatment of revenue spending.

> **Exam lens: R&D allowances (grade 1 core)**
> - **Past appearances:** N25 Q4 (CA, SBA and R&D allowances with instalment timing).
> - **Traps:** putting capital R&D spend into RDEC; forgetting the allowance exists; land.

## Where the regimes meet.

Research relief does not live alone, and the examiner likes the joins. The most important for a computation is the corporate interest restriction: the credit does not increase interest capacity, and the allowances (including the R&D allowance) do not reduce it, because tax-EBITDA is computed before both. The most important for the group's reported numbers is the accounts: the RDEC is income above the line and taxed, whereas the ERIS credit is untaxed and usually presented in the tax line. The most important for planning is transfer pricing: R&D functions create the intangibles whose profits chapters 11 and 27 follow.

| Regime | Interaction | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| CIR | RDEC and ERIS deductions excluded from tax-EBITDA (TIOPA 2010 s 407(3)(a), (b)); capital allowances (including R&D allowances) also excluded. Calder's tax-EBITDA £4.4m is before both its allowances and its credit | 28 |
| Accounts and deferred tax | RDEC above the line (operating profit) and taxable; ERIS payable credit not taxable and in practice often shown in the tax charge, affecting the reported ETR. The 100% R&D allowance on Calder's rig against depreciation over its life creates a temporary difference and a DTL that unwinds as the rig is depreciated | 6 |
| Patent Box | Ashlar patents (filed GY2) qualify from GY4; the nexus fraction favours R&D done by the company itself over acquired IP and connected-party R&D | 11 |
| Transfer pricing | Intra-group R&D services priced at arm's length (cost plus: N23 Q6); location of DEMPE functions. Contrast: if BSL does R&D for Calder after 1 July GY2, the FA 2026 UK-to-UK exemption (TIOPA 2010 s 164A) will usually take it out of TP (subject to its conditions: chapter 27), but for RDEC the two are now **connected**, so Calder's payments are measured by the connected-party rule, not 65% | 27 |
| Groups | RDEC surrender (step 2 and step 5) and FA 2026 s 31 payments; separate from loss group relief, though the credit reduces the surrendering company's loss | 15 |

## Going further, the tax function's work.

> **Going further: running R&D relief in a large group**
> 1. **Find the R&D.** Engineers do not think in tax categories, and in a group of Tarnmoor's size R&D happens where the tax team never visits (a pump-seal product team, a controls software group, last year's acquisition). In an HMRC enquiry the questions are about the project: what was uncertain, who resolved it, and how do you know? Build a process that reaches project leads, asks what was uncertain, and records answers when the work happens (time sheets, technical narratives, prototype logs). Dan Hartley's notes are worth more than an adviser's narrative written two years later.
> 2. **The calendar.** Claim notifications, AIFs and returns, company by company (above).
> 3. **Location.** Overseas contractor R&D will usually not qualify (s 1138A); weigh that with the transfer pricing consequences of placing valuable functions abroad.
> 4. **Acquisitions.** Due diligence: has the target claimed correctly? Is its SME status right? Are credits pending? Will the acquisition end ERIS (HMRC's whole-period view)? Consider ending the target's AP at completion.
> 5. **Surrender and payments.** Decide where step 2 and step 5 amounts are used; document an agreement so that payments fall within s 1042N(5)–(6).
> 6. **Ethics.** *In our invented case*, a firm offers to review the group's claims for a percentage of any extra refund. Tom asks it to explain each extra project technically; it cannot; he declines. PCRT (chapter 4) expects an adviser to stand behind every claim; HMRC's compliance work in recent years has targeted vague narratives and contingent-fee claims.

## How the examiner tests it.

R&D has appeared three times in the seven LCG papers from M23 to M26, plus the R&D allowances point in N25 Q4. Expect it to arrive **inside a computation** more often than on its own. The credit is a trading receipt, so add it to the adjusted profit, then use it at step 1 against the tax; show the step 2 rate and why; test size across linked enterprises before choosing the relief; strip out capital items, repairs and overseas contractor costs and give the R&D allowance for capital items; and if the scenario mentions a company that has not claimed before, write the words "claim notification".

> **Exam lens: how to answer**
> - **Grades (2026 grid):** R&D intensive companies **1**; RDEC **1**; R&D allowances **1**. At least 70% of the CT element comes from core material. (Check the 2027 grid; the 2028 grid keeps CTA 2009 Part 13 Chs 1, 1A, 8, 9 at 1 but drops R&D-intensive SMEs.)
> - **Style:** technical prose and computations; no letter format; 0.5–1 mark per point. R&D is usually a few marks inside a 15–20 mark computation; M24 Q4 was the only standalone question (10 marks).
> - **Tax tables:** give the RDEC rate, the ERIS extra deduction and payable credit rates and the SME definition; they do **not** give the step order, the 25%/19% notional tax rule or claim notification.
> - **Method:** (1) test size across linked and partner enterprises; (2) strip out capital items, repairs, overheads and overseas contractor costs; apply 65% to unconnected contractors and EPWs; (3) RDEC = 20%, added as trading income; (4) step 1 against the AP's CT; step 2 at 25% or 19% with the reason; (5) R&D allowances for capital items; (6) mention claim notification where the company has not claimed before.

## What to take away.

R&D relief exists because ideas leak; the state shares the cost. The price was fraud and error on a large scale, and almost every procedural rule is a fence built in response.

- **Merged RDEC** (APs beginning on or after 1 April 2024): **20%** taxable credit; net **15%** at 25% CT, **16.2%** at 19%.
- **Qualifying expenditure:** staffing, EPWs, contractor payments, consumables, software, data and cloud; unconnected contractors and EPWs at **65%**; payment condition; no capital, repairs or overheads; overseas contractor R&D excluded unless s 1138A applies (cost and availability of workers ignored). The **decider** claims.
- **Seven steps:** own CT; notional tax **25%/19%** (step 2 amount surrendered or carried forward); PAYE cap **£20,000 + 300%** unless exempt; other APs; group surrender; other taxes; cash. FA 2026 s 31: payments up to the credit surrendered ignored and not distributions.
- **ERIS:** loss-making SMEs only (**< 500 staff; ≤ €100m turnover or ≤ €86m balance sheet**, linked in full, partners pro rata); intensity **≥ 30%**; **86%** extra deduction; **14.5%** credit on the lower of the unrelieved loss and **186%** of qualifying expenditure; not taxable. A large group's subsidiary cannot claim; HMRC's view: an acquired company is large for the **whole** AP of acquisition.
- **Claim notification** for a first claim or after 3 years: within **6 months** of the end of the period of account, or the claim is invalid; **AIF** for every claim.
- **R&D allowances:** 100% for capital R&D spend (not land).
- *In our invented case*: Calder RDEC £400,000 against CT £750,000, payable £350,000; BSL ERIS credit £943,950 (GY1); BSL merged RDEC £600,000 (GY2), all used against TEL's CT.

Ashlar has produced something valuable, and the law treats valuable knowledge as an asset of its own. The next chapter follows the patents and know-how into the intangibles regime.

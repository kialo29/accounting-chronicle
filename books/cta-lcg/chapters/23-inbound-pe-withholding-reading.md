# Chapter twenty-three: Inbound: non-resident companies, permanent establishments and withholding

On 18 March 2026, Finance Act 2026 received Royal Assent. Buried in its Schedule 7 was a new sentence for the Corporation Tax Act 2010. It concerned the person who works in the UK for a foreign company. Under the old wording, that person created a taxable presence only if they had, and habitually exercised, "authority to do business on behalf of the company". Under the new wording, it is enough that they "habitually conclude contracts, or habitually play the principal role leading to the conclusion of contracts that are routinely concluded without material modification by the company" (CTA 2010 s 1141(1)(b), as substituted by FA 2026 Sch 7).

Read that again slowly, because it is the heart of this chapter. A person who never signs anything can now give a foreign company a permanent establishment (PE) in the UK. The person who closes the deal matters more than the person who holds the pen.

This chapter is about companies that are not resident here but do business here. What does the UK tax them on? When does their activity here become a PE? How are profits attributed to it? Who answers for the tax? And, from the other side of the payment, when must a UK company deduct income tax from interest or royalties it pays to someone abroad? On the LCG grid, "company residence and chargeable profits of non-UK resident companies and concept of permanent establishment/branch", "non-resident and dual resident companies" (gains), CTA 2010 Part 22 (Chs 5–7 here) and "deduction of income tax" are all **core (grade 1)**. The chapter builds on *The Living Law* (TKS), chapter 26, which introduced residence, PEs and the branch.

Statutes: Corporation Tax Act 2009 (CTA 2009), Corporation Tax Act 2010 (CTA 2010), Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act 1992 (TCGA 1992), Taxation (International and Other Provisions) Act 2010 (TIOPA 2010) and Income Tax Act 2007 (ITA 2007) Part 15. We apply the law for **FY2026**, and **2026/27** for income tax points.

## What the UK taxes on a foreign company

A UK-resident company pays corporation tax on its profits wherever they arise. A company resident elsewhere pays only where it has a link to the UK that the law recognises. Think of it as a footprint. The UK taxes a foreign company only where it has left a footprint here, and since 2026 the law also counts footprints made by others walking on its behalf.

**CTA 2009 s 5** lists the footprints. A non-UK resident company is within the charge to CT only if it:

| Head | Since |
|---|---|
| Carries on a trade of dealing in or developing UK land | 5 July 2016 (with CTA 2010 Part 8ZB) |
| Carries on a trade in the UK through a UK PE | long-standing (s 19) |
| Carries on a UK property business | **6 April 2020** (FA 2019 Sch 5) |
| Has other UK property income | 6 April 2020 |
| Loan relationship and derivative profits of that property business (s 5(3A)–(3B)) | 6 April 2020 |

Notice what is missing. A foreign company that sells to UK customers from abroad, with no PE here and no UK land, is not within CT on its trading profits at all. Trading **with** the UK is not trading **in** the UK through a PE.

For the PE head, **s 19 CTA 2009** charges the trading income arising directly or indirectly through or from the PE, plus income from property or rights used by or held by it, attributed under ss 20–24 (the cross-reference changed from "20 to 32" by FA 2026 Sch 7 for chargeable periods beginning on or after 1 January 2026). Land dealing and property income are charged under their own heads (s 19(2A)), not as PE profits.

**Gains** follow a parallel map in **TCGA 1992 s 2B**:

- **UK PE assets (s 2B(3)):** gains on assets situated in the UK, used for the PE's trade or held by it, attributable to the PE under CTA 2009 ss 20 and 24 (wording amended by FA 2026 Sch 7 para 28).
- **UK land (s 2B(4)):** since **6 April 2019**, gains on interests in UK land, whether or not the company has a PE.
- **Indirect disposals (s 2B(4), Sch 1A):** assets deriving **at least 75%** of their (gross) value from UK land ("UK property rich"), where the company has a **substantial indirect interest**. HMRC's manual explains that this means holding **at least 25%**, at some time in the **two years** before the disposal, with connected persons' interests attributed (CG73934, CG73936). There is a trading exemption where the land is used in a qualifying trade.
- **FA 2026 s 40:** each cell of a protected cell company is treated as a separate company for the Sch 1A 75% test, for disposals on or after 26 November 2025.

**In our invented case.** Tarnmoor plc is a listed engineering group; every company, person and number in it is invented for teaching. Two of its companies test the footprint rules in this chapter: **Tarnmoor Vallaria SA (TVS)**, resident in the invented country of Vallaria; and **Tarnmoor Ireland Ltd (TIL)**, which ceases to be UK resident on **30 June GY4** (chapter 22) but still owns a warehouse in England.

## A fixed place of business

**CTA 2010 s 1141(1)** gives two routes to a PE in a territory:

1. **(a) the fixed place limb:** "a fixed place of business there through which the business of the company is wholly or partly carried on"; and
2. **(b) the agent limb** (next section).

**s 1141(2)** lists fixed places: a place of management; a branch; an office; a factory; a workshop; an installation for exploring natural resources; a mine, well or quarry; and **a building site or construction or installation project**.

On the usual reading, three ideas sit inside "fixed place of business": a **place** (which may be modest, even floor space in someone else's premises); **fixed** (a link to a location with some permanence); and the company's **business carried on through it** (usually meaning the place is at the company's disposal and its business is done there). Since FA 2026, s 1140A directs that the PE chapter be read consistently with Article 5 of the OECD Model and its Commentary, so the Commentary's account of these ideas now shapes the UK reading.

**The domestic list sets no time limit for a building site.** Treaties usually do: under Article 5(3) of the OECD Model a building site or construction project is a PE only if it lasts **more than 12 months**. Under the domestic list, whether a short site is "fixed" is a question of degree (the book's reading: s 1141(2) contains no duration test). Where a treaty applies, its time limit protects the foreign company. In **M26 Q5** (20 marks) a construction PE turned on the 12-month test.

> **The subsidiary misconception.** A UK subsidiary is **not** a PE of its foreign parent merely because the parent controls it (HMRC, INTM153060, following the OECD Model). The subsidiary is a separate taxpayer. But a subsidiary **can** be the parent's dependent agent on the facts. Control alone proves nothing; conduct can prove a great deal.

> **And the examiners' complaint.** In **N25 Q2**, some candidates treated a PE as a separate legal entity. It is not. A PE is part of the foreign company: no shares, no directors, no separate legal personality. The law treats it *as if* it were a separate enterprise when attributing profits (s 21 CTA 2009), but that is a measuring hypothesis, not the law of persons.

## The agent who never signs

### Why the agent limb was rewritten

For decades, s 1141(1)(b) asked whether "an agent acting on behalf of the company has and habitually exercises there authority to do business on behalf of the company" (wording to 31 December 2025). In practice that pointed at the power to conclude contracts. Groups learned to live just outside it: a local company (often a group company) found the customer, negotiated the price and agreed the specification, then sent the contract abroad for signature. The foreign company signed; the local company earned a modest fee; the sales profit and the taxing right stayed abroad.

The international response came through the OECD/G20 base erosion and profit shifting (BEPS) project. The **Action 7** report (October 2015), "Preventing the Artificial Avoidance of Permanent Establishment Status", targeted commissionaire arrangements and similar strategies and proposed the "principal role" wording for Article 5(5), with a narrower independent agent exception in Article 5(6) (commentators' summaries; the OECD report was not opened: oecd.org is blocked). The UK's first answer was **unilateral**: the diverted profits tax (DPT) of 2015 had an "avoided PE" limb. DPT is repealed for accounting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2026 (chapter 30). The second answer is the new statutory wording, which brings the dependent agent test inside CT.

### The new test

For **chargeable periods beginning on or after 1 January 2026** (FA 2026 s 49, Sch 7 paras 12, 30), a company has a PE in a territory if:

> a person acting on behalf of the company in the territory **habitually concludes contracts**, or **habitually plays the principal role leading to the conclusion of contracts that are routinely concluded without material modification by the company**,

and the contracts are:

- (i) for the **transfer of ownership of, or the granting of the right to use, property** owned by the company or that it has the right to use; or
- (ii) for the **provision of services** by the company.

| Phrase | What it asks |
|---|---|
| Acting on behalf of the company | Is the person doing the company's business rather than its own? |
| Habitually | Repeatedly, as a pattern, not once |
| Principal role | The role that actually closes the deal: persuading the customer, settling terms, agreeing price |
| Routinely concluded without material modification | Is the foreign company's signature a formality? If head office regularly rewrites deals, no; if it signs what arrives, yes |
| Contract types (i) and (ii) | Sales or licences of the company's property, or its services |

### Commencement (labelled real-calendar examples)

| Foreign company's year end | Period | Test |
|---|---|---|
| 31 December | 1 January 2025 – 31 December 2025 | Old wording |
| 31 December | from 1 January 2026 | **New** wording |
| 30 June | 1 July 2025 – 30 June 2026 | Old wording (began before 1 January 2026) |
| 30 June | from 1 July 2026 | **New** wording |

The same date governs the attribution rewrite (ss 20–21) and the closely related rule. The Tarnmoor story's periods are simply treated as falling under the new law.

### The purpose clause

**CTA 2010 s 1140A** (FA 2026 Sch 7 para 11) requires the PE chapter to be read consistently with Article 5 of the OECD Model Tax Convention and its Commentary as approved by the OECD Council on **18 November 2025**. So when you meet "principal role", the Commentary on that phrase is now part of how the UK statute is read (chapter 2). Whether s 1140A's reference is "as amended from time to time" (as ss 20 CTA 2009 and 164 TIOPA are) was not confirmed. **Proposed, not law:** the draft Finance Bill 2026-27 (13 July 2026) would amend s 1140A further as part of reform of the foreign PE exemption (chapter 2).

## Independent agents and the closely related rule

**s 1142(1) CTA 2010:** a company does not have a PE merely because it carries on business through an **agent of independent status acting in the ordinary course of its business**. A broker, general commission agent or freight forwarder serving many clients is the classic case.

**s 1142(1A)–(1B) (FA 2026 Sch 7 para 13):** a person is **not** of independent status if it is **closely related** to the company **and** acts **exclusively or almost exclusively** on behalf of the company (or it and other companies to which it is closely related). A group sales company working only for its sister companies cannot use the exception, however separate its board and books.

**"Closely related" (s 1143(2CA), replacing former (2D); FA 2026 Sch 7 para 14):** on all the relevant facts and circumstances, one person has control of the other, or both are under the control of the same person or persons; the definition also includes the 50% investment condition linked to TIOPA 2010 s 259ND. In a wholly owned group, every company is closely related to every other.

For agents outside the group, the usual factors still matter: legal and economic independence; whether the agent bears its own entrepreneurial risk; whether it acts for several principals; the degree of detailed instruction and control. A commission agent with fifty clients who loses money when sales fall is independent; a one-client agent whose diary is set by the principal looks dependent, group or no group.

**The common exam pattern.** A UK group company works almost entirely for a foreign group company. It cannot be independent. The only questions are whether it habitually concludes contracts, or plays the principal role leading to contracts routinely concluded without material modification, and whether a treaty prevents the charge.

## Preparatory, auxiliary and the fragmentation trap

**s 1143(1)–(3) CTA 2010:** no PE where the activities, **viewed in relation to the business as a whole**, are **only** of a preparatory or auxiliary character. Examples in the statute: facilities for storage, display or delivery of the company's goods; a stock of goods for storage, display or delivery, or for processing by another person; purchasing goods or collecting information for the company.

Two words carry the weight: "only" and "as a whole". An activity that is an essential and significant part of the company's business is not auxiliary even if it is on the list: a company whose business **is** warehousing cannot call its warehouse auxiliary.

**Anti-fragmentation (s 1143(2A)–(2C), added by FA 2019 s 21).** The exception does not apply where the activities carried on by the company, or by a closely related person, at the same place or at another place in the same territory, are **complementary functions that form part of a cohesive business operation**, and either:

- the **combination** of activities is not merely preparatory or auxiliary; or
- one of the places is already a PE of the company or of the closely related person.

> **Worked example 23.1 (labelled hypothetical, not story): a parts store**
>
> Suppose TVS rented a small unit near Leeds to hold spare valve parts for delivery to UK customers.
>
> | Step | Analysis |
> |---|---|
> | Fixed place? | Yes: a unit at TVS's disposal, through which part of its business (storage and delivery) is carried on |
> | Preparatory or auxiliary alone? | Storage and delivery of the company's goods are listed examples; on their own, auxiliary |
> | Anti-fragmentation? | Suppose TEL (closely related) services and repairs the same valves for the same customers. If store and servicing are complementary functions in one cohesive operation of supplying and maintaining the valves, and the combination is more than preparatory or auxiliary, the exception is lost |
> | Result | A fixed place PE of TVS (subject to any treaty) |

## Domestic law meets the treaty

Most foreign companies trading here are resident in a treaty country. Where the two disagree, **the treaty wins**: TIOPA 2010 s 6 gives treaty arrangements effect, generally in priority to domestic law. Under **Article 7** of a treaty in OECD form, business profits of an enterprise of one state are taxable only there unless it carries on business in the other state through a PE situated there; and the treaty's own **Article 5** decides what a PE is. HMRC's manual: where a treaty applies, its PE article governs whether the UK may tax the business profits (INTM153060, INTM153080; see also INTM264200 on domestic law and treaty law).

The new domestic agent test can be **wider** than the treaty test. A treaty has the principal role wording only if the two states put it there, by a new treaty or through **Article 12 of the Multilateral Instrument (MLI)**. The UK did not take up Article 12: the HMRC synthesised text of the **UK–Ireland** treaty and MLI shows Article 12 does not apply there (**V**), and commentators report that the UK reserved on the whole article (**secondary**). So many UK treaties keep the older wording: an agent creates a PE only if it "has, and habitually exercises, an authority to conclude contracts in the name of the enterprise".

**The three-step routine.**

| Step | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Domestic: fixed place or dependent agent; any exception (independent agent, preparatory or auxiliary, subject to anti-fragmentation)? | CTA 2010 ss 1141–1143 |
| 2 | Treaty: is there a PE under the treaty's Article 5? Use the **supplied extract**, not your memory of the Model | Treaty; TIOPA s 6 |
| 3 | The UK taxes business profits only if **both** answers are yes | Art 7 |

In **N23 Q1** the examiners noted that some candidates ignored the supplied treaty article and used the OECD Model text instead.

Where there is **no treaty**, step 1 is the whole answer. Marrovia (invented) has no UK treaty. A Marrovian company whose UK sister company habitually played the principal role in its sales would have a UK PE, full stop.

## Tarnmoor Vallaria's UK customers

**In our invented case (GY4).** TVS starts selling Ashlar valves directly to large UK water and energy utilities. The valves are made in Vallaria under the patent licence from Calder (chapter 11). TVS has no UK office, warehouse or staff. TEL's key-account engineers, based in Leeds, visit the customers, scope each job, propose the specification and negotiate the price. When the customer agrees, they send a completed contract to TVS's office in Vallaria, where it is signed. In the first six months nobody in Vallaria changes a commercial term. TVS pays TEL a service fee for the engineers' time, priced under the TP rules (chapter 27).

> **Worked example 23.2: Tom Hesketh's PE memo (GY4)**
>
> | Test | Facts | Conclusion |
> |---|---|---|
> | Fixed place (s 1141(1)(a)) | Leeds offices are TEL's, not at TVS's disposal | No |
> | Acting on behalf of TVS | Engineers sell TVS's valves | Yes |
> | Habitually | Every utility deal | Yes |
> | Principal role | Engineers settle specification and price | Yes |
> | Routinely concluded without material modification | TVS signs unchanged | Yes |
> | Contract type | Transfer of ownership of goods TVS owns | (i) met |
> | Independent agent (s 1142)? | TEL is closely related (common control by TPLC) and acts almost exclusively for the group in this market (s 1142(1A)) | Not independent |
> | **Domestic result** | | **UK PE of TVS (s 1141(1)(b))** |
> | Treaty Art 5 (invented UK–Vallaria treaty: older agent wording) | Engineers have no authority to conclude contracts in TVS's name; contracts signed in Vallaria | **No treaty PE** |
> | **Answer** | Art 7: UK may not tax TVS's business profits | **No UK CT for TVS** |

**Going further: why the board still changes the model.** A conclusion resting on a treaty is only as safe as the treaty and the facts. Treaties are renegotiated; facts drift (an engineer given signing authority, or contracts routinely signed in Leeds); a principal purpose test, where a treaty has one, can deny treaty benefits (chapter 24); and the board must consider how the arrangement looks in Vallaria and under the group's published tax strategy (chapter 4). **From 1 January GY5, TEL becomes a buy-sell distributor**: it buys Ashlar valves from TVS and sells to the utilities in its own name. That replaces a PE question with a TP question about the distributor's margin (chapter 27).

> **The misconception.** "A foreign company selling into the UK through a group company's sales team cannot have a UK PE, because the team works for a different company and nobody here signs." Before 2026 this was close to the truth. Since then the **principal role** test makes signing irrelevant, and the **closely related** rule makes the separate company irrelevant. Only a treaty with the older wording now stands between the foreign company and a UK charge.

**Going further: the tax function's checklist for inbound sales models.**

- Map every foreign group company selling into the UK and who does what: who finds, negotiates, prices, signs.
- **Contract conclusion protocols:** if the foreign company genuinely negotiates and modifies terms, document it; if it rubber-stamps, assume the domestic test is met.
- Choose the model deliberately: **buy-sell distributor** (UK profit in the UK company, TP on the margin), **service provider/agent** (PE risk under the 2026 test), **commissionaire** (the arrangement Action 7 targeted).
- Check each relevant treaty's Article 5 (older or newer agent wording; construction thresholds; any PPT).
- Revisit on any change of facts and at each year end; record conclusions in the TP records (chapter 27). Where a PE exists, a non-resident must notify chargeability and file returns.

## Attributing profit to a permanent establishment

Suppose the treaty had not protected TVS. What profit would the UK tax?

**CTA 2009 s 21** (as reworded by FA 2026 Sch 7 para 5): the profits the PE "might be expected to make" if it were "a separate and independent enterprise engaged in the same or similar activities under the same or similar conditions", taking into account the **functions performed, assets used and risks assumed** through the PE and through the rest of the company. The PE is assumed to have the **same credit rating** as the company and the **equity and loan capital** it could reasonably be expected to have (s 21(2)).

**CTA 2009 s 20(1A)–(1E)** (substituted by FA 2026 Sch 7 para 4): ss 21 and 24 are to be read consistently with Article 7(2) of the OECD Model (approved 18 November 2025) and its Commentary, the **2010 Report on the Attribution of Profits to Permanent Establishments** (22 July 2010), the **Transfer Pricing Guidelines 2022** and the **March 2018 Additional Guidance**, as amended from time to time, subject to UK reservations, with a Treasury regulation power.

**The authorised OECD approach (AOA)**, as HMRC's manuals describe it (for example GIM10210; INTM450021 on PE records):

1. **Step 1: functional and factual analysis.** Hypothesise the PE as a separate enterprise; attribute functions, assets, risks and capital by reference to where the **people functions** that manage them are performed.
2. **Step 2: pricing.** Price the dealings between the PE and the rest of the enterprise at arm's length, applying TP methods by analogy.

> **Trap: repealed sections.** FA 2026 Sch 7 paras 6–8 **omitted CTA 2009 ss 22–23 and 25–32** for chargeable periods beginning on or after 1 January 2026: s 22 (transactions treated as arm's length), s 23 (goods and services provided to the PE), ss 25–28 (non-UK banks), **s 29 (allowable deductions)** and **ss 30–32 (restrictions, including s 32 on interest and financing costs)**. Citing "s 29" or "s 32" for a 2027 exam answer is quoting repealed law. s 24 remains.

**A live debate: dependent agent PEs.** One view: once the agent is paid an arm's length fee, nothing more is left for the PE. The other view, which commentators report the OECD's later work favours, looks at what the agent's people actually do: where they perform the functions that manage the enterprise's risks (inventory, credit, marketing intangibles), profit from those risks is attributed to the PE and the fee does not necessarily exhaust it. Outcomes range from nil to a large share of the sales profit. This is **disputed** territory internationally; the UK statute settles the method (s 21 read with the AOA), not the answer.

> **Worked example 23.3 (labelled illustration, not story)**
>
> | £ | |
> |---|---|
> | Profit attributed to the PE after the arm's length service fee to TEL (assumed) | 800,000 |
> | UK CT at 25% | **200,000** |
>
> TVS would then look to Vallaria for relief from double taxation under the treaty's elimination article (chapter 24). In practice the UK attribution and the TP pricing of TEL's fee must be consistent: chapter 27.

**Gains on PE assets.** Assets used for the PE's trade or held for the PE are within the CT charge on gains under TCGA s 2B(3), attributed under CTA 2009 ss 20 and 24.

**Coming from abroad.** HMRC's manual describes a compensating adjustment where a TP adjustment to a connected foreign company relates to a UK PE (INTM414510, reformed from 1 January 2026); the precise statutory route after FA 2026 was not located (flagged).

## Who answers for the foreign company

Taxing a company that lives abroad raises a practical problem: its directors, bank accounts and most assets are out of reach. **CTA 2010 Part 22** answers it in three short chapters, all core.

| Chapter | Sections | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Ch 6: UK representatives | ss 969–972 | A non-resident company's **UK PE is its UK representative**, treated as a separate person; the company's obligations on assessment, collection and recovery of CT are also the representative's (s 970); it remains the representative after the PE ceases, for tax relating to its period; limited exceptions (s 971) |
| Ch 7: recovery from related companies | ss 973–980 | If CT assessed on a **non-UK resident company** is unpaid **6 months** after it became payable, HMRC may serve a notice on a **related company** (a member of the same group, or of a consortium owning it, in the relevant period) requiring payment **within 30 days**; the notice has effect as an assessment; in consortium cases only a proportion (s 979); time limits in s 978 |
| Ch 5: set-off of income tax deductions | ss 967–968 | Income tax deducted from payments a company receives is set against its CT for the AP (s 967, UK resident; **s 968, non-UK resident**) |

**Do not confuse** Part 22 Ch 7 with **TMA 1970 ss 109B–109F** (chapter 22): the migration rules let HMRC recover unpaid exit tax from group companies and controlling directors at the moment of leaving. Ch 7 covers a company that is non-resident and remains chargeable here.

A non-resident within the charge (through a PE or a UK property business) is otherwise treated like any company for CT: it notifies chargeability, files returns, pays on the normal dates (by instalments if large) and is inside the regimes that apply to UK profits, such as the corporate interest restriction (chapter 28).

> - **Grades:** chargeable profits of non-UK resident companies **1**; non-resident and dual resident companies (gains) **1**; Part 22 CTA 2010 (excluding Chs 3, 8 and ss 990–995) **1**.

## Why the payer collects the tax

Now turn the telescope around: a UK company is paying money to someone abroad. The logic of withholding is **collection**: a foreign lender is hard to assess; the UK payer is easy to find. So the payer deducts income tax at source and pays it to HMRC, and the recipient reclaims or credits it, or a treaty cuts it.

**ITA 2007 s 874:** a duty to deduct income tax from **yearly interest arising in the UK** paid by:

- a **company**, a local authority or a partnership with a company member; or
- **any person** to a person whose usual place of abode is outside the UK.

**Rate.** For **2026/27** the deduction is at the **basic rate, 20%** (s 874 ties the deduction to the basic rate for the tax year). **Coming next (enacted):** from **2027/28** the rate becomes "the savings basic rate" (FA 2026 s 6(8), Sch 1 para 29), set at **22%** for 2027/28 by FA 2026 s 5. s 874(6A): the location of the deed is ignored in deciding whether interest arises in the UK.

**Yearly or short?** No statute defines yearly interest. HMRC notes that tax law has distinguished yearly from short interest **since 1806** and that the line rests on case law (SAIM9075). Interest on a loan intended to last, or capable of lasting, a year or more is yearly (*Bebb v Bunny*); interest on a loan for less than a year with no provision to extend is short, despite an annual rate (*Goslings and Sharpe v Blake*, CA); mere failure to enforce a debt within a year does not make interest yearly (*Gateshead Corporation v Lumsden*). Intention at the outset matters most; HMRC wants clear evidence where short loans are repeatedly rolled over (SAIM9076; INTM413210 for series of short loans to non-residents).

> **The modern landmark.** *HMRC v Joint Administrators of Lehman Brothers International (Europe)* [2019] UKSC 12 (judgment 13 March 2019; on appeal from [2017] EWCA Civ 2124). The administration of LBIE (which began in 2008) produced a surplus after all proved debts, reported at around £7bn, much of it payable as **statutory interest** to creditors for the years they had waited. The Supreme Court held unanimously that this was **yearly interest** within s 874, so income tax had to be deducted. Interest compensating a creditor for being kept out of money over more than a year can be yearly even though it never accrued as a liability from year to year.

**Administration (ITA 2007 ss 945–962).** A UK-resident company accounts for tax deducted on "section 946 payments" (interest under s 874, royalties under ss 903 and 906, and others) on **form CT61**, for quarters ending **31 March, 30 June, 30 September and 31 December**, plus a short return period at the end of its AP. The return and the tax are due **within 14 days** after the end of the return period (ss 947, 949). The recipient sets the tax against its own liability (for a company, CTA 2010 ss 967–968).

| **Treaty** | Lender claims on the **DT Company** form (relief at source or repayment, with certification of residence), or the borrower acts under an HMRC direction under the **Treaty Passport** scheme. **No self-help for interest** | TIOPA s 6; treaties |

**Treaty Passport.** A treaty-resident lender applies on **DTTP1**; a passport normally lasts **5 calendar years**. The UK borrower notifies each passported loan on **DTTP2** "as soon as possible". **Until HMRC issues a direction, the borrower must withhold** at the domestic rate.

**An exit that has closed.** The EU Interest and Royalties Directive exemptions (ITTOIA ss 757–767; ITA ss 914–917) were **repealed by FA 2021 s 34 for payments on or after 1 June 2021**. After Brexit, EU lenders need a treaty like everyone else.

> **Worked example 23.4: withholding across the Tarnmoor group (FY2026 rates; invented figures)**
>
> | Payment (annual) | Payer → payee | £ | Withholding? | Why |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Interest on £430m of loans at 6% (TEL £200m, TPLC £120m, TES £70m, TWS £40m) | UK companies → TFL | 25,800,000 | **None** | Excepted payment: beneficial owner a UK company (s 933) |
> | Interest on £550m 5.5% listed notes | TFL → bondholders | 30,250,000 | **None** | Quoted Eurobonds (s 882) |
> | (Counterfactual if unlisted, no treaty claims) | | | 20%: **6,050,000** | s 874 |
> | Project Undertow coupon on £80m 7% notes (rejected; chapter 29) | TFL → TCM (Marrovia: no treaty) | 5,600,000 | 20%: **1,120,000**; at 22% from 2027/28: **1,232,000** | s 874; no exit applies |
>
> TVS's interest to TFL (£7.2m) is paid by a Vallarian company: any withholding is Vallarian (10% under the invented treaty) and a DTR question for TFL (chapter 24), not a UK withholding point.

> **Going further: designing group debt for withholding.**
> - External bonds: list them (quoted Eurobond) or use a QPP.
> - Intra-group UK lending: excepted payments; keep the "reasonable belief" evidence (residence, beneficial ownership).
> - Overseas lenders: diarise DTTP2 filings and do not pay gross until HMRC's direction arrives; for non-passport lenders, the DT Company form.
> - Check whether interest is "yearly" on short facilities that are rolled over.
> - Withholding is rarely why a structure fails, but it is often the cost nobody costed. **M23 Q3** buried interest to **Bermuda** in a TP and CIR question; the examiners added that time was wasted on DPT, which does not apply to loan relationships.

## Royalties, and paying at the treaty rate

| Rule | Detail | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Patent royalties | Deduct at the **basic rate** from UK patent royalties charged to tax | ITA 2007 s 903 |
| IP royalties to non-residents | Deduct at the **basic rate** from royalties for the use of intellectual property where the owner's usual place of abode is outside the UK | s 906 (substituted FA 2016 s 40) |
| Rate | Basic rate (20% for 2026/27). The 22% savings basic rate from 2027/28 is for **interest only**; FA 2026 did not change ss 903 or 906 | FA 2026 s 6 |
| **Treaty rate on reasonable belief** | The payer may deduct at the treaty rate **without clearance** if it reasonably believes the payee is entitled to treaty relief; if wrong, it is liable as if s 911 had not applied | s 911 |
| **Anti-treaty-shopping** | Where the payee is **connected** with the payer (participation condition) and the royalty is paid under "DTA tax avoidance arrangements" (one of the main purposes was a treaty advantage contrary to the treaty's object and purpose), deduction is at the **full domestic rate** whatever the treaty says, and the tax **cannot be set off** under CTA 2010 ss 967–968; applies to payments on or after 17 March 2016; overrides TIOPA s 6 | s 917A (FA 2016 s 41; INTM630210) |

> **Worked example 23.5: Brackenwell's royalty (invented)**
>
> Brackenwell Sensors Ltd (BSL) pays an unconnected Vallarian university **£50,000** a year for a sensor patent licence, each **31 December**.
>
> | £ | Domestic (s 906) | Treaty rate on reasonable belief (s 911) |
> |---|---|---|
> | Royalty | 50,000 | 50,000 |
> | Withholding rate | 20% | 5% (invented UK–Vallaria treaty) |
> | Income tax deducted | 10,000 | **2,500** |
> | Paid to the university | 40,000 | **47,500** |
>
> BSL holds the university's evidence of Vallarian residence and entitlement, so it deducts **£2,500**. If its belief proved wrong, BSL would owe the further **£7,500** itself. The deduction goes on the **CT61 for the quarter to 31 December**, due by **14 January**. s 917A cannot apply: the university is unconnected. The royalty itself is deductible for BSL under the intangibles rules (chapter 11).

**Dividends.** No UK withholding on dividends (none of the Part 15 duties covers them). **FA 2026 s 42** abolished, from 2026/27, the notional tax credit non-UK resident recipients could treat as attached to UK distributions (ITTOIA s 399 omitted): an income tax point, **awareness** only.

> **Exam lens: deduction of income tax**
> - **Grade:** deduction of income tax, **core (1)**.
> - **Past appearances:** **M23 Q3** (15 marks): a loan from a US company that may take 60% control: TP, CIR and **WHT on interest to Bermuda**; examiners: time wasted on DPT. Withholding also appears as a natural tail on financing and IP questions.
> - **Traps:** using 22% for 2026/27 payments (22% is from 2027/28, and for interest only); assuming treaty rates on interest apply automatically (they need a DT Company claim or a Treaty Passport direction); forgetting the **reasonable belief** rules (s 930 for excepted payments; s 911 for royalty treaty rates); citing the EU Interest and Royalties Directive (repealed for payments from 1 June 2021); forgetting quoted Eurobonds; CT61 within 14 days.

## When the foreign company is a landlord

**In our invented case.** Until 30 June GY4 TIL's board met in Leeds, so it was UK resident (chapter 22 tells the migration story: exit charges and a £525,000 payment plan). Afterwards TIL is non-resident, earning its distribution profits in Ireland with no UK PE. But it still owns the **UK warehouse** bought for **£3.0m** in GY1, let to an unconnected logistics company.

- **Income:** TIL carries on a **UK property business** (CTA 2009 s 5), within **CT**, not income tax, since **6 April 2020**. It files CT returns on its rental profits at the main rate (with its many associated companies, its marginal relief upper limit is far below its profits) and is inside the corporate interest restriction like any group company with UK profits.
- **Non-resident landlord scheme.** The letting agent, or the tenant if there is no agent, must normally deduct income tax at the basic rate from rent paid to a landlord whose usual place of abode is abroad, unless HMRC has approved the landlord to receive the rent gross. Companies remain within the scheme after the 2020 move to CT; tax deducted is credited against the company's CT on that rental income (CTA 2010 s 968) (HMRC guidance note on the 2020 changes; professional summaries).
- **Gains:** on a sale of the warehouse TIL is chargeable under TCGA s 2B(4) (direct interest in UK land). The **£0.8m** gain postponed on migration under s 187B comes back into charge on that disposal (chapter 22).

> **Worked example 23.6 (labelled illustration, not story)**
>
> | £ | |
> |---|---|
> | Rent (assumed) | 200,000 |
> | Income tax deducted by the tenant without HMRC approval (20%) | 40,000 |
> | TIL's UK property business profit (assumed, after expenses) | 180,000 |
> | CT at 25% | 45,000 |
> | Less: income tax deducted (CTA 2010 s 968) | (40,000) |

> Tom's team applies for approval to receive the rent gross, so £40,000 a year is not tied up until the CT return.

> **Going further: inbound groups and the tax function.** For each non-resident group company with UK income: confirm the head of charge (s 5); register for CT; NRL approval for rent; Part 22 Ch 7 means the UK group bears the collection risk; include the company in CIR (chapter 28), Pillar Two data (chapter 30) and the SAO review only if UK-incorporated (chapter 4: TIL is Irish-incorporated, never within SAO).

## What to take away

- The UK taxes a non-resident company only where it leaves a footprint (CTA 2009 s 5): **dealing in or developing UK land**, **trading through a UK PE**, and since **6 April 2020** a **UK property business**. Gains follow (TCGA s 2B): PE assets; UK land and indirect disposals since **6 April 2019** (**75%** UK property richness; **25%** substantial indirect interest in the previous 2 years).
- A PE arises through a **fixed place of business** (domestic list has no time limit; treaties typically 12 months for construction) or a **dependent agent**. For chargeable periods beginning on or after **1 January 2026**, the agent test is **"habitually concludes contracts, or habitually plays the principal role leading to the conclusion of contracts that are routinely concluded without material modification"**. A **closely related** agent acting **exclusively or almost exclusively** for its group cannot be independent.
- **Preparatory or auxiliary** activity is excluded, but **anti-fragmentation** removes the exclusion for complementary functions in a cohesive business operation.
- A subsidiary is not a PE by control alone; a PE is never a separate legal person.
- **The treaty wins** (TIOPA s 6; Art 5, Art 7). The UK did not adopt **MLI Art 12**, so many treaties keep the older wording. Domestic test, treaty test, then the answer. TVS had a domestic PE in GY4 but no treaty PE; TEL became a buy-sell distributor from GY5.
- Attribution: **separate and independent enterprise** (s 21), read with the **AOA** (s 20(1A)–(1E)); **ss 22–23 and 25–32 are omitted**.
- **Part 22 CTA 2010:** the PE is the UK representative (Ch 6); recovery from related companies after **6 months**, payable within **30 days** (Ch 7); income tax deducted set against CT (Ch 5).
- **Withholding:** yearly interest **20%** (2026/27), **22%** from 2027/28; exits: excepted payments (UK companies, UK PEs; reasonable belief), banks, **quoted Eurobonds**, **QPPs**, treaty via **DT Company** form or **Treaty Passport** (DTTP1/DTTP2; withhold until directed). Royalties: basic rate, treaty rate on **reasonable belief** (s 911), subject to **s 917A**. **CT61** within **14 days**.

So, can a foreign company that never signs a contract here be taxed here? Yes, if someone closely related habitually closes its deals, and if no treaty stops it.

The treaty has been the hero of this chapter. Chapter 24, on treaties and double tax relief, looks at it properly.

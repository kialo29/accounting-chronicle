Chapter seven: The large company computation.

On the morning our invented group, Tarnmoor, published its results for Group Year Two, an analyst rang the investor relations team with a simple question. Tarnmoor Engineering, the group's main United Kingdom trading company, had made a profit before tax of twenty three and a half million pounds. The main rate of corporation tax is twenty five per cent. So why was its current tax bill about two million pounds, and not nearly six?

The call was passed to Tom Hesketh, the group's head of tax. Like everyone and everything at Tarnmoor, he is invented for teaching. His answer took twenty minutes. This chapter takes a little longer, because it is the full answer.

This is the third chapter of Part Two, on measuring profit. Chapter five showed that tax starts from the accounts. Chapter six showed how the accounts then report the tax. This chapter is the engine room between them. It covers the corporation tax computation of a large company, at the depth the Advanced Technical paper expects. Every topic in it is core in the syllabus grid, and every sitting of the paper since twenty twenty three has carried a computation question worth fifteen or twenty marks. It builds on The Living Law, chapters twenty and twenty one, which set out the basic pro forma and the rates. Throughout, the law is that for the financial year twenty twenty six.

A checkpoint, not a calculator.

Start with the misconception, because the analyst held it and so do many people who should know better. The belief is that a large company simply pays twenty five per cent of its accounting profit. It is easy to see why. The headline rate is twenty five per cent, the accounts show a profit, and the tax note in the accounts even reconciles one to the other. But the accounting profit is where the computation starts, not where it ends.

Think of the computation as a customs checkpoint. Everything in the profit and loss account passes through it. Some items are stopped and sent back, because tax refuses them, such as a fine or a gift of whisky to a customer. Some are held at the barrier and released later, because tax allows them only when cash moves, such as unpaid bonuses and pension contributions. And some are swapped for a different document, because tax has its own measure, such as capital allowances in place of depreciation, or a share-based deduction in place of the accounting charge for share options. The officer at the checkpoint is the law, and the law decides which items go through unchanged.

The Living Law, chapters twenty and twenty one, set out the shape. Corporation tax is charged on a company's taxable total profits for an accounting period. Those profits gather together income under several headings and chargeable gains, less reliefs, and the rate is applied to the result. At Advanced Technical level the shape is the same, but every line has more rules behind it.

Two statutes do most of the work in this chapter. The Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, which I will call the two thousand and nine Act, holds the rules for computing income. The Corporation Tax Act twenty ten, which I will call the twenty ten Act, holds the rates, the reliefs and the charitable donations rules. Pensions come from the Finance Act two thousand and four.

Here is the order the examiner expects, spoken once so you can hear its rhythm. Begin with profit before tax. Make the adjustments, each labelled with its reason. That gives the tax-adjusted trading profit before capital allowances. Deduct capital allowances to reach trading profits. Add the other sources. These are property income, profits on non-trading loan relationships, non-trading gains on intangible fixed assets, income not otherwise charged, and chargeable gains. That is total profits. Then come the reliefs, in statutory order. Losses of the same period and losses brought forward come first, then qualifying charitable donations, and group relief last. What remains is taxable total profits. Apply the rate, with marginal relief if it applies, set off any expenditure credit, and work out when the tax is paid.

Several of those lines have chapters of their own. Capital allowances belong to chapters eight and nine, research and development to chapter ten, intangibles to chapter eleven, loan relationships to chapter twelve, and losses and group relief to chapters fourteen and fifteen. This chapter owns the trading adjustments, the employment costs, entertaining and gifts, fines, income not otherwise charged, charitable donations, the lessee's side of long funding leases, and the rates. Then, at the end, it puts the whole of Tarnmoor Engineering's computation together.

Trade or investment, for a company.

Before any adjustment, the computation asks a prior question. Is this company trading at all? For an individual the answer decides whether there is any business income. For a company the stakes are different, but they are real. A trading company gets capital allowances against its trade and flexible relief for trading losses. A company with investment business gets management expenses instead, under a separate code that chapter thirteen explains. A trading company's interest is a trading debit, while an investment company's is non-trading, with different loss rules. The substantial shareholding exemption looks for trading groups, the research and development reliefs need a trade, and land dealing has its own regime.

The Living Law, chapter eleven, taught the badges of trade. These are the signs the courts look for, such as the subject matter, the length of ownership, the frequency of transactions, supplementary work, the circumstances of a sale and the motive. Two cases show how the courts use them. In Marson versus Morton, decided in the High Court in nineteen eighty six, Sir Nicolas Browne-Wilkinson, then Vice-Chancellor, stressed that whether there is a trade is a question of fact on all the circumstances. He added that the badges are not a complete list and no single one decides every case. And in Eclipse Film Partners Number Thirty Five versus H M R C, decided by the Court of Appeal in February twenty fifteen, a partnership acquired film rights and licensed them on to a distributor. The tribunal found it played no meaningful part in marketing or distributing the films. The Court of Appeal held that the tribunal was entitled to find no trade, and that whether a trade exists is a question for the tribunal of fact. H M R C estimated that about six hundred and thirty five million pounds of tax was protected, which is its estimate, not a finding of the court.

Eclipse matters for groups because it shows that activity, money and paperwork are not enough. A trade needs the company to do something commercial itself.

Inside a group, the question arises company by company, because each company is a separate taxpayer. In our invented group, Tarnmoor Engineering obviously trades. It designs, makes and sells valves, actuators and pumps. Tarnmoor plc, the listed parent, holds shares, raises money and directs the group, so it is a company with investment business. Tarnmoor Estates owns sites and lets them, mostly to group companies, so it too is an investment company. But if it bought land to develop and sell, the badges, and the transactions in land rules in chapter sixteen, could make the profit trading. Tarnmoor Finance, the treasury company, borrows on the bond market and lends to the group. Whether its loan relationships are trading or non-trading is a question chapter twelve takes up.

The exam point is simple. The badges of trade are core. When a scenario describes a property company selling a site, or a holding company receiving fees, ask first whether it trades, and say why, before you reach for the computation.

From profit before tax to trading profit.

The starting rule comes from section forty six of the two thousand and nine Act. Trade profits are calculated in accordance with generally accepted accounting practice, subject to any adjustment required or authorised by law. Chapter five explored the first half of that sentence. This chapter lives in the second half. The main general adjustments are section fifty three, which blocks capital items, and section fifty four, which allows only expenses incurred wholly and exclusively for the trade. Part twenty of the two thousand and nine Act then adds specific rules, several of which we meet in the sections that follow.

The method is mechanical, which is why it earns marks. Start from profit before tax, not operating profit. Add back items the accounts deducted that tax disallows or delays. Deduct items tax allows that the accounts did not deduct, and items in the accounts that are taxed elsewhere or not at all, such as dividends received or an accounting profit on selling plant. The examiners have reported candidates adding items that should have been deducted, so say plus or minus clearly for every line.

Depreciation is the commonest add-back. Tax replaces it with capital allowances, and on a disposal it replaces any accounting profit or loss with the sale proceeds coming off the pool. So an accounting profit on selling plant is deducted, and the proceeds go to the capital allowances computation in chapter eight. Depreciation on buildings is added back too, and the structures and buildings allowance in chapter nine may replace part of it.

Interest needs a pause. Tarnmoor Engineering borrowed two hundred million pounds from Tarnmoor Finance at six per cent to fund its trade, so it pays twelve million pounds of interest a year. That is a trading loan relationship debit, so it stays in the trading computation with no adjustment. Chapter twelve explains why, and chapter twenty eight explains when the corporate interest restriction claws some interest back. In Tarnmoor's case the restriction falls on Tarnmoor plc, not on Tarnmoor Engineering.

Impairments split two ways. A specific impairment of a trade debt owed by a customer follows the accounts and is deductible. An impairment of a loan to a fellow subsidiary is a loan relationship debit between connected companies, and the connected party rules generally give no debit. The November twenty twenty four computation turned on exactly that contrast.

Legal and professional costs follow the purpose. Fees to collect trade debts, defend a patent or fight a claim from a customer are revenue. Fees to acquire or improve a capital asset are capital. In Group Year Two Tarnmoor Engineering spent seventy thousand pounds on a planning appeal for a factory extension. That is capital, so it is added back, and it does not qualify for the structures and buildings allowance either.

Some items need no adjustment at all, and saying so earns marks. In Group Year Two Tarnmoor Engineering also expensed twenty four million pounds of costs for a new enterprise resource planning system. The accounts treat them as revenue and so does the computation, but H M R C might argue that part is capital. Chapter four explains why that uncertainty had to be notified under the uncertain tax treatment rules. In the computation itself, the costs simply pass through.

Pay that waits.

Wages and salaries are deductible when they are charged in the accounts, in the ordinary way. Bonuses are where the law steps in. The danger it guards against is an accrual for remuneration that is deducted now but paid much later, or never, while the employee is taxed only when the money arrives. Section twelve eighty eight of the two thousand and nine Act closes the gap.

The rule is this. If remuneration is charged in the accounts for a period of account but is not paid within nine months after the end of that period, it is deductible only in the period in which it is paid. A provision for future remuneration counts as an amount charged. If the computation is prepared before the nine months have run, the law assumes the amount is unpaid, and the return is amended if it is paid in time. Paid has the same meaning as for the employee's income tax. So a bonus counts as paid at the earliest of several moments, including actual payment and the moment the employee becomes entitled to it, with extra moments for directors, such as when the bonus is credited in the company's records.

In our invented case, Tarnmoor Engineering accrued two kinds of reward at the end of Group Year Two. The first was its annual bonus, two million pounds, paid through payroll on the thirty first of March in Group Year Three. That is well inside nine months, which run to the thirtieth of September, so the bonus is deductible in Group Year Two as charged. No adjustment.

The second was a cash long-term incentive plan for senior managers. The award vested on the thirty first of December, at the end of Group Year Two, and the accounts charged one point four million pounds. The cash was paid on the fifteenth of November in Group Year Three, more than nine months after the year end. So the one point four million pounds is added back in Group Year Two and deducted in Group Year Three, when paid. The November twenty twenty five examiners set exactly this trap, with an incentive plan bonus unpaid at nine months.

Two refinements matter at this level. First, the nine months run from the end of the period of account, so a short period moves the deadline. When Calder Valve Engineering changed its year end, its nine month accounting period ended on the thirty first of December in Group Year Three, so its nine month window ran to the following thirtieth of September. Second, the rule is about remuneration. Share awards have their own regime, which we reach shortly, and contributions to trusts for employees have theirs, which comes next.

There is a simple planning point. A tax function that sees large bonuses accrued at the year end should check the payroll calendar. Paying inside nine months keeps the deduction in the year of the charge. Paying in month ten moves it a whole year, which matters most when the rate or the company's loss position is about to change.

Money held in trust for employees.

An employee benefit trust is a trust set up by an employer to hold money or shares for its staff. For years they were used to win a deduction now and defer, or avoid, the employees' tax. Two cases tell the story.

In Macdonald versus Dextra Accessories, decided by the House of Lords in July two thousand and five, a group of companies paid money into an employee benefit trust and deducted it. The trustee paid out no salaries or bonuses in the relevant periods. Instead it made loans to beneficiaries. The House of Lords held that the contributions were potential emoluments, held with a view to becoming pay, so the companies could not deduct them until they were actually paid out as emoluments. H M R C's guidance says the Dextra decision governs contributions made before the twenty seventh of November, two thousand and two. Later contributions fall under the statutory rule that replaced it. Then came the Rangers case, R F C twenty twelve versus the Advocate General for Scotland, decided by the Supreme Court in twenty seventeen, which The Living Law, chapter three, told. It confirmed that pay routed through a trust is still the employee's earnings.

The statutory rule is now in section twelve ninety of the two thousand and nine Act. An employee benefit contribution is deductible only so far as qualifying benefits are provided out of it, or qualifying expenses paid, in the period or within nine months after it. Anything else waits until benefits are provided. Qualifying benefits are, in broad terms, payments that bring both an income tax charge and a national insurance charge on the employee, together with termination payments and the disguised remuneration charges. Loans are not qualifying benefits.

The Finance Number Two Act twenty seventeen tightened the rule in two ways. No deduction is allowed for a period beginning more than five years after the end of the period of contribution. And where the benefits attract income tax and national insurance contributions, the deduction is allowed only if those charges are paid within twelve months after the end of the period. Contributions to registered pension schemes, and share awards relieved under the share rules, are outside this regime.

For this example, suppose Tarnmoor Engineering paid one million pounds into an employee benefit trust in Group Year Two. By the thirtieth of September in Group Year Three, the trustee had paid six hundred thousand pounds of cash bonuses through payroll, and the income tax and national insurance contributions were paid on time. Six hundred thousand pounds is deductible in Group Year Two. The other four hundred thousand pounds is deductible only when it is paid out as qualifying benefits, and never if that happens in a period beginning more than five years later. This is a labelled hypothetical, not part of the story.

Pensions, paid and sometimes spread.

Pension contributions follow a different clock. Section one hundred and ninety six of the Finance Act two thousand and four says that employer contributions to a registered pension scheme are not capital, and that, so far as they are deductible, they are deducted in the period in which they are paid. The accounts usually charge pension cost on an accruals basis. So the computation adds back contributions accrued but unpaid at the year end, and deducts contributions paid in the year that were accrued in an earlier year.

In our invented case, Tarnmoor Engineering's defined contribution scheme receives employer contributions each month, paid on the twenty second of the following month. At the end of Group Year One, six hundred thousand pounds was accrued and paid in the January. At the end of Group Year Two, a larger accrual of nine hundred thousand pounds, reflecting a bigger workforce and higher contribution rates, was paid in the February. So in Group Year Two the computation adds back nine hundred thousand pounds and deducts six hundred thousand pounds. The net effect is three hundred thousand pounds added.

Spreading is the second rule, and the examiners say it is over-applied. A company might pay one enormous contribution, perhaps to repair a defined benefit deficit, and wipe out a year's profits. Section one hundred and ninety seven of the same Act spreads the deduction, but only when two tests are both met.

The first test compares contributions paid in the current chargeable period with those paid in the previous one. Spreading is possible only if the current contributions exceed two hundred and ten per cent of the previous period's. The second test measures the relevant excess, which is the current contributions less one hundred and ten per cent of the previous period's. If the relevant excess is under five hundred thousand pounds, there is no spreading. From five hundred thousand pounds to under one million pounds, half the excess moves to the next period. From one million pounds to under two million pounds, the excess is spread in thirds over this period and the next two. At two million pounds or more, it is spread in quarters over this period and the next three.

Tarnmoor Engineering paid eleven million pounds in Group Year One and eleven and a half million pounds in Group Year Two. Eleven and a half is nowhere near two hundred and ten per cent of eleven, so there is no spreading. The whole amount paid is deductible in the year of payment.

For this example, suppose a different company paid one million pounds last year and two point six million pounds this year. Two point six million pounds exceeds two point one million pounds, which is two hundred and ten per cent of last year, so the first test is met. The relevant excess is two point six million pounds less one point one million pounds, which is one point five million pounds. That falls in the thirds band. So one third of the excess, five hundred thousand pounds, is treated as paid in each of the next two periods. This year's deduction is one point six million pounds, and the next two years get five hundred thousand pounds each.

The examiners' report on the November twenty twenty five paper records that many candidates spread pension contributions when the rules were not triggered. Run both tests before you spread anything.

Shares for staff.

Large listed groups reward their people with shares. That raises a question the accounts answer one way and tax another. Under the international standard on share-based payment, I F R S two, and its United Kingdom equivalents, a company charges the fair value of share options to profit over the vesting period, though no cash leaves the company. What should tax allow?

Part twelve of the two thousand and nine Act gives the answer, and it ties the company's deduction to the employee's gain. Relief is available where an employee, or another person, acquires shares because of employment with the company. The shares must be ordinary, fully paid and not redeemable. They must be listed, or shares in a company not controlled by another company, or in a company controlled by a listed company. They must be shares in the employer, its parent, or a consortium member that owns the employer or its parent. And the employee must be subject to an income tax charge on the acquisition, or meet the conditions for restricted shares. The employing company must be within the charge to corporation tax on the business.

The amount of relief is the market value of the shares when acquired less the amount the employee paid. It is given for the accounting period in which the shares are acquired, which for options means the year of exercise, not the year of grant. A trading company deducts it in computing its trade profits. A company with investment business treats it as a management expense.

Then comes the exclusion, in section ten thirty eight. Where Part twelve relief is available, no other deduction is allowed for providing the shares or the options. That includes the accounting charge. Costs of setting up and administering the scheme remain deductible. Options that lapse unexercised get nothing, unless the employee is taxed on something, under section ten thirty eight A. Both rules were rewritten by the Finance Act twenty thirteen. Chapter five told how the Supreme Court, in N C L Investments, allowed accounting debits for share options under the earlier law. These rules are why that story stops in twenty thirteen.

In our invented case, Tarnmoor plc runs an option plan for senior staff across the group. The options are not tax-advantaged, so employees pay income tax when they exercise. In Group Year Two, Tarnmoor Engineering's accounts carried an accounting charge for the options of one point one million pounds. In the same year, Tarnmoor Engineering employees exercised options, and the market value of the shares they acquired exceeded the exercise prices by eight hundred thousand pounds in total.

So the computation adds back one point one million pounds and deducts eight hundred thousand pounds. The relief goes to Tarnmoor Engineering as the employer, even though the shares are in Tarnmoor plc. The difference in timing between the accounting charge and the tax relief is a deferred tax matter for chapter six.

Two planning points follow. The deduction moves with the share price and with employees' decisions to exercise, so the tax function forecasts it with the reward team, not from the accounts. And because relief lands in the employing company, the group should check that the charge sits in the company where the employees work, and that this company has profits to absorb it.

When people leave.

In Group Year Three, Calder Valve Engineering closed its process valves division. Dan Hartley, the invented engineer whose earlier years The Living Law followed, was made redundant on the thirtieth of September after twelve years' service. The Living Law, chapter nine, worked through his side of it. Here is Calder's side.

Calder paid Dan a statutory redundancy payment of nine thousand and twelve pounds, holiday pay of three thousand eight hundred pounds, post-employment notice pay of twenty five thousand pounds and an ex gratia payment of sixty thousand pounds. Of the ex gratia sum, ten thousand pounds went straight into his pension. Calder also paid his legal fee directly. Leaving the legal fee aside, the package came to ninety seven thousand eight hundred and twelve pounds.

The two thousand and nine Act has special rules for these payments, in sections seventy six to seventy nine. Section seventy six allows a deduction for a statutory redundancy payment, or an approved contractual payment in its place, where it would not otherwise be deductible. Section seventy nine deals with payments beyond the statutory amount, made when a trade, or part of a trade, permanently ceases. If such a payment would have been deductible but for the cessation, it is allowed, but only up to three times the statutory redundancy payment. A payment made after the cessation is treated as made on the last day of trading.

Why the cap? Because on a cessation the usual justification for a payment, that it serves the continuing trade, has gone. Parliament allows a generous but limited amount anyway.

Calder's position is better than that. Its trade did not cease. Only one division closed, and the company carried on making and selling other valves. In our invented case, the payments were made to secure an orderly closure and to protect Calder's standing with the staff who remained, so they are deductible under the ordinary rules as expenses of the continuing trade. That is the view this book takes on the invented facts. If H M R C argued instead that the extra payments were made only because part of the trade ceased, section seventy nine would still allow them up to three times nine thousand and twelve pounds, which is twenty seven thousand and thirty six pounds. The pension element follows the paid basis, and Calder paid it in the period.

Everything was paid on the thirtieth of September, inside Calder's nine month accounting period to the thirty first of December in Group Year Three, so it is deductible in that period. It formed part of the costs covered by the restructuring provision that chapter five discussed.

One warning for later chapters. H M R C's manual notes a case, George Peters and Company versus Smith, in which payments made as part of a bargain for the sale of shares did not qualify. Compensation paid to directors when a company is sold belongs to the buyer and seller's negotiation, and chapter twenty returns to it.

Entertaining, gifts, fines and crimes.

Section twelve ninety eight of the two thousand and nine Act sets a blunt rule. No deduction is allowed for business entertainment or gifts, however genuinely they serve the trade. Section twelve ninety nine then makes two exceptions for entertainment. The first covers entertainment of a kind the company provides in the ordinary course of its business, either for payment or free to advertise to the public generally. The second covers entertainment provided for employees. That exception is lost only if the entertainment is also provided for others and the employees' part is merely incidental to it.

So Tarnmoor Engineering's staff party, one hundred and twenty thousand pounds, is deductible. The amount per head does not matter. The one hundred and fifty pounds a head figure that keeps an annual party tax-free for employees is an income tax rule for the employees, not a limit on the company's deduction. The May twenty twenty six examiners reported candidates applying the employee limit to the company's deduction. By contrast, Tarnmoor Engineering's hospitality for customers at a trade fair, fifty thousand pounds, is business entertainment and is added back, including the cost of the staff who hosted it.

Gifts have their own exception in section thirteen hundred. A gift is allowed if it carries a conspicuous advertisement for the company, is not food, drink, tobacco or a voucher exchangeable for goods, and the company's gifts to that person in the accounting period cost no more than fifty pounds. Tarnmoor Engineering gave bottles of whisky with its logo on the label to its main customers, thirty thousand pounds in all. Drink is excluded whatever the branding, so the whole amount is added back. Branded pens, diaries and mouse mats under the limit would have been allowed. The examiners have used branded drink and branded pens at more than one sitting.

Fines are not in the statute at all. They are disallowed by case law. In McKnight versus Sheppard, decided by the House of Lords in nineteen ninety nine, a stockbroker was fined by the Stock Exchange and spent heavily defending himself. The legal costs were held deductible, as incurred wholly and exclusively to preserve his trade. The fines were not. Lord Hoffmann explained that a fine is meant to punish, and that a deduction would pass part of the punishment to the general body of taxpayers. In Group Year Two, Tarnmoor Engineering was fined two hundred and fifty thousand pounds after a health and safety prosecution. The fine is added back. Its legal costs of defending the prosecution stay deductible.

The edge of that principle is being litigated now. Between twenty thirteen and twenty sixteen, ScottishPower settled investigations by the energy regulator, paying nominal penalties and about twenty eight million pounds to consumers and consumer organisations. H M R C said the payments were in substance penalties. The First-tier Tribunal largely agreed and the Upper Tribunal wholly agreed. But in January twenty twenty five the Court of Appeal held that the payments were not fines or penalties, so nothing prevented their deduction, and it rejected the idea that a payment takes the tax treatment of whatever it replaces. H M R C appealed. The Supreme Court heard the case in May twenty twenty six and, as this chapter was written, had reserved judgment. Check the outcome before the exam.

Statute does cover two related items. Section thirteen oh three blocks deductions for a listed set of tax penalties and interest, such as penalties for inaccurate returns and V A T penalties. And section thirteen oh four denies any deduction for a payment whose making is a criminal offence, such as a bribe, or a payment made in response to blackmail. Chapter four explains the corporate offence of failing to prevent tax evasion, which sits alongside it.

Income the other rules miss.

Most of a company's income falls under a named heading, such as trading profits, property income, loan relationships, intangibles or distributions. But some income fits none of them. Part ten, chapter eight, of the two thousand and nine Act is the sweeper. Section nine hundred and seventy nine charges corporation tax on income that is not otherwise charged. It is the descendant of the old Case six of Schedule D.

Why have a sweeper at all? Because a tax built on named headings leaks at the joins. Without a residual charge, any income cleverly shaped to fall between trading, property and financing would escape. The sweeper means that the question is never whether income is taxed, only under which heading. That matters, because the heading decides what can be set against it and how losses behave.

Three limits keep the sweeper honest. It charges income, not capital, so a capital receipt that escapes every other rule is not taxed here, and gains belong to the gains rules. It does not apply to annual payments, which have their own chapter, or to income that is exempt. And sections nine hundred and eighty and nine hundred and eighty one exempt some items outright, such as profits from commercial woodlands and certain gains on financial futures. Priority rules then stop it overlapping other charges.

In a group, the sweeper earns its place at head office. In our invented case, Tarnmoor plc provides management services to the group's overseas subsidiary in Vallaria, an invented country, and charges it for them. In Group Year Two the charge, set at arm's length, was two point two million pounds. Tarnmoor plc is not trading. Its fee income is not property income or a loan relationship. If it is not a trade, it is income not otherwise charged, and Tom Hesketh's computation treats it that way.

The characterisation has a knock-on effect. Tarnmoor plc's management expenses, eight and a half million pounds in Group Year Two, are set first against its own profits, including that fee income. Only the excess, six point three million pounds, can be surrendered to Tarnmoor Engineering as group relief. Chapter thirteen develops the point, and chapter fifteen explains why surrendered management expenses are limited to the amount that exceeds the surrendering company's own profits.

For the exam, the point is recognition. When income appears in a scenario and fits no other heading, name the charge and say why it applies. Income not otherwise charged is a core item, and naming it correctly is worth a mark.

Giving to charity.

Companies give to charity, and the law gives relief, but not as a trading expense. Section thirteen oh one B of the two thousand and nine Act says a qualifying charitable donation is not deductible in computing income from any source. So if the accounts charge a donation, the computation adds it back. Relief comes instead under Part six of the twenty ten Act, as a deduction from total profits.

Two kinds of gift qualify. The first is a payment of money to a charity. It must not be repayable, the payer must not be a charity, there must be no associated acquisition, it must not be a disqualifying distribution, and any benefits the donor receives must stay within limits. The limits are twenty five per cent of a gift of one hundred pounds or less, and twenty five pounds plus five per cent of the excess for larger gifts. The benefits from all the company's gifts to that charity in the accounting period must not exceed two thousand five hundred pounds. The second kind is a gift of listed shares or similar investments, or an interest in land, to a charity. Then the market value, less anything the charity pays, is treated as a qualifying donation on a claim.

The order and the limits matter most in the exam. Qualifying donations are deducted after all other reliefs except group relief. They can reduce taxable total profits to nil but cannot create a loss, and they cannot be carried forward. The exception is a company with investment business, which can carry forward an excess donation made for its investment business as a management expense. An excess can be surrendered as group relief, but section one hundred and five of the twenty ten Act allows it only so far as it exceeds the surrendering company's own profits for the period, and donations are treated as the first of those amounts surrendered.

For this example, suppose Tarnmoor Engineering had given two hundred thousand pounds to an engineering education charity in Group Year Two. It would be added back in the trading computation and deducted from total profits after other reliefs, before group relief. Its taxable total profits would fall from eight point oh eight million pounds to seven point eight eight million pounds, saving fifty thousand pounds of tax. In the story, Tarnmoor Engineering made no such gift, and its figures stand.

Now the change for the financial year twenty twenty six. Donations can be tainted when they come back to the donor's circle. The Finance Act twenty twenty six, section fifty six and Schedule nine, replaced the old purpose test with an outcome test for donations made on or after the sixth of April, twenty twenty six. A donation is now tainted if, under or in connection with the arrangements, a linked person who is not a charity receives financial assistance from the charity or a connected charity. Financial assistance includes a loan, a guarantee, an indemnity or any investment, even one on arm's length terms. Schedule nine adds rules for associated donations. The law says a tainted donation gets no relief, so a group that gives to a charity which then lends to or invests in a group company has a real problem.

The planning angle is to give shares rather than cash where the group holds listed investments, since the relief is measured by market value, and to place the gift in a company with profits to absorb it.

Leases the lessee pays for.

When a company leases plant under a long funding lease, the tax system treats the lessee, not the lessor, as owning the plant. Chapter nine explains how the lessee gets capital allowances on deemed expenditure. This section deals with the other half, which this chapter owns. If the lessee gets allowances for the capital, it must not also deduct the capital through the rentals.

The rules are in the twenty ten Act. For a long funding finance lease, section three hundred and seventy seven caps the lessee's deduction for the rentals at the finance charge, or interest expense, shown under generally accepted accounting practice. For a lease accounted for as a right-of-use asset, section three hundred and seventy seven A adjusts the deduction when the lease liability is remeasured. For a long funding operating lease, section three hundred and seventy nine reduces the deductions by the expected fall in the plant's value over the lease, spread over time.

Why does the lessee own the plant for tax at all? Because a long funding lease is, in economic substance, a way of buying the plant with borrowed money. The rules give the allowances to the party that bears the economics of ownership, and then stop the same capital being relieved a second time through the rent. So the lessee's computation always has two halves that must match. The capital goes through allowances, and only the financing cost goes through the profit and loss account.

Two gateways come first. A lease of seven years or less is a short lease and cannot be a long funding lease. And the lessee's own return for the first period must treat the lease as a long funding lease. That choice cannot later be corrected as an error.

For this example, suppose Tarnmoor Engineering leases a machine for ten years under a long funding finance lease. The rentals charged in a year are five hundred thousand pounds, of which one hundred and twenty thousand pounds is the finance charge. Only one hundred and twenty thousand pounds is deductible as a rental. The other three hundred and eighty thousand pounds is relieved through capital allowances. In the November twenty twenty five paper, the examiners reported candidates putting long funding lease plant in the special rate pool. It belonged in the main pool. And remember that the lessee's choice in its first return is binding, so the tax team must decide the treatment when the lease is signed, not when the first computation is prepared.

Rates, marginal relief and the straddle.

For the financial year twenty twenty six the main rate is twenty five per cent and the small profits rate nineteen per cent. The lower limit is fifty thousand pounds and the upper limit two hundred and fifty thousand pounds. Both limits are divided by one plus the number of associated companies, and reduced in proportion for an accounting period shorter than twelve months. Between the limits, marginal relief reduces tax at the main rate by three two hundredths of the difference between the upper limit and the company's augmented profits, scaled by the ratio of taxable total profits to augmented profits. Augmented profits are taxable total profits plus exempt dividends received, other than those from fellow group companies.

The arithmetic produces a marginal rate of twenty six and a half per cent on profits between the limits. That figure is a trap. In the words of the examiners after the May twenty twenty six sitting, whilst the marginal rate of tax between the thresholds is twenty six point five per cent, this is not the rate that should be applied in full. For this example, suppose a stand-alone company with taxable total profits of one hundred and fifty thousand pounds and no dividends. Tax at twenty five per cent is thirty seven thousand five hundred pounds. Marginal relief is three two hundredths of one hundred thousand pounds, which is one thousand five hundred pounds. Tax is thirty six thousand pounds. You get the same answer by charging nineteen per cent on the first fifty thousand pounds and twenty six and a half per cent on the next one hundred thousand pounds. The marginal rate applies to the slice, never to the whole.

In a large group the limits almost vanish. In Group Year Two, Tarnmoor Engineering had nine associated companies, so each limit is divided by ten. The upper limit is twenty five thousand pounds. Any member with real profits pays the full twenty five per cent. Calder's nine month period shrinks the limits further, to three thousand seven hundred and fifty pounds and eighteen thousand seven hundred and fifty pounds. Marginal relief questions therefore come in small groups, such as the two-company group in May twenty twenty six, where the best answers used group relief to keep profits in the nineteen per cent band.

Finally, the straddle. Rates are set for financial years, which run from the first of April. A company whose accounting period straddles the first of April splits its profits between the two financial years by time, and applies each year's rates and limits to its share. Take a real-calendar example, labelled as such. A company with a year ended the thirty first of December, twenty twenty six, has ninety days in the financial year twenty twenty five and two hundred and seventy five days in the financial year twenty twenty six. The rates and limits are the same in both years, so the split changes nothing, and saying so earns the mark. The split mattered when the main rate rose from nineteen to twenty five per cent on the first of April, twenty twenty three, which The Living Law, chapter twenty, described. Because the financial year twenty twenty seven keeps the same rates, every Tarnmoor computation in this book uses one set of figures.

Tarnmoor Engineering's computation, start to finish.

Now the analyst's question can be answered in full. Here is Tarnmoor Engineering's computation for Group Year Two, told in round numbers. The full working, laid out as the examiner wants it, is in the reading edition.

Profit before tax was twenty three and a half million pounds. Add back depreciation of plant and buildings, sixteen point two million pounds in total. Add back the long-term incentive plan accrual unpaid at nine months, one point four million pounds. Add back pension contributions accrued but unpaid, nine hundred thousand pounds. Add back the share option charge, one point one million pounds. Add back the fine, the customer hospitality, the whisky and the planning appeal fees, four hundred thousand pounds between them. The add-backs total twenty million pounds.

Then deduct three items. Last year's pension accrual, paid this year, six hundred thousand pounds. The share relief on options exercised, eight hundred thousand pounds. And the accounting profit on selling old plant, one hundred thousand pounds. The deductions total one and a half million pounds.

So the tax-adjusted trading profit before capital allowances is forty two million pounds. That figure is after deducting the twelve million pounds of trading interest to Tarnmoor Finance. Capital allowances, which chapter eight builds line by line, are sixteen million pounds. Trading profits are twenty six million pounds, and Tarnmoor Engineering has no other income, so total profits are twenty six million pounds.

Then group relief, which chapter fifteen explains. Tarnmoor plc surrenders excess management expenses of six point three million pounds and a non-trading loan relationship deficit of seven point six seven million pounds. Brackenwell Sensors, bought in the middle of the year, surrenders its post-acquisition loss of two point six million pounds. And Helmside Energy, the hydrogen joint venture, provides consortium relief of one point three five million pounds. Together that is seventeen point nine two million pounds.

Taxable total profits are eight point oh eight million pounds. With nine associated companies there is no marginal relief, so tax at twenty five per cent is two point oh two million pounds. Chapter ten will show how an expenditure credit surrendered by Brackenwell then discharges part of that liability. Tarnmoor Engineering pays as a very large company, so, as chapter three explained, the tax falls due in four instalments during the year itself, of five hundred and five thousand pounds each.

Here, then, is the answer to the analyst, in four steps. Twenty five per cent of twenty three and a half million pounds is about five point nine million pounds. The adjustments add eighteen and a half million pounds of profit, which adds about four point six million pounds of tax. Capital allowances take away sixteen million pounds of profit, about four million pounds of tax. Group relief takes away about seventeen point nine million pounds, about four and a half million pounds of tax. What remains is two point oh two million pounds. The low figure is not a gap in the law. It is the law working as designed, with much of the relief bought by losses and expenses elsewhere in the same group.

How the examiner sets it.

Every Larger Companies and Groups paper from May twenty twenty three to May twenty twenty six has included a computation question of fifteen or twenty marks. The scenario is usually one company, or a two-company group. The requirement says calculate, with explanations, and the marks come at half a mark or one mark per point.

The same items keep returning. Bonuses and incentive plans unpaid at nine months. Pension contributions, with spreading tested but usually not triggered. Staff entertaining against customer entertaining. Branded drink against branded pens. Fines and penalties. Donations. Impairments of trade debts against loans to group companies. Long funding leases. Then capital allowances, losses, and increasingly a tail on instalments, current tax and deferred tax.

The computation also travels with friends. Its favourite companions are capital allowances, losses, quarterly instalments and deferred tax, and in group questions it is often paired with group relief or a property disposal. So when you practise, do not practise the adjustments alone. Practise carrying the adjusted profit forward into allowances, losses, instalments and the tax charge, because that is how the marks are spread.

Four habits earn marks. First, list every item, including those needing no adjustment, with a few words of reason. Nil adjustments earn marks too. Second, follow the statutory order of reliefs. Third, keep units consistent. Use pounds or thousands of pounds throughout a table, never both, because the examiners have complained about mixed units. Fourth, deal with the rate properly, including marginal relief, associated companies and any straddle, and never apply twenty six and a half per cent to the whole profit.

The traps the examiners report are consistent. Spreading pension contributions when neither test is met. Applying the employee entertaining limits to the company's deduction. Treating a long funding lease as special rate expenditure. Missing marginal relief in a small group, or applying twenty six and a half per cent to everything. And skipping the tax charge at the end of the question, which is often worth easy marks. Each of these appears in an examiners' report from twenty twenty three to twenty twenty six.

And under the new exam software, any working left in the spreadsheet below the answer box and not copied into the answer will not be marked. Build the computation in the answer box, or copy it there, before you move on.

What to take away.

A large company does not pay twenty five per cent of its accounting profit. It pays twenty five per cent of taxable total profits, after a checkpoint that stops some items, delays others and swaps some for tax's own measures, and after reliefs that can come from elsewhere in the group.

The computation starts at profit before tax and follows section forty six of the two thousand and nine Act. Accounts first, then adjustments required or authorised by law. Capital is out, and expenses must be wholly and exclusively for the trade.

Whether a company trades is a question of fact on all the circumstances. Marson versus Morton and Eclipse Film Partners Number Thirty Five show the courts looking at what the company actually does.

Remuneration not paid within nine months after the end of the period of account is deductible only when paid. Employee benefit trust contributions are deductible only as qualifying benefits are provided within nine months, with a five year cut-off and a twelve month deadline for the employee's income tax and national insurance contributions.

Pension contributions are deductible when paid. Spreading applies only if this period's contributions exceed two hundred and ten per cent of last period's, and the excess over one hundred and ten per cent is at least five hundred thousand pounds. Then halves, thirds or quarters, at five hundred thousand pounds, one million pounds and two million pounds.

Share relief under Part twelve is the market value less the price paid, in the year the shares are acquired. The accounting charge is added back, set-up and running costs stay deductible, and lapsed options get nothing.

Redundancy payments are deductible when the trade continues. On a cessation of a trade or part of a trade, extra payments are allowed up to three times the statutory payment.

Business entertainment and gifts are disallowed. Staff entertaining is allowed whatever its cost per head. Branded gifts up to fifty pounds per person are allowed, but never food, drink, tobacco or vouchers. Fines are disallowed by case law, McKnight versus Sheppard, while the ScottishPower appeal tests where a penalty ends and a payment to consumers begins.

Income not otherwise charged sweeps up income that fits no other heading, such as head office fees in a holding company that does not trade.

Qualifying charitable donations come off total profits after other reliefs but before group relief. They cannot create a loss and cannot be carried forward, except by an investment company. Since the sixth of April, twenty twenty six, an outcome test taints donations where the charity gives financial assistance back to the donor's circle.

Long funding lease lessees deduct only the finance charge, or a reduced operating rental, because the capital comes through allowances.

Rates for the financial year twenty twenty six are twenty five and nineteen per cent, with limits of fifty thousand pounds and two hundred and fifty thousand pounds, divided by one plus the associated companies. Twenty six and a half per cent is a marginal rate on a slice, never the rate on the whole.

And Tarnmoor Engineering's figures for Group Year Two were forty two million pounds before allowances, twenty six million pounds of trading profits, seventeen point nine two million pounds of group relief, eight point oh eight million pounds taxable, and two point oh two million pounds of tax.

Prologue: One loan, two answers.

On the eleventh of April, twenty twenty four, at ten o'clock in the morning, the Court of Appeal handed down a judgment remotely. Its front page records the time. The case was BlackRock HoldCo Five versus H M R C, decided by the Court of Appeal in twenty twenty four. H M R C means His Majesty's Revenue and Customs.

The case was about one set of loans, worth about four billion dollars, made inside one group of companies. Two rules of United Kingdom corporation tax looked at those loans. The first rule asked what strangers would have done, and it found nothing wrong. The second rule asked why the borrower had borrowed, and it took away every pound of the interest deductions. The taxpayer won the first argument and lost the case.

This prologue tells that story and then stops. It opens a book written for the Chartered Tax Adviser Advanced Technical paper, Taxation of Larger Companies and Groups. The law throughout is the law for the financial year twenty twenty six. You met the basic loan relationship rules, and group relief, in The Living Law, chapters twenty one and twenty four. Here the same rules meet a group on a far larger scale.

A British link in an American chain.

In two thousand and nine, the BlackRock group bought the business of Barclays Global Investors. Part of the money for that acquisition travelled down a chain of companies formed in Delaware. Each was a limited liability company, and the judgments number them like stepping stones. The one that matters is L L C Five.

L L C Five was a Delaware company. But it was managed and controlled in the United Kingdom, so for corporation tax it was a company resident here. Its American parent, L L C Four, put cash and shares into it. In return, L L C Four took ordinary shares and loan notes worth about four billion dollars. L L C Five then put the money and shares into the next company down the chain, L L C Six, and took preference shares. L L C Six used the funds towards the purchase.

Picture the chain as a sandwich. American bread on top, American bread underneath, and one British filling in the middle. The filling had a very particular taste.

Look at L L C Five's tax position. Its income was the dividends on its preference shares, and those dividends were exempt from United Kingdom tax. Its expense was the interest on the loan notes. Under the loan relationship rules, that interest was a deductible debit. So each year L L C Five had no taxable income and a large deficit. It surrendered that deficit, as group relief, to other BlackRock companies in the United Kingdom, without asking for payment. They set it against their own profits.

Commentators put the deductions claimed at about six hundred and fifty four million pounds. H M R C refused them. It gave two reasons. The first was transfer pricing. The second was the unallowable purpose rule. The case climbed through three courts. On each rule the answer changed at least once.

What strangers would have done.

Start with transfer pricing. The rules now live in Part four of the Taxation, International and Other Provisions, Act twenty ten, which I will call the International Act. They apply when two connected parties make a deal on terms that differ from the terms independent parties would have agreed. If the actual terms give one side a United Kingdom tax advantage, that side is taxed as if the arm's length terms had applied. The arm's length deal can even be no deal at all, where strangers would never have done business.

H M R C argued exactly that. No independent lender, it said, would have lent four billion dollars to L L C Five on those terms.

The First-tier Tribunal disagreed. Judge John Brooks gave its decision on the third of November, twenty twenty. He found that an independent lender would have made loans of the same amount, on the same interest terms. There was one condition. A stranger would have demanded covenants, the promises a borrower gives to protect its lender. The tribunal found those covenants would have been given.

The Upper Tribunal reversed him. Mr Justice Michael Green and Judge Rupert Jones decided the appeal on the nineteenth of July, twenty twenty two. They held that you cannot import covenants that never existed in the real deal. Without them, the comparison failed, and H M R C won.

Then the Court of Appeal reversed the Upper Tribunal. The court was Lord Justice Peter Jackson, Lord Justice Nugee and Lady Justice Falk, and Lady Justice Falk gave the main judgment. Her reasoning ran like this. In the real deal, the lender belonged to the same group as everyone else in the chain. A stranger lending to L L C Five would face risks the group lender did not face. Other companies in the group might act in ways that damaged the loan. An independent lender would protect itself against that, so the hypothetical deal must include the protection. The covenants were part of a fair comparison, not an invention. The tribunal's finding was restored.

So on transfer pricing, BlackRock won. The interest was priced at a level strangers would have accepted.

Commentators read one passage of Lady Justice Falk's judgment as a hint. H M R C might have done better by looking at the borrower's side. Would L L C Five, if it were independent, have wanted to borrow at all? That argument was not run, and the case did not decide it. Chapter twenty seven returns to it.

Why this company borrowed.

The unallowable purpose rule is in section four four one of the Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, which I will call the two thousand and nine Act. It works like this. If a loan relationship has an unallowable purpose in a period, the debits are disallowed. Only the share attributable to that purpose goes, on a just and reasonable apportionment.

A purpose is unallowable if it is not among the company's business or other commercial purposes. A tax avoidance purpose can count as commercial, but only if it is not the main purpose, or one of the main purposes. And the tax advantage need not be the company's own. A tax advantage for any other person counts too.

The First-tier Tribunal found that L L C Five had two main purposes. One was commercial. The other was to obtain a tax advantage. But it apportioned all the debits to the commercial purpose, so everything stayed deductible. The Upper Tribunal disagreed. It treated the commercial benefit as a by-product of a decision driven by tax, and it apportioned all the debits to the tax purpose.

The Court of Appeal agreed with the Upper Tribunal's result, though not with all of its reasoning. Purpose, it was common ground, is a question of the company's own subjective intention. The court began with a plain question. Why did L L C Five enter into the loans? The answer was to obtain a tax advantage.

The court did not say that every deductible interest payment is suspect. Lady Justice Falk was careful on this. A company's awareness that interest is deductible does not make tax a main purpose. Nor does the size of the deduction, on its own. Something more is needed. Here there was more. L L C Five's board did see a commercial margin, because dividends in would exceed interest out. But the tax advantage belonged to the group, and the group's plan was why L L C Five was there. In the words Lord Justice Nugee repeated from Lady Justice Falk's judgment, taking the loans to secure that advantage was the company's sole raison d'être.

Then came the apportionment. The court asked what the debits would have been but for the tax purpose. The answer was nothing, because without the tax advantage the loans would not have been made. So all the debits were attributable to the unallowable purpose, and none was deductible. The approach echoes an earlier Court of Appeal case, Fidex, from twenty sixteen.

BlackRock asked the Supreme Court for permission to appeal. Lord Hodge, Lord Hamblen and Lady Simler refused it, because the appeal did not raise an arguable point of law. The refusal appeared on a list in the autumn of twenty twenty four. The Court of Appeal's judgment stands.

Why one loan can get two answers.

Here is the heart of it. The two rules ask different questions, so they can give different answers about the same loan.

Transfer pricing is a valuer's question. It looks at the terms and compares them with a market of strangers. It does not ask why the group did the deal. A loan can pass that test even if tax was the whole reason for it.

The unallowable purpose rule is a detective's question. It looks at motive. It does not care whether the interest rate was fair. A loan can pass the market test perfectly and still fail on purpose.

That leads to a common misconception. Many people believe that if an intra-group loan is priced at arm's length, the interest is safe. It is not. Arm's length pricing gets you through the first gate. It says nothing about the second.

BlackRock was not alone. Within weeks the Court of Appeal decided two more cases on the same rule. Kwik-Fit came on the third of May, twenty twenty four, and was about an internal reorganisation of loans so that trapped losses could be used. J T I Acquisition Company came on the thirteenth of June, twenty twenty four, and concerned borrowing to fund a real acquisition. In J T I the court treated the tax advantage as bolted on to a commercial deal, and still disallowed everything. H M R C won all three. The Supreme Court refused permission in all three on the same list. Chapter twelve tells the other two stories and the case still working its way up.

There is also a gate that BlackRock's loans never had to pass. They were made in two thousand and nine. The corporate interest restriction arrived later, for periods of account starting on or after the first of April, twenty seventeen. It caps a group's net interest deductions in the United Kingdom, usually at thirty per cent of tax E B I T D A. E B I T D A stands for earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. Below a de minimis of two million pounds a year, there is no restriction. A group borrowing like this today, in the financial year twenty twenty six, would meet all three gates. Chapter twenty eight teaches the third.

The questions this book asks.

This one loan leads straight into the four questions that run through the book.

First, is a group one taxpayer or many? L L C Five was a separate company with its own return. Yet its purpose was read through the group's plan, and its deficit was used by other companies through group relief. The law treated it as one company when that suited the rule, and as part of a group when that told the truth.

Second, where are profits made, and what stops them drifting? A British link in an American chain created a United Kingdom deduction with no matching United Kingdom income. Much of this book is about the rules that watch borders.

Third, what does the law follow? The accounts, the deal, or its own purpose? The loan relationship rules start from the accounts. Transfer pricing tests the deal against the market. The unallowable purpose rule overrides both when the purpose is wrong.

Fourth, what must a group's tax function spot? An adviser who checked only the interest rate would have missed the point that decided the case. The examiner wants you to identify the issues, all of them, and know which specialist rule answers each one.

So when you meet an intra-group loan, in practice or in the exam, ask three things. Are the terms what strangers would agree? Why did this company borrow? And how much of the group's interest can the United Kingdom take, in total? Chapter twenty seven answers the first, chapter twelve the second, and chapter twenty eight the third.

Most of this book follows an invented group of companies, Tarnmoor, whose story begins in chapter one. Everything about Tarnmoor is made up. BlackRock is real, and so is every rule in this prologue.

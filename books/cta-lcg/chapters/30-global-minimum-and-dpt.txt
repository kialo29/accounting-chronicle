Chapter thirty: The global minimum and the end of diverted profits tax.

A floor under the world.

In October twenty twenty one, more than one hundred and thirty five countries agreed something that tax policy had never managed before. They agreed a minimum. The largest multinational groups would pay at least fifteen per cent on their profits in every country where they operate, or someone, somewhere, would collect the difference. The deal was struck through the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, which I will call the O E C D, and its wider Inclusive Framework of countries. The United Kingdom wrote its share of the deal into the Finance Number Two Act twenty twenty three, which I will call the Pillar Two legislation.

Seven years earlier, the United Kingdom had reached for a blunter weapon. In his Autumn Statement on the third of December, twenty fourteen, the Chancellor, George Osborne, announced a diverted profits tax. The press called it the Google tax. It sat outside corporation tax, charged a higher rate than corporation tax, and made the company pay before it could argue. For accounting periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six, it is gone. Its work now belongs to a charge inside corporation tax, on what the law calls unassessed transfer pricing profits.

Both changes share a single idea. Profit should be taxed somewhere, at a fair rate, and a group that shifts it away should gain nothing. So this chapter asks two linked questions. When a large group's profits end up somewhere taxed below the floor, who collects the difference? And now that diverted profits tax has gone, what stops a group from pricing its own dealings so that profit quietly leaves the United Kingdom?

This is chapter thirty, the last chapter of Part Four, on profits that cross borders. Everything in it is awareness level on the Advanced Technical paper, grade three in the syllabus grid. That covers multinational top-up tax, domestic top-up tax and diverted profits tax. None of them was examined between May twenty twenty three and May twenty twenty six. The Living Law only signposted these rules, so we start from first principles, but we keep to the depth that an awareness topic deserves. Its close neighbours are chapters twenty six and twenty seven, on controlled foreign companies and transfer pricing. Throughout, we apply the law for the financial year twenty twenty six, under the Finance Act twenty twenty six.

Why a minimum tax.

Start with the problem. Profit is easy to move and hard to pin down. A group can put its patents, its brands or its lending in a subsidiary in a low tax country, and pay that subsidiary royalties or interest from everywhere else. Transfer pricing asks whether the price is right. Controlled foreign company rules, chapter twenty six, ask whether the parent's country should tax the subsidiary's profit. But neither rule touches the low rate itself. And for decades countries competed on that rate, each cutting a little to attract the mobile profit of large groups.

The minimum tax does not forbid a low rate. A country may still tax at nine per cent, or at nothing. What changes is that the low rate stops working for the largest groups, because the difference up to fifteen per cent is collected anyway. Picture a floor fitted under every country's tax rate. If a country leaves a gap below the floor, another country fills it. The precise rule is this. For each country where a group operates, the group works out an effective tax rate on a common measure of profit. Where that rate is below fifteen per cent, a top-up tax brings it up to fifteen.

In December twenty twenty one the O E C D published model rules for this, called the global anti-base erosion rules. I will call them the GloBE rules. As commentators describe the arrangement, countries were not obliged to adopt them, but those that did were expected to follow the model, so that the pieces would fit together across borders. A floor only works if every country measures the room the same way. The whole project is usually called Pillar Two. A separate first pillar is not on this paper.

Is it a good idea? That is a live debate, and the paper will not ask you to settle it. Supporters say it ends a race to the bottom that drained public revenue and rewarded paper structures over real investment. Critics say it erodes a country's right to set its own taxes, blunts tax incentives that small economies use to attract real activity, and buries large groups in compliance. Both points have force, and economists still argue about the evidence. The law, for now, has chosen the floor, and the United Kingdom has built it into two taxes.

The United Kingdom's two taxes, and who is in.

The first is multinational top-up tax, in Part three of the Pillar Two legislation. It applies to accounting periods commencing on or after the thirty first of December, twenty twenty three. Its main engine is the income inclusion rule. A United Kingdom parent of a qualifying group pays top-up tax on the low taxed profits of its members abroad. The Finance Act twenty twenty five added a second engine, the undertaxed profits rule, for accounting periods commencing on or after the thirty first of December, twenty twenty four. It is a backstop. Where low taxed profit escapes every income inclusion rule, perhaps because the parent's country has none, other countries collect a share.

The second is domestic top-up tax, in Part four of the same Act, from the same date in twenty twenty three. It applies the same arithmetic to the group's United Kingdom entities, so that any top-up on United Kingdom profit is collected by the United Kingdom, not by a parent's country abroad. H M R C's manual describes it as the United Kingdom's qualified domestic minimum top-up tax. So the order of collection is this. The country where the profit arises has first claim, through its own domestic top-up tax, which is credited in the group calculation. Then the parent's country collects through the income inclusion rule. The undertaxed profits rule mops up anything left.

Who is in? The threshold is in section one hundred and twenty nine of the Pillar Two legislation. A group qualifies if the revenue in its ultimate parent's consolidated accounts exceeds seven hundred and fifty million euros in at least two of the four previous accounting periods, with short periods scaled down, and at least one member is located in the United Kingdom. Notice the word exceeds. The statute says it, and so does H M R C's manual. But H M R C's online guidance on whether you need to report says seven hundred and fifty million euros or more. That only matters for a group at exactly the line, and there the statute governs.

Now meet our invented group, Tarnmoor. Every company, person and country in it is invented for teaching. Tarnmoor P L C is a widely held engineering group listed in London. It is the ultimate parent entity, the company at the top of the consolidated accounts. Its consolidated revenue in Group Year One was one thousand, one hundred and eighty million pounds. At the story's invented exchange rate of one euro fifteen to the pound, that is about one point three six billion euros, and every year from Group Year One to Group Year Seven is far above the line. In our invented case Tarnmoor was already in scope before Group Year One, so Tarnmoor P L C long ago registered as the group's filing member.

Measuring the rate.

The GloBE rules do not use any country's own tax computation. They start from the accounting profit of each group entity, as used for the consolidated accounts, and make a set of adjustments of their own. The result is called GloBE income. Against it they set covered taxes, broadly taxes on income, including the group's own deferred tax adjustments and some taxes paid by other members on that entity's profit.

The effective tax rate is then worked out country by country, not company by company. All the group's entities in one territory are blended together. Total covered taxes in the territory, divided by total GloBE income in the territory, gives the jurisdictional rate. If it is below fifteen per cent, H M R C's manual explains, the top-up percentage is fifteen per cent less that rate.

The top-up percentage is not applied to the whole profit. First, the rules deduct a substance-based income exclusion. It is a deduction in proportion to the payroll costs and tangible assets the group has in the territory, a rough return on real people and real plant. What remains is the excess profit, and the top-up percentage applies to that. A territory with many staff and factories may owe little or nothing even at a low rate. A territory holding only patents or loans gets almost no exclusion. In the Tarnmoor figures that follow, we leave the exclusion out, which overstates the top-up a little, and we treat accounting profit as GloBE income.

Here is the misconception to drop. People say that under Pillar Two every company must now pay at least fifteen per cent. It is not true. First, the rules reach only groups over the threshold. Second, the test is a blended rate for each territory, so one company at ten per cent and its sister at twenty may together clear the floor. Third, the measure is GloBE income and covered taxes, not taxable profit and tax paid. A United Kingdom company whose current tax is low because of full expensing does not create a top-up, because the GloBE rules count the deferred tax as well. As chapter six explained, the accounts themselves keep Pillar Two out of deferred tax under the temporary exception in I A S twelve, and show any top-up tax separately as current tax.

So the rule is a floor for each territory of a large group, measured on its own base. It is not a minimum rate for each company.

Tarnmoor's Marrovian test.

In our invented case, the group's finance company, Tarnmoor Capital, is resident in Marrovia, an invented country with a corporation tax rate of nine per cent and, in the story, no domestic minimum tax of its own.

In Group Year Five its profit is five point one million pounds, interest of five point four million less three hundred thousand of expenses. Marrovia taxes that at nine per cent, which is four hundred and fifty nine thousand pounds.

The United Kingdom also taxes some of that profit. As chapter twenty six shows, Tarnmoor Capital is a controlled foreign company, which I will call a C F C. After the finance company exemption, a quarter of its profit is chargeable, one point two seven five million pounds. Tarnmoor P L C pays a C F C charge of two hundred and four thousand pounds on it, after credit for the Marrovian tax.

Now the Pillar Two step. Section one hundred and seventy nine of the legislation pushes a parent's C F C tax down to the C F C for this purpose, so it counts as a covered tax of Marrovia. For mobile income such as interest, the amount pushed down is capped. The cap is fifteen per cent less the local rate, applied to that income. Here that is six per cent of five point one million pounds, or three hundred and six thousand pounds. The C F C charge of two hundred and four thousand pounds sits inside the cap, so all of it moves.

Marrovia's covered taxes are therefore four hundred and fifty nine thousand plus two hundred and four thousand, which is six hundred and sixty three thousand pounds. Divided by five point one million, the effective rate is thirteen per cent. The top-up percentage is two per cent. Two per cent of five point one million pounds is one hundred and two thousand pounds. Tarnmoor P L C pays it as multinational top-up tax under the income inclusion rule. The full working is in the reading edition.

Look at the total. Marrovian tax, the C F C charge and the top-up come to seven hundred and sixty five thousand pounds. That is exactly fifteen per cent of five point one million. Without the push-down, the top-up alone would have been three hundred and six thousand pounds, exactly what the C F C charge and the top-up now make together. The floor holds whichever route the money takes. The same arithmetic in Group Years One to Four, on a profit of three point three million pounds, gives a top-up of sixty six thousand pounds a year.

Elsewhere the floor does not bite on the story's figures. Vallaria taxes Tarnmoor Vallaria at twenty per cent. The United Kingdom companies pay twenty five per cent, so on the story's figures there is no domestic top-up tax. Tarnmoor Ireland, Irish resident from Group Year Four, pays Ireland's twelve and a half per cent trading rate, below the floor. Who collects any Irish top-up depends on Irish law, which this paper does not examine, so the story leaves it aside.

Safe harbours.

A full GloBE computation for every territory, every year, is a heavy task. The law offers safe harbours, routes that treat a territory's top-up as nil without the full calculation.

The most used is the transitional country-by-country reporting safe harbour, in Schedule sixteen to the Pillar Two legislation. It borrows figures from the group's country-by-country report. A group may elect for it for accounting periods commencing on or before the thirty first of December, twenty twenty six, and ending on or before the thirtieth of June, twenty twenty eight. A territory passes if it meets any one of three tests. The de minimis test needs revenue under ten million euros and profit under one million euros. The simplified effective tax rate test compares tax with profit before tax and needs a rate of at least fifteen per cent for periods beginning before twenty twenty five, sixteen per cent for periods beginning in twenty twenty five, and seventeen per cent for periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six. The routine profits test is met if profit is no more than the substance-based income exclusion. If a group does not use the safe harbour for a territory in one year, it cannot return to it for that territory later. Once out, always out.

In real calendar terms, a group with a calendar year gets the harbour for twenty twenty six, at the seventeen per cent test, and that is its last year. A group whose year runs from July twenty twenty six to June twenty twenty seven also gets that year.

In our invented case we run the numbers on this year's rules. Vallaria, at twenty per cent, passes the seventeen per cent test where the harbour is available, and that saves the group a computation it would win anyway. Marrovia fails all three tests. Tarnmoor Capital's revenue, about six point two million euros, is under ten million, but its profit, nearly six million euros, is far above one million. Its rate is below seventeen per cent. And a finance company with a small office has almost no substance-based exclusion. So Marrovia always needs the full calculation.

Two other harbours are worth recognising. The transitional safe harbour for the undertaxed profits rule protected a parent's territory with a nominal corporate rate of at least twenty per cent, for periods of up to twelve months commencing by the end of twenty twenty five and ending before the thirty first of December, twenty twenty six. The qualified domestic minimum top-up tax safe harbour lets a group that pays an accredited domestic top-up tax in a territory treat the top-up there as nil under everyone else's rules, so one calculation does. The Finance Act twenty twenty six adds simplified calculations for members that are not material.

Registration, returns and a moving rulebook.

The administration is where an awareness question may well find its marks.

A qualifying group must register within six months after the end of its first accounting period as a qualifying group, even if no top-up tax is due. A filing member then files a GloBE information return within fifteen months after the end of each accounting period, or eighteen months for the first. No deadline falls before the thirtieth of June, twenty twenty six. If the information return is filed with a qualifying authority abroad, the United Kingdom member may notify that instead. The United Kingdom self-assessment return for the top-up taxes follows the same timetable. H M R C announced a transitional approach for the first wave. It would charge no late filing penalties on submissions made before the first of August, twenty twenty six.

In our invented case, Tarnmoor P L C is the filing member. For Group Year Five, which ends on the thirty first of December, its information return, its United Kingdom return and its payment of the one hundred and two thousand pounds are due by the thirty first of March, Group Year Seven, fifteen months on. In the group accounts the top-up is current tax, shown separately, with no deferred tax, as chapter six explained.

The rulebook keeps moving. The Finance Act twenty twenty six, in Schedule eight, makes a long list of changes. They include elective qualified domestic top-up taxes, rules for permanent establishments and for flow-through parents, a qualifying undertaxed profits tax, group relief payments counted as covered taxes, and the simplified calculations. The general start date is accounting periods beginning on or after the thirty first of December, twenty twenty five, with an election to apply them back to the start.

Coming next, and not law. In January twenty twenty six the O E C D released what is called the side-by-side package, designed, as its name suggests, to let some countries' own minimum tax systems operate alongside the GloBE rules. On the thirteenth of July, twenty twenty six, the government published draft legislation for the Finance Bill twenty twenty six to twenty seven to bring it in. The draft would apply to periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six, and includes elective safe harbours, one aimed at groups with United States parents. It is proposed, not law, and it is not examinable on the Finance Act twenty twenty six.

Diverted profits tax, and pay now, argue later.

Now the other half of the chapter. Diverted profits tax was in Part three of the Finance Act twenty fifteen. It applied to accounting periods beginning on or after the first of April, twenty fifteen. It had two limbs. The first caught a United Kingdom company whose arrangements with a related party involved entities or transactions lacking economic substance. The second caught a foreign company that arranged its affairs to avoid having a taxable presence in the United Kingdom, the avoided permanent establishment. The rate was twenty five per cent, set above corporation tax on purpose. For accounting periods beginning on or after the first of April, twenty twenty three, it rose to thirty one per cent, six points above the new main rate.

The design was as important as the rate. H M R C issued notices, and the company had to pay the tax before it could argue. Review and appeal came later. The aim was plain. A group facing an immediate cash bill at a premium rate had every reason to agree its transfer pricing on the ordinary corporation tax basis instead.

The design met its first test in the courts in twenty seventeen. H M R C issued Glencore Energy U K Limited with a charging notice of twenty one million, one hundred and twenty nine thousand, three hundred and forty nine pounds, plus interest, for its nine month accounting period to the thirty first of December, twenty fifteen. Glencore did not wait for the statutory review and appeal. It asked the High Court for judicial review of the notice.

The case is Glencore Energy U K Limited versus H M R C, decided by the High Court in twenty seventeen. Mr Justice Green refused permission. The statute provided its own route, a review and then an appeal to the First-tier Tribunal, and that was an adequate alternative remedy. Judicial review is a remedy of last resort. Glencore appealed, and later that year the Court of Appeal dismissed the appeal on the same ground. Judicial review would be right only in an exceptional case.

Glencore's lesson is the one the design intended. Pay now, argue later. The tax would be argued, if at all, after the money had moved.

The weakness lay elsewhere. A free standing tax, outside corporation tax, sat awkwardly with double tax treaties and the mutual agreement procedure between tax authorities. That is the problem the replacement solves.

From diverted profits tax to unassessed transfer pricing profits.

Section forty six of the Finance Act twenty twenty six, with Schedule five, repealed diverted profits tax for accounting periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six. In its place is a new Part four A of the Taxation, International and Other Provisions, Act twenty ten, which I will call the International Act, running from section two hundred and seventeen A to section two hundred and seventeen T.

Watch the commencement, in real calendar terms. A company with a calendar year is under the new rules for twenty twenty six. A company whose year ran from the first of April, twenty twenty five, to the thirty first of March, twenty twenty six, stays under diverted profits tax for that whole year, and moves to the new rules from the first of April, twenty twenty six. Old periods keep the old tax, so an enquiry into an earlier year can still involve diverted profits tax, and an adviser must check the period before naming the charge.

Which brings us to the second misconception of the chapter. Diverted profits tax still exists. For current periods it does not. For any accounting period beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six, the answer is unassessed transfer pricing profits, and on a paper examined on the Finance Act twenty twenty six, that is the name to use.

What does the new charge tax? Unassessed transfer pricing profits are profits that a transfer pricing requirement brings into charge, but that the company has not reflected in its own return. Chapter twenty seven explains the transfer pricing rules themselves. The new charge has no measure of its own. It borrows the arm's length price, and taxes the gap between that price and the return. The charge is corporation tax, so the government's documents say treaties and the mutual agreement procedure apply to it. The rate is the underlying corporation tax rate plus six per cent. At the main rate of twenty five per cent, that is thirty one per cent. Diverted profits tax's separate notification duty has not been kept.

And the avoided permanent establishment limb? It has no direct successor in the new Part. On this book's reading, that work now falls to the rewritten definition of a permanent establishment in the Finance Act twenty twenty six, with its principal role test for dependent agents, which chapter twenty three explains. The government has not put it in exactly those words.

Three gates.

Unassessed transfer pricing profits are not charged at the premium rate just because a price was wrong. Three gates must all open.

The first is an effective tax mismatch outcome. The underlying corporation tax amount is United Kingdom tax, at the underlying rate, on the profit the company should have reported. The corresponding amount is the tax the other party pays on the matching profit. The gate opens only if the corresponding amount is less than eighty per cent of the underlying amount. H M R C's manual gives an example. A United Kingdom company pays twenty million pounds for services worth fifteen million. The unassessed profit is five million pounds, and United Kingdom tax on it would be one point two five million. The overseas company pays one million pounds of tax on the same five million. That is exactly eighty per cent, so there is no mismatch.

The second gate is the tax design condition. It is met if it is reasonable to assume that the arrangements were designed to reduce, eliminate or delay United Kingdom tax. H M R C's manual says this is not the main purpose test found elsewhere, and that the charge will not apply merely because a group moves a whole activity to a lower tax country. The target is separating United Kingdom activity from the income it produces.

The third gate is that the profits must not arise wholly from excepted loan relationship arrangements, meaning loans and the derivatives that hedge them. H M R C's guidance adds that a loan inside a wider arrangement does not automatically qualify.

For this example, suppose a United Kingdom distributor in another group overpays a royalty by four million pounds to a related company taxed at five per cent. United Kingdom tax on four million would be one million pounds. The other company pays two hundred thousand, only twenty per cent of that. The first gate opens. If the design condition is also met, the charge at thirty one per cent is one point two four million pounds. Had the company corrected the price in its own return, it would have paid one million. The premium is two hundred and forty thousand pounds.

Now Tarnmoor. The invented Vallarian rate of twenty per cent is exactly eighty per cent of the United Kingdom's twenty five. So on the story's simplified figures, a United Kingdom to Vallaria provision never opens the first gate. Calder's sale of its process valve business to Tarnmoor Vallaria in Group Year Three is the test. When the price was later raised by two million pounds, as chapter twenty seven tells, United Kingdom tax on the increase was five hundred thousand pounds, and Vallarian tax on the matching profit four hundred thousand. Eighty per cent, not less. And the sale moved a real business in any case. Marrovia is different. Nine per cent is only thirty six per cent of twenty five, so a Marrovian provision would open the first gate. The one United Kingdom to Marrovia deal ever proposed, Project Undertow, was a loan, and the board rejected it anyway.

Pay first, and what the examiner wants.

The procedure keeps the old pay first design, now inside corporation tax.

A designated H M R C officer may give a preliminary notice no later than four years after the end of the accounting period. The company has thirty days to make representations, and only on limited grounds. The officer then has sixty days from the preliminary notice to make an assessment, or thirty days after a closure notice if that is later. Then comes a fifteen month period for amendments. During it the company may amend its return to bring the profits into ordinary corporation tax, though not in the last twenty one days, and H M R C may correct or increase the assessment.

The company must pay. Postponement is allowed only for tax equal to tax already paid on the corresponding profits. It may appeal only after paying and after the period for amendments, within thirty days. The tribunal may reduce the charge to the ordinary rate if a gate was not in fact open. And no relief, deduction or set-off of any description is allowed against the profits or the tax.

The lesson for a tax function is simple. The charge is a lever, not a destination. Correct transfer pricing in the company's own return removes the exposure, and even after a notice, an amendment brings the profits back to the ordinary rate.

So Tarnmoor's tax function, led in the story by Tom Hesketh, keeps two lists. For unassessed transfer pricing profits, it lists every cross-border provision with a counterparty taxed at less than eighty per cent of the United Kingdom rate, and makes sure the transfer pricing documentation supports the price in the return. For Pillar Two, it gathers data from every entity well before the fifteen month deadline, decides each year, territory by territory, whether a safe harbour election is available and wanted, and reconciles the C F C charge with the top-up so nothing is counted twice.

In the exam, these topics are awareness level, grade three. None was examined between May twenty twenty three and May twenty twenty six. At awareness level the marks go to recognition, a sentence or two showing the rule exists and when it applies.

Diverted profits tax has a history on this paper, as a trap. The examiners reported, after the May twenty twenty three sitting, that time was wasted on diverted profits tax in a question about a loan, because it does not apply to loan relationships. The same discipline holds now. For any period beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six, say unassessed transfer pricing profits, not diverted profits tax. And do not raise it unless the question gives you a low-taxed related party, a pricing problem and a hint of design.

For Pillar Two, the likely marks are the threshold and its two of four test, which tax applies to which entity, registration within six months, the fifteen month return, and the accounting exception. Do not attempt a full GloBE computation unless the requirement asks for one.

What to take away.

Pillar Two puts a fifteen per cent floor under the profits of large multinational groups, measured territory by territory. More than one hundred and thirty five countries agreed it in October twenty twenty one, and the United Kingdom enacted it in the Finance Number Two Act twenty twenty three.

Multinational top-up tax, from the thirty first of December, twenty twenty three, makes a United Kingdom parent pay top-up on low taxed members abroad through the income inclusion rule, with the undertaxed profits rule as backstop from a year later. Domestic top-up tax collects any top-up on United Kingdom profits first.

A group is in if consolidated revenue exceeds seven hundred and fifty million euros in at least two of the previous four periods. The statute says exceeds. Some H M R C guidance says or more.

The effective rate is covered taxes over GloBE income for each territory. The top-up percentage is fifteen per cent less that rate, applied to profit after the substance-based income exclusion.

In our invented case, Marrovia's nine per cent plus the pushed down C F C charge of two hundred and four thousand pounds gives thirteen per cent on five point one million pounds in Group Year Five. Tarnmoor P L C pays one hundred and two thousand pounds of top-up tax. Everything together comes to exactly fifteen per cent.

The transitional country-by-country safe harbour runs out with periods commencing in twenty twenty six, when its simplified rate test is seventeen per cent. Once out, always out.

Register within six months of the end of the first qualifying period. File the information return and the United Kingdom return within fifteen months, eighteen for the first. The side-by-side package is proposed, not law.

Diverted profits tax ran from the first of April, twenty fifteen, at twenty five per cent and later thirty one per cent, and a pay now, argue later design that survived the Glencore challenge. For accounting periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six, it is repealed. Unassessed transfer pricing profits are charged as corporation tax at thirty one per cent, the main rate plus six, through three gates. The other party's tax is under eighty per cent of the United Kingdom tax, the design condition is met, and the profits do not arise wholly from excepted loan arrangements. Pay first. No reliefs. Correct pricing in the return removes the charge.

So, who collects the difference? The country where the profit arises if it chooses to, then the parent's country, then everyone else. And what stops profit leaving through mispricing? Transfer pricing, backed now by a premium rate of corporation tax that the group can avoid simply by getting the price right.

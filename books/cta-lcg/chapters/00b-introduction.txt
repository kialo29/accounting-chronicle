Introduction.

The prologue followed one loan through three courts and found two answers. Keep that picture in mind, because it is a fair picture of the ground ahead. A larger company is not a small company with more noughts on the end. It borrows from its own relatives. It owns companies that own companies. It trades across borders, reports to investors, and answers to a board that must publish its approach to tax. Almost every rule you met in The Living Law still applies to it. But around those rules sits a second layer of law, written for scale.

This introduction is a map. It sets out the four questions the book keeps asking, the paper the book serves, the way the chapters are arranged, the invented group whose story carries the law, and how the two editions of each chapter work. It ends with some advice on using the book alongside the question practice you already have. The law throughout is the law for the financial year twenty twenty six, under the Finance Act twenty twenty six.

Five ways the larger company is different.

The first difference is scale. Size switches rules on. A company with profits above one and a half million pounds pays its tax in instalments, and above twenty million pounds it pays even earlier, both thresholds shared out among the companies it is associated with. A group with net interest above two million pounds a year meets the corporate interest restriction. Above two hundred million pounds of United Kingdom turnover, or two billion pounds of balance sheet, a group needs a senior accounting officer, a published tax strategy and a habit of telling H M R C, His Majesty's Revenue and Customs, about uncertain positions. Above seven hundred and fifty million euros of consolidated revenue come country-by-country reports, transfer pricing records and the global minimum tax. Chapter one sets out that ladder, rung by rung.

The second difference is the group. United Kingdom law taxes each company separately. There is no group return for corporation tax. Yet a group plans, borrows and invests as one business, and the law builds bridges between its companies, with group relief for losses, tax-free transfers of assets, a shared allowance here and a shared threshold there. Each bridge has its own entry test, and the tests do not match.

The third difference is the border. A large group earns profits in many countries and moves money, goods, people and ideas between them. Most of the second half of this book is about the rules that decide where those profits are taxed, and the rules that stop them drifting to wherever tax is lowest.

The fourth difference is governance. A large company's tax is not only computed. It is managed, certified and reported. H M R C's large business directorate says it works with around two thousand of the largest businesses, and its customer compliance managers assess each group's tax risk and how well the group manages it. The law adds personal duties for a named officer and a criminal offence for failing to prevent the facilitation of tax evasion.

The fifth difference is the accounts. For a larger company the accounts are not a starting point that tax quickly leaves behind. They decide the timing of loan relationship profits, intangible asset profits and much else. And the accounts must also show a tax charge that investors read, current and deferred, which is a different number from the tax bill.

Four questions.

Those five differences produce four questions, and the book asks them in every chapter.

The first is this. Is a group one taxpayer or many? The law taxes each company on its own profits, yet it lets losses travel, assets move and gains be rolled over across the group. Where does it build those bridges, and where does it refuse? Chapters one, fifteen and seventeen carry this question furthest, and the interest restriction and the global minimum tax push it in a new direction.

The second is this. Where are profits made, and what stops them drifting? Residence, permanent establishments, treaties and the foreign branch exemption decide where profits are taxed. Transfer pricing, controlled foreign companies, hybrid mismatches, the interest restriction and the global minimum tax stop them moving to where they are not. Part Four of the book is built around this question.

The third is this. What does the law follow? The accounts, the deal, or its own purpose? Tax starts from the accounts. It respects many commercial transactions, such as share exchanges and demergers, on their own terms. And then, again and again, it asks why a company did something, and overrides both the accounts and the deal when the answer is tax. The prologue's loan was the first example. It will not be the last.

The fourth is this. How does a large group govern its tax, and what must an adviser spot? The Chartered Institute of Taxation says that candidates for this paper are not expected to have deep knowledge of all tax areas but should be able to identify what the potential tax issues are. So every chapter asks what the tax function must do, by when, and what a good adviser would flag.

The paper this book serves.

This book is written for the Chartered Tax Adviser Advanced Technical paper, Taxation of Larger Companies and Groups. Until twenty twenty three it was called Taxation of Major Corporates. The paper lasts three and a half hours. It normally has six questions, each worth ten, fifteen or twenty marks, and one hundred marks in total. The pass mark is fifty per cent.

The Institute describes its scenarios plainly. They will typically be based around multinational groups and larger companies that may be listed, with no individual controlling shareholders. So owner-managed companies, close company loans and profit extraction are not this paper's business, and they are not this book's either.

The syllabus has three elements. Corporation tax, including corporation tax on chargeable gains. Capital allowances. And stamp duty and stamp duty land tax, at awareness level. Within the corporation tax element, at least seventy per cent of the marks come from core material.

Core is a grade. The Institute's syllabus grid gives every topic a grade. Grade one is core, examined at depth, with computations. Grade two is non-core, where you need working knowledge and the main computation. Grade three is awareness, where you need to recognise the rule and state it. This book gives core topics full depth, non-core topics a working treatment, and awareness topics their principles. Each chapter's reading edition says which grade applies to what.

The grid this book follows is the twenty twenty six grid. As this book was finished, in October twenty twenty six, the Institute had not yet published a grid, a prospectus or tax tables for twenty twenty seven. The settled pattern is that the exams in one year test the Finance Act of the year before, so a sitting in twenty twenty seven should test the Finance Act twenty twenty six. The likely sitting is the fourth of May, twenty twenty seven, with the twenty sixth of October, twenty twenty seven, as the fallback. When the twenty twenty seven grid and tables appear, check them against what this book says.

The book also follows the paper's habits. Between May twenty twenty three and May twenty twenty six, every sitting had a corporation tax computation worth fifteen or twenty marks. Capital allowances appeared in nearly every paper. Groups and consortia came six times. Property gains and leases, and the shareholding exemption with reorganisations, came five times each. International questions, on residence, permanent establishments, the branch exemption or migration, have come at every sitting since November twenty twenty four. Controlled foreign companies came four times, transfer pricing five times, the interest restriction four times, and compliance and governance eight times. The longest chapters in this book sit where the marks have been.

How the book is built.

After this introduction come thirty two chapters in five Parts, then a conclusion and a note on sources.

Part One, The Larger Company, has four chapters. They ask what makes a company large and who counts as its group, where corporate tax law lives, how returns, payments and enquiries work for a large group, and how the law governs a group's tax through officers, strategies, disclosures and the anti-avoidance rules.

Part Two, Measuring Profit, has ten chapters. It starts with the accounts and deferred tax, builds the large company computation line by line, and then opens each line in turn. Plant and machinery. Buildings, fixtures, leasing and successions. Research and development. Intangible assets and intellectual property. Loan relationships and derivatives. Companies with investment business. And losses.

Part Three, The Group, has seven chapters. Group relief, consortia and joint ventures. Company gains on property and leases. Gains inside the group. Shares, the shareholding exemption, reorganisations and earn-outs. Reconstructions, demergers and distributions. Buying and selling companies. And stamp taxes for groups.

Part Four, Across Borders, has nine chapters. Where a company lives and how it leaves. Non-resident companies, permanent establishments and withholding. Treaties and double tax relief. The choice between a foreign branch and a subsidiary. Controlled foreign companies. Transfer pricing and advance pricing agreements. The corporate interest restriction. Hybrid mismatches. And the global minimum tax, with the end of diverted profits tax.

Part Five, Putting It Together, has two chapters. Chapter thirty one follows one acquisition through every relevant rule, the way a long examination question does. Chapter thirty two is about the craft of the paper itself, from the exam software to the layouts the examiners expect.

Where The Living Law ends.

This book assumes that you know The Living Law, the earlier book written for the Tax Knowledge and Skills paper. It does not teach those foundations twice. Where a chapter builds on one, it recaps it in one to three sentences and names the chapter, for example The Living Law, chapter twenty four, on groups. Then it goes further.

Going further means two things. Some topics The Living Law touched, such as group relief, loan relationships, research and development, company gains and the foreign branch exemption, are taught again here in full, because the Advanced Technical examiner expects mastery, not recall. And some topics The Living Law only signposted are taught here from first principles. Transfer pricing, controlled foreign companies, the interest restriction, hybrid mismatches, the global minimum tax, company migration, deferred tax and demergers all belong in that second group.

Tarnmoor, an invented group.

Most chapters follow one group of companies, Tarnmoor. Everything about Tarnmoor is invented for teaching. Its companies, its people, its numbers and its two overseas countries, Vallaria and Marrovia, are all made up. Ireland is real, and so are its twelve and a half per cent trading rate and its treaty with the United Kingdom, but Tarnmoor's Irish company is not.

Tarnmoor P L C, a public limited company, is a listed engineering group with its head office in Leeds and no controlling shareholder. It was built to match the examiner's description of a typical scenario, and to be large enough to switch on every regime the paper tests. Its main trading company makes industrial valves, actuators and pumps. Around it sit a property company, a treasury company, a joint venture, overseas subsidiaries, and the companies it buys and sells as the story runs.

The story begins in chapter one, when Tarnmoor buys Calder Valve Engineering for thirty two million pounds. If you have read The Living Law, you know Calder. It is the invented valve maker where Dan Hartley worked as an engineer, and his story continues here. So does his sister Jess's. Some years after the rest of the group's story, Tarnmoor buys her business, Ridgeway, and chapter thirty one tells that deal.

Tarnmoor lives in Group Years, not calendar years. Group Years are Tarnmoor's accounting years to the thirty first of December. Group Year One is the year in which Calder joins, Group Year Two the next, and so on. No calendar year is ever attached to a Tarnmoor event, and every Tarnmoor computation applies the law for the financial year twenty twenty six, whichever Group Year the story has reached. There is a reason for that. Real rules start on real dates, such as accounting periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six. Where a chapter needs to teach a real commencement date, it uses a labelled example with real dates, outside the story. That keeps the story simple and the dates honest.

The story is the spine of the book, not its cage. When the examiner tests a variation the story never reaches, a chapter will say suppose, and work a labelled example. Those examples never become part of the story.

Two editions.

Every chapter comes in two editions.

The audio script is written to be heard once. Numbers are spoken as words, and worked examples are told as stories with a few memorable figures. Statutes are named in full the first time in each chapter and then given a short name. The Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine becomes the two thousand and nine Act, and the Corporation Tax Act twenty ten becomes the twenty ten Act. The Taxation, International and Other Provisions, Act twenty ten becomes the International Act. The Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act nineteen ninety two becomes the Gains Act. Long section numbers are said the way practitioners say them, so section eleven forty one, or section one seven one for no gain, no loss transfers, or section four four one for unallowable purpose. Abbreviations are spelled out first, and then spoken as separate letters where practitioners use letters, such as H M R C, or C F C for a controlled foreign company. Each script ends with a section called What to take away, a spoken recap of the rules and numbers to keep.

The reading edition is the same chapter for the page. It has digits, statutory references and case citations in their standard form, and it lays out every computation the way the examiner expects, with one unit in each table. It adds three kinds of box. A worked example box gives the full numbers behind each story. A going further box covers planning, the tax function's tasks and deadlines, and the interaction between regimes. And an exam lens box gives each topic's syllabus grade, its past appearances on the paper, what the examiners said, and the traps. Each reading edition ends with a table of key rules and figures and a list of statutory and case references.

Both editions use the same words for the status of a claim. The law says means the statute. The courts have held means case law. H M R C's view is means guidance, which is not law. Disputed, or being litigated, means what it says. Announced means a government statement. Proposed, not law, means a consultation or draft legislation that Parliament has not passed. And in our invented case means Tarnmoor.

How to use this book.

The book teaches. It does not test. There are no practice questions here, because you have the Institute's past papers and your own question bank, and those are the right tools for practice.

A good rhythm is this. Listen to a chapter for the story and the reasons. Then read its reading edition with a pen, working through each computation yourself. Then sit a past question on the same topic, under time, and mark it against the Institute's suggested answer and marking guide. Read the examiners' report for that sitting. From May twenty twenty four the Institute has also published example scripts, banded by mark. Comparing a scraped pass with a strong answer to the same question is one of the best lessons available, and chapter thirty two shows what to look for.

Practise on the student legislation product you will use on the day, Croner-i or Tolley, because in the exam the legislation is your only reference. And practise with the right numbers. The twenty twenty six tax tables, the only ones published as this book was finished, reflect the Finance Act twenty twenty five, with a main pool writing down allowance of eighteen per cent. The law this book teaches, and the law for a twenty twenty seven sitting, has fourteen per cent and a new forty per cent first-year allowance. When the twenty twenty seven tables appear, check every rate against them.

Finally, keep the status words in your ear. Several things described in this book are proposals, not law. A draft Finance Bill was published in July twenty twenty six, and a Budget is due on the twenty eighth of October, twenty twenty six. None of that changes the law for your sitting. The conclusion says what may be coming, and the note on sources says how to keep up.

So the book starts where Tarnmoor's story starts, on the first of April in Group Year One, with a family firm that becomes one company among many.

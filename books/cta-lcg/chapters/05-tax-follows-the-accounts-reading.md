# Part Two: Measuring Profit.

# Chapter five: Tax follows the accounts.

In 2022 the Supreme Court decided a case about money that never moved. A group of companies had recorded a large expense in their accounts for share options granted to their staff. No cash left the companies. The options came from a trust set up by the parent. Yet the accounting standard (IFRS 2) said the staff's services had been consumed, and their value had to be charged as an expense.

HMRC said the tax computation should ignore that charge. It argued four separate grounds. It lost on every one. The case was *HMRC v NCL Investments Ltd* [2022] UKSC 9, and the judgment was given by Lord Hamblen and Lady Rose, with whom Lord Reed, Lord Briggs and Lord Sales agreed.

So here is this chapter's question. When the accountants change their minds, or their rulebook, does the tax bill change too? And if it does, who decides when the law should step in and say no?

This chapter opens Part Two, on measuring profit. It sits underneath almost every computation the Advanced Technical examiner sets. The impact of accounting standards on taxable profits is **core (grade 1)** in the LCG syllabus, and so are the **general calculation rules in Part 20 CTA 2009**. *The Living Law* (TKS), chapter 11, taught the adjustment of profits and the line between capital and revenue. This chapter asks why the starting point is the accounts at all, and what happens when the accounts themselves change. Everything here is **FY2026 law** (1 April 2026 to 31 March 2027) under Finance Act 2026.

---

## The shadow and the walker.

Start with the statute. **CTA 2009 s 46(1)**: the profits of a trade "must be calculated in accordance with generally accepted accounting practice, subject to any adjustment required or authorised by law".

That one sentence carries two ideas. Tax begins with the accountant's number; and the law may overrule it, but only where a rule says so. Section 46(2) adds that there is no requirement to comply with company law on accounts except as to the basis of calculation. The tax computation borrows the method, not the paperwork.

Why build tax on the accounts? Three reasons:
- **Cost.** Accounts already exist; a second, separate measurement of profit would double the compliance cost for every company.
- **Discipline.** Large companies' accounts are audited and must give a true and fair view.
- **Expertise.** Accounting standards are written by people whose job is to measure profit over a period, which is exactly the question tax needs answered.

The courts reached the same view long before Parliament wrote it down. In *Odeon Associated Theatres Ltd v Jones* (1971) 48 TC 257 (CA), the issue was the cost of catching up on deferred repairs to cinemas the company had acquired. HMRC's manual (BIM31095) summarises Salmon LJ's approach as two steps: follow the ordinary principles of commercial accounting so far as practicable, then check that no rule of tax law is violated.

Two later cases pushed further. In *Gallagher v Jones* (1993) 66 TC 77 (CA; [1994] Ch 107), a taxpayer leased boats with heavily front-loaded rents; the Court of Appeal held that the rents should be spread across the lease as the accounts spread them, not deducted when paid. In *Johnston v Britannia Airways Ltd* (1994) 67 TC 99 (Knox J), an airline provided each year for the future cost of overhauling its jet engines (certification required an overhaul after 17,000 hours); the provision was accepted for tax because it reflected accepted accounting practice.

Here is an image to hold. Tax follows the accounts the way a shadow follows a walker. Wherever the accountant goes, the taxable profit goes too. But at certain spots Parliament has nailed the shadow to the floor: the walker moves on, and the shadow stays put. Those nails are the adjustments "required or authorised by law".

The precise rule matters more than the image. Accounting practice decides **when** income and expense are recognised and **how** they are measured. It does not decide whether an item is capital or revenue for tax, whether an expense is wholly and exclusively for the trade (s 54), or whether a specific statute blocks it. Those are questions of law. Accounting evidence is relevant to them, often persuasive, never conclusive.

## What counts as generally accepted.

GAAP has a statutory meaning: **CTA 2010 s 1127**. In outline, UK GAAP means practice for UK companies' accounts intended to give a true and fair view; for companies preparing IAS accounts, GAAP means international accounting standards.

A large group meets three rulebooks:

| Framework | What it is | Typical user in a listed group |
|---|---|---|
| IFRS (international accounting standards) | Issued by the IASB | Consolidated accounts of every listed group |
| **FRS 101** (reduced disclosure framework) | UK GAAP: IFRS recognition and measurement with reduced disclosures | UK subsidiaries of IFRS groups |
| **FRS 102** | The main UK standard, originally based on the IFRS for SMEs; amended by the FRC's Periodic Review 2024 (periods beginning on or after 1 January 2026) | Independent companies; some subsidiaries |

The FRC's standards run from FRS 100 to FRS 105 (HMRC BIM31020, CFM20030). For tax, the distinction matters because recognition and measurement drive the numbers: a company on FRS 101 usually recognises income and expense at the same time as its parent's group accounts.

Our invented group, **Tarnmoor**, shows the pattern. Tarnmoor plc is a listed engineering group, invented for this book; it prepares consolidated accounts under IFRS, and its group policy (invented) is that every UK subsidiary reports under FRS 101, so each subsidiary's numbers feed into the group accounts without a second set of adjustments.

**Mixed GAAP in a group (CTA 2010 s 996).** A group that mixes rulebooks can create a tax gap. Suppose one company uses IFRS and its sister uses FRS 102, and they transact with each other: one standard makes the seller recognise income later, the other lets the buyer take the expense earlier, and across the group the deduction runs ahead of the taxable income. Section 996 closes that gap: where two companies in a group use different frameworks and the difference gives a tax advantage on a transaction between them, **both are treated as using UK GAAP**. The rule does not ban mixed frameworks; it removes the advantage that mixing would create.

## Where Parliament nails the shadow down.

The adjustments required by law come in layers.

**Layer 1: case law and general statute** (TKS chapter 11 recap). Capital expenditure is not deductible (CTA 2009 s 53); expenses not incurred wholly and exclusively for the trade are not deductible (s 54); depreciation is replaced by capital allowances.

**Layer 2: Part 20 CTA 2009, "General calculation rules" (ss 1288–1309), grade 1.** It reads like a list of the places where the accounts are not trusted:

| Section (CTA 2009) | Rule | Owned by |
|---|---|---|
| s 1288 | Employees' remuneration unpaid 9 months after the period end: deductible only when paid | Ch 7 |
| ss 1290–1297 | Employee benefit contributions: qualifying benefits within 9 months; 5-year cut-off; tax and NIC within 12 months | Ch 7 |
| s 1298 (exceptions ss 1299–1300) | Business entertainment and gifts | Ch 7 |
| s 1301B | No deduction for qualifying charitable donations in computing income (relief instead against total profits, CTA 2010 Part 6) | Ch 7 |
| s 1302 | Social security contributions | — |
| s 1303 | Penalties and interest on tax | Ch 7 |
| s 1304 | Crime-related payments | Ch 7 |
| s 1305 | Dividends and other distributions | Ch 19 |
| s 1305A | Transfer of corporate profits | — |
| **s 1305B (new, FA 2026 s 63)** | Non-derecognition liabilities | this chapter |

**Section 1305B** is the newest nail. A company transfers assets to a securitisation company (or a party to the same capital market arrangement) but the assets stay on the transferor group's balance sheet because the risks never left; the company shows a liability alongside the asset. Deductions for losses, expenses or debits on that liability are denied, on a just and reasonable apportionment, where **a main purpose of any party** is a tax advantage. It has effect for **accounting periods beginning on or after 26 November 2025** (Budget day), with straddling periods split. Awareness-level detail; the point for the exam is that Part 20 is still growing.

**Layer 3: statutory tests that override correct accounts.** *GDF Suez Teesside Ltd v HMRC* [2018] EWCA Civ 2075. The company (formerly Teesside Power, which had operated the Teesside power station) held claims against companies in the collapsed Enron group and transferred them to a non-resident subsidiary in exchange for shares. Its accounts complied with GAAP, and on that basis the transfer produced no taxable credit reflecting the claims' value. The loan relationship rules then in force (FA 1996 s 84(1)) required the accounting to "fairly represent" the company's profits and losses. The Court of Appeal held that requirement could override GAAP-compliant accounts: a credit equal to the claims' value had to be brought in, and the appeal was dismissed.

The lesson is not that accounts can be ignored at will. "Fairly represents" was itself a statutory adjustment. When the loan relationship rules were reformed from 2016 (F(No.2)A 2015), Parliament moved them onto a simpler footing (amounts in profit or loss: see below; CFM76010). The principle survived: tax follows the accounts unless a rule of law says otherwise, and the rule must be found, not assumed.

## A debit with no cash.

In *NCL Investments*, the companies employed staff and made them available to other group companies for a fee. The parent's employee benefit trust granted options over the parent's shares to those staff (accounting periods to 30 April 2010, 2011 and 2012). IFRS 2 required the companies to charge the fair value of the options as an expense, because the staff's services had been consumed in return for them. The matching credit went to equity as a capital contribution from the parent.

HMRC argued four grounds:
1. an adjustment was **required by law** under s 46;
2. the expense was not **wholly and exclusively** for the trade (s 54);
3. the debits were **capital** (s 53);
4. the **employee benefit contribution** rules (s 1290) blocked them.

The First-tier Tribunal, the Upper Tribunal, the Court of Appeal and the Supreme Court rejected every argument. The debits were properly computed under GAAP; they reflected a real cost of the trade (employees' services consumed); no statute required their removal. In the Supreme Court's words: "What matters is the character of the Debits, not that of any corresponding credit". A capital credit to equity did not turn a revenue expense into a capital one.

The case is a warning to anyone who thinks a deduction needs a cash payment. Under s 46 it needs an accounting debit the law does not reject, and the burden is on the person who says the accounts should be overridden to find the rule.

**The exam twist.** NCL concerned periods before **FA 2013 s 40** substituted **CTA 2009 s 1038** and inserted **s 1038A**. For shares and options within Part 12 (employee share acquisition relief), s 1038 now blocks a deduction for the IFRS 2 (or FRS 102 s 26) charge itself; the company gets the statutory Part 12 deduction instead (broadly market value less consideration, in the period the employee acquires the shares; lapsed options nothing, s 1038A). Chapter 7 owns that relief. The point here is method: the courts followed the accounts, and when Parliament wanted a different answer, it legislated.

## Loan relationships and derivatives follow profit or loss.

The loan relationship rules apply the same philosophy with a sharper edge (chapter 12 owns the regimes):

| Rule | Reference |
|---|---|
| Amounts brought into account are those recognised in determining profit or loss under GAAP | CTA 2009 s 307(2), s 308(1) |
| Amounts in OCI are taxed only when recycled into profit or loss | s 308(1A) |
| Same for derivative contracts | ss 595, 597 |
| Any GAAP-compliant basis may normally be used | s 313(1) |
| Connected companies relationships: amortised cost compulsory, even if the accounts fair value | s 349 |
| Certain fair value movements on hedging derivatives left out, since 2015 only by election or in automatic cases | Disregard Regulations, SI 2004/3256 regs 6A–9 |

An entry made directly in **equity** is not an amount recognised in profit or loss at all. *Union Castle Mail Steamship Co Ltd v HMRC* [2020] EWCA Civ 547, decided under the older derivative rules, concerned a scheme that tried to turn an equity entry on derivatives (the derecognition of FTSE put options, debited to equity) into a £39.1m tax debit; the Court of Appeal held the amount did not count, and the appeals (with Ladbrokes') were dismissed. Today's profit-or-loss focus makes that kind of scheme harder still.

In our invented group, Tarnmoor Finance Ltd hedges its £550m listed notes with an interest rate swap designated as a fair value hedge. Since 2015 the Disregard Regulations' hedging rules (regs 7–9) apply only by election (reg 6A) or in the automatic cases HMRC lists; TFL has made no election and, on HMRC's guidance, because its notes are themselves taxed in line with the accounts, the swap simply follows profit or loss (chapter 12).

## Provisions, present obligations and the general reserve.

A provision is an accountant's estimate of a cost the business has become committed to but not yet paid. It is where the shadow most often parts company with the cash, so it is where HMRC looks hardest.

**HMRC's view (BIM46510): a provision is deductible if**
1. it is made in accordance with GAAP;
2. it relates to revenue, not capital, expenditure;
3. it does not conflict with a statutory rule about when the expense is allowed (BIM46565: for example s 1288 unpaid remuneration; FA 2004 s 196 pension contributions, deductible when paid); and
4. it is estimated with sufficient accuracy (BIM46555).

**The accounting test now has teeth.** IAS 37 and FRS 102 Section 21 allow a provision only where there is a present obligation from a past event, an outflow is probable, and the amount can be reliably estimated. HMRC's manual (BIM46550) points out that today's standards would **not** allow *Britannia Airways*' provision for overhauling engines on aircraft the company owns: no present obligation exists until the work is done. The tax law has not changed; the accounts have, and the tax has followed them.

**The capital test** catches provisions for costs that would themselves be capital (a write-down of plant; demolition of a building). **The timing test** catches provisions a statute overrides (unpaid pay beyond 9 months; pensions on a paid basis). **The accuracy test** catches round-sum guesses (*Southern Railway of Peru* is the classic authority on imprecise provisions [S, via secondary commentary]).

**General versus specific bad debt provisions.** A general provision (a reserve against unidentified customers' default, often a flat percentage of debtors) is not an estimate of any particular loss, so it is not deductible. A specific impairment of a named customer's debt, properly estimated, is deductible. For companies, trade debts are money debts and impairment losses on them come in through the loan relationship rules (CTA 2009 Part 6 Ch 2, relevant non-lending relationships; HMRC BIM42701 notes that companies' bad trade debts are dealt with under the loan relationship rules; CTA 2009 s 55 now deals only with non-money debts). Modern expected credit loss accounting (IFRS 9) blurs the old general/specific line; that debate is beyond the exam. **In an exam question, follow the examiner's label.**

> **Worked example: the direction of a general provision (illustrative, not story)**
>
> For this example, suppose a company's general bad debt provision falls from £50,000 to £30,000. The accounts show a £20,000 **credit** in profit. Because the increases were never deductible, the release is not taxable: **deduct £20,000** in the computation. Had the provision risen by £20,000, you would **add back** £20,000.

### Calder's restructuring provision (GY3)

Now a moment from our invented case. On **15 June GY3**, Calder Valve Engineering Ltd (the regional manufacturer *The Living Law* followed, invented for that book, and by now a Tarnmoor subsidiary reporting under FRS 101) announces the closure of its process valves division. The announcement gives staff a valid expectation that the closure will happen: under IAS 37 a constructive obligation, so a provision is recognised.

> **Worked example: Calder's restructuring provision, 9-month AP 1 April to 31 December GY3 (invented)**
>
> | Component | Charged in AP (£) | Paid by 31 Dec GY3 (£) | Provided at 31 Dec GY3 (£) |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Redundancy and notice pay (including Dan Hartley's package) | 1,200,000 | 1,000,000 | 200,000 (paid March GY4) |
> | Onerous supply contract (castings no longer usable) | 400,000 | 200,000 | 200,000 |
> | Outplacement and retraining | 200,000 | 100,000 | 100,000 |
> | **Total** | **1,800,000** | **1,300,000** | **500,000** |
>
> **Tax analysis (Tom Hesketh, Tarnmoor's head of tax; invented):**
> - GAAP-compliant (IAS 37 via FRS 101): yes.
> - Revenue, not capital: yes; Calder's trade continues in its other divisions, so redundancy costs are ordinary trading expenses; no asset write-down is included (the process-valve plant is sold to TVS on 1 October GY3 at £1.4m: a capital allowances disposal, chapters 8 and 11).
> - Statutory timing rules: the redundancy pay still owed is paid in March GY4, within 9 months of 31 December GY3, so s 1288 cannot bite whatever its scope; Dan's £10,000 pension contribution was paid on 30 September GY3 (FA 2004 s 196: paid basis satisfied).
> - Accuracy: specific obligations, carefully estimated.
>
> **Result: the whole £1,800,000 is deductible in Calder's 9-month AP to 31 December GY3.**

Graham Pike (Calder's finance director, invented) asked whether the £500,000 still provided was deductible now. On HMRC's four tests, yes. Dan Hartley's package (statutory £9,012; ex gratia £60,000 of which £10,000 into his pension; PENP £25,000), told from his side in TKS chapter 9, is part of the redundancy line (his holiday pay of £3,800 is ordinary pay, outside the provision); chapter 7 deals with the employer's deduction for each element.

> **Exam lens: provisions and impairments**
> - **Grade:** impact of accounting standards **1**; Part 20 **1**.
> - **Past appearances:** M23 Q1 (20 marks): a **general bad debt provision** movement was a credit, not a debit; the examiners reported this as the common mistake in an otherwise well answered computation. N24 Q4 (20 marks): impairments of a trade debt (deductible) versus a loan to a fellow subsidiary (connected loan relationship: no debit, CTA 2009 s 354). M25 Q2 (15 marks): impairment of **land** (capital: add back). N23 Q4 (20 marks): **capitalised revenue expenditure** handled badly (it should be deducted unless capital allowances apply or it is truly capital).
> - **Traps:** the direction of a provision movement; treating a capital write-down as deductible; forgetting that a specific timing rule (s 1288, FA 2004 s 196) overrides an accrual.
> - **Layout:** start from profit before tax; one line per adjustment with the reason and authority; £ or £000 throughout, never both.

## When the accountants change their minds.

Accounting policies change: a new standard arrives, a group imposes its rulebook on a company it buys, or a company adopts a policy that gives a truer picture. Each time, income or expense can fall between two stools: taxed twice, or never taxed.

**CTA 2009 Part 3 Chapter 14 (ss 180–187)** stops both outcomes.

| Element | Rule | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| When it applies | A company changes, from one period of account to the next, the basis on which it calculates trade profits; the old basis accorded with the law and practice for the earlier period and the new basis with the law and practice for the later period | s 180(1) |
| What is a change of basis | (a) a **change of accounting policy**, expressly including UK GAAP to IAS and IAS to UK GAAP (s 180(4)); HMRC treats any change of accounting framework as a change of policy (BIM34050); or (b) a change in the **tax adjustments** applied, including a change of view as to what the law requires | s 180(3), (4), (6) |
| What is not | A change made to comply with **amending legislation** not applying to the previous period (s 180(5)); a prior period adjustment **correcting a material error** (the old basis was not valid: BIM34135) | s 180(5) |
| Computing the adjustment | Step 1: understatements (receipts that fell out of the old periods untaxed; expenses deducted twice; stock and WIP mismatches; depreciation not adjusted); Step 2: overstatements; net = adjustment | s 182 |
| **Timing** | Positive adjustment = **receipt**; negative = **expense**; either way arising on the **first day of the first period of account** on the new basis. **No general spreading for CT** | s 181 |
| No double relief | Expenses deducted under the old basis that would be deducted again under the new: no adjustment and no further deduction | s 183 |
| Stock and depreciation | Where the change is a tax adjustment affecting stock or depreciation, the receipt or expense is deferred until the asset is realised or written off | s 184 |
| Mark to market | Trading stock moved from realisation basis to fair value: receipt deferred to realisation (s 185) **or** a **6-period spreading election** at one-sixth a year, made within **12 months** of the end of the first new-basis period (s 186) | ss 185–186 |

Property businesses have the equivalent rules in CTA 2009 ss 261–262; partnerships in ss 1267–1269.

## Calder changes its rulebook.

Back to our invented case, a year earlier. Tarnmoor bought Calder on **1 April GY1**; Calder kept its 31 March year end, and its accounts for the AP to 31 March GY2 were still prepared under FRS 102. From **1 April GY2**, group policy applies and Calder reports under **FRS 101**.

The switch is a change of accounting policy within Chapter 14 (both frameworks valid for their periods). In our invented case, applying the group's IFRS 15 revenue policies (as FRS 101 requires) brings forward revenue on part-completed installation contracts: revenue that Calder's old policy would have recognised in the new period is, on the new view, already earned in the old one. Without Chapter 14 it would never be taxed.

> **Worked example: Calder's change of basis (invented; AP 1 April GY2 to 31 March GY3)**
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Step 1: understatement (revenue earned before 1 April GY2 on the new basis, not recognised under the old) | 400,000 |
> | Step 2: overstatements | nil |
> | **Net adjustment: receipt arising on 1 April GY2 (s 181)** | **400,000** |
> | Taxed in the AP to 31 March GY3, in full (no spreading) | 400,000 |
> | Additional CT at 25% | **100,000** |
>
> **Instalments.** Calder is a very large company in the Tarnmoor group (chapter 3 explains the thresholds and the divisor), so it pays in months 3, 6, 9 and 12 of the AP:
>
> | Instalment due | Additional amount (£) |
> |---|---|
> | 14 June GY2 | 25,000 |
> | 14 September GY2 | 25,000 |
> | 14 December GY2 | 25,000 |
> | 14 March GY3 | 25,000 |
> | **Total** | **100,000** |

Graham Pike's cash forecast had assumed the change cost nothing, because the group's consolidated (IFRS) accounts were unaffected. The tax function's job is to spot the gap before the first instalment, not after it.

## Leases come onto the balance sheet.

For decades a tenant's accounts showed rent as an expense, and the tax computation deducted it. Then the accounting changed. Under **IFRS 16** (periods beginning on or after 1 January 2019) a lessee no longer distinguishes finance and operating leases: almost every lease goes on balance sheet as a **right-of-use (RoU) asset** with a **lease liability**, and the profit and loss account shows depreciation plus interest instead of rent. Short-term (12 months or less) and low-value leases are exempt.

**FRS 102 has followed.** The FRC's Periodic Review 2024 amendments (new Section 20 Leases; new Section 23 Revenue) apply to **accounting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2026** (early application permitted if all amendments are applied together). FRS 102 transition uses a modified retrospective approach only (comparatives not restated; cumulative effect to opening reserves), so adjustments arise less often than under IFRS 16, because the liability usually equals the RoU asset (BLM17050, BLM52005) [S]. In our invented case, Calder adopted the amended standard in the year TKS followed it through (its year to 31 March 2027, the first beginning after 1 January 2026).

**Parliament's two instincts.** FA 2011 s 53 froze the tax treatment of leases against accounting change. When IFRS 16 arrived, **FA 2019 Sch 14** took the opposite view: para 11 repealed the freeze, and **Part 3 (paras 12–17)** let tax follow the new accounts with a cushion.

**The cushion: spreading.** For lessee periods of account beginning on or after 1 January 2019, where a change of accounting policy results in an RoU asset being first recognised (for a lease that would not be a finance lease if classification were required), any change-of-basis receipt or expense under CTA 2009 Part 3 Ch 14 (or ss 261–262) is spread over the **spreading period**: the **weighted average remaining lease term**. Each lease's share of the total net adjustment is multiplied by its unexpired term and the results summed (Steps 1–5); amounts are apportioned by days. The spread follows a lease transferred to a connected UK-taxable transferee where there is no avoidance (para 15), accelerates on cessation (para 16), and covers portfolios (para 17). The rule is not tied to IFRS 16 by name, so it **automatically covers FRS 102 (2024) adopters**; HMRC BLM52005 confirms that transition adjustments from adopting a standard introducing RoU assets are spread. No FA 2026 provision was needed.

> **Worked example: lease transition spreading (illustrative; "for this example, suppose")**
>
> Suppose Calder's transition to the amended FRS 102 left a net expense of £90,000:
>
> | Lease | Adjustment (£) | Share of total | Remaining term (years) | Weighted term (years) |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Warehouse | 60,000 | 2/3 | 8 | 5.333 |
> | Forklift fleet | 30,000 | 1/3 | 2 | 0.667 |
> | **Total** | **90,000** | 1 | | **6.000** |
>
> Spreading period 6 years: deduction **£15,000 a year** (apportioned by days in any short period) instead of £90,000 at once.

**Beyond transition, the tax law is unchanged.** HMRC's guidance (BLM50010) says the FA 2019 changes were designed to give "no tax advantage or disadvantage to lessees". HMRC's view is that the tenant typically deducts the depreciation and interest; capital elements inside the RoU asset (premiums, SDLT, fit-out, payments to a previous tenant, dilapidations) must be stripped out (BLM50005, BLM50010; ICAEW, 23 March 2026) [S]. Every lease must still be tested against the **long funding lease** rules (CAA 2001 ss 70A–70YI; CTA 2010 ss 377, 377A, 379): a long funding lease of plant gives the lessee capital allowances and restricts its revenue deduction (chapter 9).

**CIR interaction.** TIOPA 2010 s 426 (amended by FA 2019 Sch 14 para 18) brings the spread amounts into the CIR group computations; para 19 excludes certain lessee change-of-policy amounts; TIOPA s 494 defines a "right-of-use lease". The ICAEW's reading is that interest on a lease liability counts as tax-interest only where the lease would have been a finance lease under the old classification, so for leases that would have been operating leases there should be no CIR impact, but an implicit classification test means the old classification must be kept in the records [S]. Chapter 28 picks this up.

## The spreading myth.

**Misconception: "a change of accounting standard can be spread for tax."** Usually it cannot. People believe it because the most famous recent change (leasing) was spread, and the loan relationship rules spread some amounts; the exception is mistaken for the rule.

The rule is Chapter 14's: a trading change of basis is taxed or relieved **in full on the first day of the new basis**. That includes the revenue change in the amended FRS 102 (Section 23: a five-step model aligned with IFRS 15). The ICAEW warned on 23 March 2026 that FRS 102 transition profits could push a company into QIPs (payments "as early as 14 March 2026" for some 31 December 2026 year ends), into very large company QIPs, or over the £5m deductions allowance for carried-forward losses, in a single year [S].

**Only three established routes to spreading:**

| Route | What is spread | How | Reference | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lease transition | Change-of-basis adjustment on first recognising RoU assets | Weighted average remaining lease term, by days | FA 2019 Sch 14 Part 3 | V |
| Mark-to-market election | Trading stock moved to fair value | One-sixth a year over 6 periods (election within 12 months of the end of the first new-basis period) | CTA 2009 s 186 | V |
| COAP Regulations | **Prescribed** loan relationship and derivative transition amounts | **One-tenth a year over 10 years** (reg 3A); IFRS 9 own-credit amounts over 5 years at **40%, 25%, 15%, 10%, 10%** (reg 3A(2A)); acceleration if the company leaves the CT charge (with exceptions) | SI 2004/3271 as amended | S (amended text not on legislation.gov.uk; CFM76090) |

> **Worked example: the COAP patterns (illustrative)**
>
> A prescribed transition debit of £300,000: £30,000 a year for 10 years. An own-credit transition amount of £200,000: £80,000, £50,000, £30,000, £20,000, £20,000 over 5 years (total £200,000).

"Prescribed" amounts are those under CTA 2009 ss 308 and 597 (prior period adjustments) and ss 316, 318, 614 and 615 (carrying value differences), excluding instruments maturing in the transition period (except IFRS 9 impairment transition amounts) (CFM76080) [S]. The COAP Regulations were made on 9 December 2004 for the move to IAS 39 and FRS 26 and now cover FRS 101/102 (2015) and IFRS 9 (2018) transitions (CFM76010).

**Loan relationship transitions outside COAP (CTA 2009 ss 315–316; derivatives ss 613–615).** Where a change of accounting policy (or a change to comply with Part 5) means the tax-adjusted carrying value at the end of one period differs from that at the start of the next, a credit or debit equal to the difference is brought in for the later period. A change to comply with amending legislation not applying to the earlier period is excluded (s 315).

So the honest answer to a finance director who asks whether a change of standard can be spread is a question back: which change, and under which rule?

> **Exam lens: change of accounting basis**
> - **Grade:** impact of accounting standards **1** (the LCG prospectus expects "an understanding of current accounting issues relevant to tax such as deferred tax, profit recognition and share schemes").
> - **Past appearances:** not the main focus of any M23–M26 question; a natural add-on to a computation or an "advise the CFO" requirement on a GAAP transition.
> - **What earns marks:** (1) identify Chapter 14 and why it applies (change of policy between valid bases); (2) compute the adjustment; (3) say it arises on day one of the new basis and is taxed in that AP; (4) state that no spreading applies (unless leases, s 186 or COAP); (5) the knock-ons: QIPs, the £5m allowance, CIR, deferred tax (chapter 6).
> - **Traps:** spreading a revenue transition; treating an error correction as a change of basis; treating a change forced by new legislation as within Chapter 14; forgetting that a change of view of the law **is** a change of basis (s 180(6)).

## Accounts in another currency.

Some businesses do not think in pounds. A UK company that buys, sells and borrows mainly in euros may find that its accounts measure everything in euros: its **functional currency** is the currency of the primary economic environment in which it operates.

**CTA 2010 Part 2 Chapter 4 (ss 5–17).**

| Section | Rule |
|---|---|
| s 5 | Basic rule: taxable total profits are calculated and expressed in sterling |
| s 6 | UK resident company whose accounts are in a currency other than sterling but whose functional currency is sterling: profits calculated in sterling as if the accounts had been prepared in sterling |
| s 7 | UK resident company whose functional currency (or, for an investment company, designated currency) is not sterling: profits calculated in that currency |
| s 8 | Residual rule: accounts in a non-sterling currency, outside ss 6 and 7 (for example where the functional currency is the accounts currency): profits calculated in the accounts currency |
| s 9A (with ss 9B–9C) | **Designated currency election** for a UK resident **investment company** (Condition A: HMRC reads it as a significant proportion of assets and liabilities in that currency; or Condition B); profits computed as if the designated currency were the functional currency (s 9A(9), as substituted by F(No.2)A 2015); periods of account beginning on or after 1 April 2011 (CFM64510) |
| s 11 | Translation into sterling: HMRC (CFM64330, CFM64170) says the average rate for the period or an appropriate spot rate (a just and reasonable blend where several transactions); an average rate usually appropriate; method applied consistently |
| ss 12–17 | Losses carried back or forward between periods with different tax calculation currencies: special translation rules (for example s 15: translate into the later currency at the start of the first affected period, then to sterling at the later period's s 11 rate) |

Status: ss 5–8 from the legislation.gov.uk explanatory notes and FA 2011 Sch 7 amendments; ss 9A and 11 from HMRC's manual (search extracts, 9 October 2026). The full current text was not opened.

> **Worked example: euro functional currency (illustrative; not story)**
>
> For this example, suppose a UK subsidiary with a euro functional currency has taxable profits of €4,600,000. At the book's **assumed** rate of €1.15 per £ (invented for illustration; never a real rate): £4,000,000. CT at 25%: **£1,000,000**. The point: exchange movements on its euro assets and debts largely drop out, because tax is measured in euros; its sterling items now generate the exchange differences.

**Exchange gains and losses on loans** are loan relationship amounts (CTA 2009 s 328(1)), except translation differences on a translation of results into another currency recognised in **OCI** (s 328(3)); for investment companies, no amount arises only from a change of functional currency (s 328(3B)). Matching of liabilities hedging shares: Disregard Regulations reg 3 (chapter 12).

> **Exam lens: foreign currency**
> - **Grade:** the LCG grid's core row "Calculation of liability in respect of profits" (grade **1**) is not limited to sterling (the OMB row reads "excluding foreign currency"); the LCG narrative syllabus names "profits accounted for in a foreign currency and the functional currency election" and "exchange gains and losses".
> - **Past appearances:** none as a main topic M23–M26. Expect add-on marks.
> - **Traps:** translating each line instead of the final profit; forgetting that the designated currency election is for **investment companies** only.

## Going further: the tax function and accounting change.

> **Going further**
>
> For a group tax function, accounting change is a project, not a footnote. Four tasks recur.
>
> 1. **Get a seat at the table early.** When a group moves a subsidiary onto its policies (as Tarnmoor did with Calder), model the Chapter 14 adjustment **before** the first day of the new basis. A positive adjustment is taxed at once, through instalments that may start in month 3.
> 2. **Quantify the cash effect separately from the accounting effect.** A change that leaves the consolidated accounts untouched can still move a subsidiary's CT, its QIPs, its use of carried-forward losses (the £5m allowance), the group's CIR position (tax-EBITDA and tax-interest) and its deferred tax (chapter 6).
> 3. **Document provisions as they are made.** HMRC's four tests are factual. A board paper with the announcement date, the people affected and the basis of each estimate is the evidence that wins an enquiry years later (chapter 3 on enquiry windows for large-group companies).
> 4. **Connect accounting judgements to governance.** A provision reflecting uncertainty about a tax position can itself trigger notification under the uncertain tax treatment rules (FA 2022 Sch 17; chapter 4, where Tarnmoor Engineering's £24m ERP costs are the example). The SAO's certificate (FA 2009 Sch 46) depends on accounting systems that get these adjustments right.
>
> **Judgement and ethics.** A company may face a choice of accounting policy that happens to defer tax. Choosing a policy because it gives a truer picture is ordinary. Choosing it only for the tax, especially across group companies, invites s 996, the loan relationship purpose tests (s 441, chapter 12), and the PCRT question every adviser should ask (TKS chapter 4).

## How the examiner tests it.

> **Exam lens: tax follows the accounts (whole chapter)**
> - **Grades (2026 grid, LCG column):** impact of accounting standards on taxable profits **1**; general calculation rules, Part 20 CTA 2009 **1**; deferred tax **1** (chapter 6). At least 70% of the CT element comes from core material.
> - **How it appears:** rarely a whole question; accounting items run through every computation (M23 Q1, N23 Q4, M24 Q6, N24 Q4, M25 Q2, M25 Q4, N25 Q1, M26 Q4), increasingly with a tax-accounting tail.
> - **What the examiners said:** M23 Q1, the general bad debt provision credit taken in the wrong direction; N23 Q4, capitalised revenue expenditure handled badly; N24 Q4, impairments split between a trade debt and a loan to a fellow subsidiary; M25 Q2, impairment of land; M26 Q4, errors of direction when adjusting the accounting profit, and inconsistent units (£ versus £000).
> - **Style:** technical prose and computation; no letter format; 0.5–1 mark per point; "Calculate, with explanations".
> - **Layout:** profit before tax → adjustments (each labelled with reason and authority) → tax-adjusted trading profit → capital allowances → other income → total profits → reliefs → TTP → CT. One unit throughout.
> - **Traps:** adding when you should deduct (and vice versa); general versus specific provisions; capital write-downs; s 1288 and pension timing overriding accruals; spreading a change of basis that cannot be spread; mistaking a change forced by legislation or an error correction for a Chapter 14 change; forgetting OCI amounts are not taxed until recycled.
> - **Check the 2027 grid** when published: a grade change would not be expected here, but confirm.

## What to take away.

The chapter asked whether the tax bill changes when the accountants change their minds. Mostly, yes. Tax follows the accounts by Parliament's deliberate choice, because accounts are already prepared, audited and built to measure profit over time. But the law nails the shadow down wherever it chooses to.

- **s 46 CTA 2009:** GAAP, subject to adjustments required or authorised by law. Accounts decide timing and measurement; the law decides capital/revenue, wholly and exclusively, and statutory blocks.
- **Cases:** *Odeon*, *Gallagher*, *Britannia Airways* built the principle; *NCL* [2022] UKSC 9: a properly computed debit counts unless a statute removes it, cash or no cash; *GDF Suez Teesside* [2018] EWCA Civ 2075: a statutory test overriding correct accounts.
- **Part 20:** ss 1288, 1290–1297, 1298, 1301B, 1303, 1304, 1305, and new s 1305B (FA 2026, APs beginning on or after 26 November 2025).
- **Loan relationships and derivatives:** profit or loss; OCI only when recycled; amortised cost between connected companies.
- **Provisions:** GAAP, revenue, no statutory timing conflict, accurate (HMRC BIM46510). General provisions not deductible; watch the direction. Calder's £1.8m restructuring provision deductible in its 9-month AP.
- **Change of basis:** Ch 14, ss 180–187: day one of the new basis, no general spreading. Calder's FRS 101 move: +£400,000, CT £100,000, +£25,000 per instalment.
- **Spreading routes:** leases (FA 2019 Sch 14), s 186 mark to market (6 periods), COAP (10 years; own credit 5 years at 40/25/15/10/10).
- **Leases:** amended FRS 102 for periods beginning on or after 1 January 2026; tax law unchanged; records matter.
- **Mixed frameworks:** CTA 2010 s 996.
- **Currency:** sterling, or the functional currency where that is not sterling, translated at (usually) an average rate; designated currency election for investment companies (s 9A).

The accounts give tax its profit. Chapter 6 turns to the other side of the same page: the tax charge the accounts must show, and the deferred tax that measures what the timing differences will cost in years to come.

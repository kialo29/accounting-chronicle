Chapter twelve: Loan relationships and derivatives.

Between the eleventh of April and the thirteenth of June, twenty twenty four, the Court of Appeal decided three corporation tax cases about interest. In each one, a group had lent money from one of its companies to another. In each one, the group said the borrowing served a commercial end. And in each one, HMRC won. That October, the Supreme Court refused permission to appeal in all three.

Those cases are the sharp end of a much larger body of law. Every company that borrows or lends, issues a bond, pays an arrangement fee, writes off a loan or hedges a price with a derivative is inside it. So this chapter asks a plain question. The tax system follows the accountant on debt. When does it stop following?

This is the financing chapter of Part Two. Loan relationships are core material on the Advanced Technical paper, and derivatives and the special relationships treated as loans are non-core. The Living Law, chapter twenty one, introduced the basic code. Here we take it to the depth the examiner expects, with the groups, the cases and the traps.

Debt as income: the nineteen ninety six bargain.

Start with the problem the code was built to solve. Before nineteen ninety six, the tax treatment of company debt turned on labels. Was a payment interest, or a premium? Was a loss on a loan capital, or revenue? The answers decided whether anything was taxed or relieved at all, and clever drafting could move a return from one label to another.

The Finance Act nineteen ninety six replaced the labels with a single idea for companies. Debt is income. Every profit, loss, interest payment and cost connected with lending money is a credit or a debit in computing income, and the amounts come from the accounts. The courts have noted that origin. The Court of Appeal's judgment in Greene King, in twenty sixteen, sets out the original nineteen ninety six provisions. The rules now live in Part five of the Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, which I will call the two thousand and nine Act.

A quick recap from The Living Law. A loan relationship is a money debt, owed to or by a company, that arises from a transaction for the lending of money. Trading debits are those on borrowing for the trade. Trading credits arise only where lending is integral to the trade, as for a bank. Everything else goes into a non-trading pot, which produces either a non-trading profit or a non-trading deficit.

Now the Advanced Technical layer. The law says the matters taxed are the profits and losses on the relationships and related transactions, the interest, and the expenses incurred directly in bringing a relationship into existence, in related transactions, in making payments, or in securing receipts. Costs of a loan that never happens, and costs incurred before a loan is made, are also brought in by a separate rule.

The amounts are those recognised in profit or loss under generally accepted accounting practice. Amounts that the accounts put in other comprehensive income are left out until they are recycled into profit or loss. Where the accounting period and the period of account differ, the amounts are time-apportioned.

The accounts can measure a loan at amortised cost, using the effective interest method, or at fair value. Tax usually accepts whichever basis the accounts properly use. The important exception is that connected companies must use amortised cost, which we will come to.

So the starting rule is simple. Find the debt, find the amounts in profit or loss, and tax them as income. The rest of this chapter is about where Parliament stops the accountant at the door. Think of three bouncers. The first is the connected-party rules, which make a group's two sides of a debt mirror each other. The second is the unallowable purpose rule, which turns away debits that exist for a tax advantage. The third is the Disregard Regulations, which decide how derivatives that hedge risks are let in. Each bouncer has its own guest list, and the examiner tests all three.

What goes into the pot.

The pot holds more than interest. Discounts and premiums accrue over the life of a loan under the effective interest method. Profits and losses on selling or repaying a loan are credits and debits. Fees and other costs of raising finance are debits, spread as the accounts spread them. And exchange gains and losses on a loan go in too.

Fees are the classic examination point. When a company borrows to buy a business, the arrangement fee is not part of the cost of the shares, and for an investment holding company it is not a management expense either. It is a loan relationship debit. The examiners reported, after the May twenty twenty three sitting, that some candidates missed exactly this.

Our invented group, Tarnmoor, shows how it works. Tarnmoor plc, the listed parent, drew thirty million pounds on a revolving credit facility from its bank on the first of April in Group Year One, to buy Calder Valve Engineering. It drew twenty four million pounds more on the first of July in Group Year Two, to buy Brackenwell Sensors. In our invented case, each drawing carried an arrangement fee of three hundred thousand pounds, so six hundred thousand pounds in all.

The accounts spread each fee over the expected life of its drawing using the effective interest method. The group expected to pay most of the facility down quickly, which it did, so the fees are front-loaded. The debits that follow are two hundred and fifty thousand pounds in Group Year One, two hundred and fifty thousand pounds in Group Year Two, and one hundred thousand pounds in Group Year Three. They are non-trading debits of Tarnmoor plc, because Tarnmoor plc does not trade. Chapter thirteen explains its management expenses. These fees are kept apart from them.

Exchange movements follow the same logic. The law brings exchange gains and losses on loan relationships into account, except translation differences that the accounts take to other comprehensive income. Suppose, for this example only, that a British company lent euros to a foreign subsidiary, and over a year the pound value of the loan rose by two point four million pounds. That is a taxable exchange gain of two point four million pounds, and six hundred thousand pounds of tax, even though no cash moved.

Groups manage this in two ways. They hedge the currency, which brings in the derivative rules later in this chapter. Or, where a company borrows in a foreign currency to fund a holding of shares in that currency, the Disregard Regulations can match the loan's exchange movements with the shares, so that the movements are left out of income. The matching rule exists because the shares are capital assets whose exchange movement is not taxed as it accrues.

Tarnmoor's treasury.

For the financial year twenty twenty six, the law we apply throughout, Tarnmoor's debt sits mainly in one company. In our invented case, Tarnmoor Finance Limited is the group treasury company. Before the story begins, it issued five hundred and fifty million pounds of listed notes paying five point five per cent. That is thirty point two five million pounds of interest a year. Because the notes are listed and pay interest, they are quoted Eurobonds, so the interest can be paid without deducting income tax. Chapter twenty three explains that rule.

Tarnmoor Finance lends every pound on at six per cent. Two hundred million pounds goes to Tarnmoor Engineering, the main trading company. Forty million goes to Tarnmoor Water Systems, seventy million to Tarnmoor Estates, the property company, and one hundred and twenty million to the parent. A final one hundred and twenty million pounds goes to Tarnmoor Vallaria, the subsidiary in the invented country of Vallaria. Its interest income is thirty three million pounds a year.

Take away the thirty point two five million pounds paid on the notes and six hundred thousand pounds of running costs. Tarnmoor Finance is left with taxable profits of about two point one five million pounds a year. Its lending is not a trade of banking, so its credits and debits sit in the non-trading pot. The pot is in profit, and the profit is taxed.

Now look at the borrowers, because the same interest can be trading for one company and non-trading for another. Tarnmoor Engineering borrows for its trade, so its twelve million pounds of interest is a trading debit. It reduces the trade profit. Tarnmoor Water Systems' two point four million pounds is a trading debit for the same reason. Tarnmoor Estates is a property investment company, so its four point two million pounds is a non-trading debit. Tarnmoor plc's seven point two million pounds is non-trading too.

That distinction matters three times over. It decides where a deficit goes. It decides which debits the corporate interest restriction disallows first. And it decides whether a loss is trading or non-trading when the group plans its claims.

Two more features complete the picture. Tarnmoor Finance has an interest rate swap, designated in its accounts as a fair value hedge of the notes. Its net settlements are left out of the book's figures for simplicity. And the loan to Tarnmoor Vallaria crosses a border, so transfer pricing applies to it, as chapter twenty seven explains. The loans between British companies fall within the exemption for wholly domestic transactions that applies from chargeable periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six.

When both sides belong to the group.

Here is the first bouncer. Inside a group, one board controls both sides of a debt. Left to the accounts, the creditor could write a loan down and claim a debit, while the debtor kept the full liability and recognised no income. The group would have a deduction with no matching receipt. The connected companies rules stop that by forcing the two sides to mirror each other.

The test is connection, and connection means control. Two companies are connected if one controls the other, or both are under the control of the same person. The law asks whether there was a connection at any time in the accounting period, and if there was, the relationship is connected for the whole period. It also reaches indirect connections through a series of loans. Every loan from Tarnmoor Finance to a group company is a connected companies relationship.

Three consequences follow. First, a connected companies relationship must be accounted for on an amortised cost basis. If the accounts use fair value, tax ignores them and uses amortised cost instead. So a group cannot put an intra-group loan at a falling market value and claim the fall.

Second, related transactions are disregarded where they would reduce credits or increase debits. A creditor cannot sell or assign a connected loan in a way that produces a tax result worse for HMRC than if the transaction had not happened.

Third, and most examined, the rules on impairment and release, to which we turn next.

Why does the law accept the cost of all this rigidity? Because the alternative is an open invitation. If a group could choose the measurement basis for loans between its own companies, it would choose whichever one gave a debit today and a credit never. The amortised cost rule means the debtor's liability and the creditor's asset are measured on the same footing, so what one side loses the other gains.

There is one practical warning. Connection is about control, not about the seventy five per cent test for group relief, or the fifty one per cent test for group payment arrangements. A company can be connected with a joint venture it controls jointly with no one else, and can be outside a group relief group but still connected. The examiner likes a company owned at, say, sixty per cent. It is outside group relief, but it is connected for loan relationship purposes.

No bad debt, no free release.

The creditor side first. The law says that a company with a connected creditor relationship brings in no impairment loss and no release debit. If Tarnmoor Engineering lent money to a struggling fellow subsidiary and wrote the loan down, the write-down would be added back. If it then forgave the loan, the release would be added back too.

There are two exceptions. One is a debt-for-equity swap, where the creditor releases the debt in exchange for ordinary shares in the debtor. The other is where the creditor itself is insolvent. Exchange losses are not affected by the bar.

Now the debtor side. A debtor in a connected relationship, using amortised cost, brings in no credit when the debt is released. Put the two sides together and the result is symmetry. The creditor gets no debit, the debtor pays no tax, and the group is left where it started. This is the bargain that makes intra-group rescues tax-neutral.

The examiner tests this constantly. In May twenty twenty four, a twenty mark computation contained an intra-group debt write-off that had to be reversed, and the examiners reported that candidates were weaker on that point. In November twenty twenty four, a computation set an impairment of a trade debt beside an impairment of a loan to a fellow subsidiary. The trade debt was deductible. The loan to the fellow subsidiary was not.

The misconception to clear away here is a natural one. People assume a bad debt is a bad debt, and that relief follows the loss. Between connected companies, relief does not follow the loss, because the matching income never arises either.

Outside a group, the rules are different. When an unconnected lender releases a debt, the debtor normally has a taxable credit. That is the point of following the accounts. But the law lifts the credit in five situations. The release is part of a statutory insolvency arrangement. The release is in exchange for ordinary shares in the debtor. The debtor is in insolvent liquidation, administration or a similar process, and the relationship is not connected. The release is under the bank resolution regime. Or, the fifth condition, the corporate rescue exception, which HMRC's guidance says applies to releases on or after the first of January, twenty fifteen. It applies where, without the release, there would be a material risk that within twelve months the company would be unable to pay its debts.

A related rule covers a debt that is substantially modified, or replaced, when the company is in the same distress. No credit arises on the change in carrying value.

Why the generosity? Because taxing a distressed company on the very debts it cannot pay would kill the rescue the creditors are trying to achieve. The tax bill would simply become one more debt the company could not meet.

Buying the debt cheaply.

The connected companies symmetry has a gap, and the deemed release rule closes it. Imagine a group that wants to buy a struggling company. The target owes ten million pounds to outside lenders, and the debt trades at four million pounds because the market doubts it will be repaid. The group buys the debt for four million pounds, buys the shares, and then releases the debt.

Without a special rule, the result would be extraordinary. Once the group owns both, the debtor and the creditor are connected. The release produces no credit for the debtor. A six million pound debt has vanished, and nobody has paid tax on that gain. Had the outside lenders released it themselves, the debtor would normally have been taxed.

The deemed release rule answers this. Where a company acquires a debt for less than the debtor's carrying value, and immediately afterwards the creditor and the debtor are connected, the creditor is treated as releasing the shortfall at that moment. The debtor then brings in a credit for the difference, before the connected companies protection can take effect. In the example, that is six million pounds.

The law then allows two exceptions, both from the Finance Number Two Act twenty fifteen, which rewrote these rules. HMRC's guidance says they apply to acquisitions of impaired debt on or after the eighteenth of November, twenty fifteen.

The first is the equity-for-debt exception. The acquisition is at arm's length, and the only consideration is ordinary shares in the acquiring company or a company connected with it. The creditors swap debt for equity, which is the classic restructuring.

The second is the corporate rescue exception, and its conditions are worth learning word for word. The acquisition must be at arm's length. The new creditor, or a company connected with it, must release the debt within sixty days of acquiring it. And it must be reasonable to assume that, without the release and the arrangements around it, there would be a material risk that at some time within the next twelve months the debtor would be unable to pay its debts.

A parallel pair of rules deals with companies that become connected while the creditor already holds an impaired debt. There too a deemed release can arise, with a matching corporate rescue exception.

The design is careful. The law is not against groups buying distressed debt. It is against a group using the connected companies rules to make a commercial gain disappear. Where the purchase is part of a genuine rescue, and the group follows through quickly by releasing the debt, the law lets the rescue proceed without a tax charge on money the company never had.

Brackenwell's notes.

Here the story supplies a case. Brackenwell Sensors, in our invented case, was a loss-making sensor developer founded by Dr Asha Varma. Before Tarnmoor bought it, its venture investors held three million pounds of its convertible notes. On the first of July in Group Year Two, Tarnmoor plc bought Brackenwell's shares, and on the same day it bought the notes from the venture investors for one point eight million pounds.

The investors were unconnected, and the price was negotiated at arm's length. But immediately afterwards Tarnmoor plc and Brackenwell were connected, and Tarnmoor plc had paid one point eight million pounds for a debt carried at three million pounds. That is the deemed release rule's exact target. Unless an exception applied, Brackenwell would be treated as released from one point two million pounds and would bring in a credit of that amount. At twenty five per cent, that is three hundred thousand pounds of potential tax. Whether Brackenwell's old losses could have absorbed it is a question for chapter fourteen, and the group did not rely on them.

Tom Hesketh, Tarnmoor's head of tax, built the deal timetable around the corporate rescue exception. Tarnmoor plc released the notes within sixty days, by the twenty ninth of August in Group Year Two. The board minutes recorded the evidence for the third condition. Brackenwell's cash flow forecasts showed that, without the release, there was a material risk it could not pay its debts within twelve months. The rescue exception applied. There was no deemed release credit.

Then the actual release. Tarnmoor plc's notes, carried at one point eight million pounds, were written off in its accounts. That is a connected creditor relationship, so no debit is allowed. Brackenwell's liability of three million pounds was released, and the accounts showed a gain. That is a connected debtor relationship, so no credit arises either. The result is symmetry again, and the rescue was tax-neutral, as Parliament intended.

Three practical lessons come from those few weeks. First, the sixty-day clock starts on acquisition, so the release has to be planned before completion, not after it. Second, the material risk condition is a matter of evidence, and the evidence should be made at the time, in board papers and forecasts. Third, the exceptions are conditions, not elections, so if they fail, the credit arises whether or not anyone notices. The book treats the notes as plain debt in Tarnmoor plc's hands. Their conversion rights fell away with the release.

Interest paid late.

For years, one of the most common traps in company tax was late-paid interest. The rule's aim was sensible. A debtor company accruing interest it did not pay could take a deduction year after year, while a creditor outside the charge to corporation tax paid nothing until the cash arrived, if it ever did. So in certain cases the debtor's deduction was deferred until payment.

The general rule still exists. If interest is not paid within twelve months after the end of the accounting period in which it accrues, and the creditor does not bring the full amount into account as it accrues, the debtor gets relief only when the interest is paid. But the rule now applies only in two kinds of case.

The first is the close company case. The debtor is a close company, and the creditor is a participator, an associate of one, or a company controlled by or with a major interest held by a participator. There are carve-outs for certain small company and collective investment cases. And where the creditor is itself a company, the rule applies only if that company is resident in a non-qualifying territory, essentially a territory without a suitable tax treaty with the U K.

The second is a loan by the trustees of an occupational pension scheme to the employer company, or to certain connected companies.

The cases that once caught groups, interest owed to a connected company and interest owed to a company with a major interest, were removed by the Finance Act twenty fifteen. So the old reflex of treating every unpaid intra-group interest balance as relieved only on payment is now wrong. Connected British companies accrue interest in the normal way.

For Tarnmoor, the point is quickly answered. A subsidiary controlled by a widely held listed parent is not a close company. None of Tarnmoor's companies is close, so the late interest rules do not apply to its intra-group debts. The examiner still sets owner-managed scenarios on other papers, but on this one, recognising when the rule does not apply is the skill.

The purpose test.

Now the second bouncer, and the most powerful. The rule is in section four four one of the two thousand and nine Act, which practitioners call section four four one. If a loan relationship has an unallowable purpose in an accounting period, the debits for that period are disallowed so far as they are attributable to that purpose on a just and reasonable apportionment.

A purpose is unallowable if it is not among the company's business or other commercial purposes. Two refinements make the test bite. First, a tax avoidance purpose counts as a business purpose only if it is not the main purpose, or one of the main purposes, for which the company is a party to the loan. Second, a tax avoidance purpose means any purpose of securing a tax advantage for the company or for any other person. That last phrase means a group's advantage counts, not just the borrower's. The law also says that the purposes of activities outside the charge to corporation tax are not business purposes for this test.

Notice what the test examines. It asks about the purpose of being a party to the loan, period by period. A loan taken for good reasons can acquire an unallowable purpose later, if it is restructured for tax. And the apportionment is not mechanical. The tribunal decides how much of the debits is attributable to the bad purpose, and the answer can be all of them.

The leading earlier case is Fidex Limited versus H M R C, decided by the Court of Appeal in twenty sixteen. It concerned a scheme, known as Project Zephyr, that used the move to international accounting standards to produce a debit on bonds. It was decided under the predecessor rule in the Finance Act nineteen ninety six. The scheme failed. In the words of the Court of Appeal, but for this tax avoidance scheme there would have been no debit at all. Once a debit exists only because of the scheme, all of it is attributable to the scheme.

That but for reasoning became the template. If you strip away the tax avoidance purpose and the debit disappears, the debit belongs to the unallowable purpose. The hard question, and the one the twenty twenty four cases answered, is what happens when there is a real commercial transaction in the picture too.

Three cases in nine weeks.

The first was BlackRock HoldCo Five versus H M R C, decided by the Court of Appeal on the eleventh of April, twenty twenty four. The prologue told its story. An American group bought a business, and a British resident company in the group took on about four billion dollars of intra-group loans. The company won on transfer pricing, because the loans could have been made at arm's length. But it lost on unallowable purpose. The court held that the loans had a main tax avoidance purpose and that, on the facts, all the debits were attributable to it.

The second was Kwik-Fit Group versus H M R C, decided on the third of May, twenty twenty four. As the reported facts describe it, one group company held about forty eight million pounds of old non-trading deficits that could only be used against its own non-trading profits. The group expected them to take about twenty five years to use. It reorganised its intra-group loans so that interest income would flow into that company and the losses would be used in about three years. Other group companies took on new loans, or higher interest rates, and claimed the debits.

The Court of Appeal upheld the tribunal's view that the debits on the new loans were attributable to an unallowable purpose. The tax advantage was the deductions made available to the paying companies, while the receiving company's income was sheltered. Kwik-Fit also adds a time dimension. HMRC accepted that once the target losses had been used, continued denial would no longer be just and reasonable.

The third was J T I Acquisition Company twenty eleven Limited versus H M R C, decided on the thirteenth of June, twenty twenty four. A British company was set up by an American group to buy an American business, LeTourneau Technologies, and borrowed from a group company to do so. The interest was surrendered as group relief against the group's British profits. The acquisition was real. But the tribunal had found that the British company's own part in the deal, as borrower, had no commercial purpose. The Court of Appeal would not disturb that finding. The tax advantage had been bolted on to a commercial deal, and the debits could be attributed wholly to it.

In October twenty twenty four, the Supreme Court refused permission to appeal in all three cases.

Here is the chapter's misconception. People say interest on money borrowed to buy a company must be deductible, because buying a company is commercial. The Court of Appeal has now closed that argument. The test looks at the borrower's purpose in being party to the loan, not at the purpose of the acquisition. A commercial deal can carry a loan whose own purpose is tax.

The debate is not quite over. The profession argues that this leaves groups uncertain, because ordinary acquisition finance with a tax benefit attached is common, and the line between a tax-efficient structure and a main purpose is not easy to draw. HMRC's view is that the rule asks a factual question, and the tribunals answer it. One case is still running. In Syngenta Holdings versus H M R C, the First-tier Tribunal found for HMRC in twenty twenty four, and in October twenty twenty five the Upper Tribunal gave permission to appeal on some grounds. Commentators report that the main question is whether a genuine business purpose must lead to apportionment rather than total disallowance. No Upper Tribunal decision had been published by October twenty twenty six.

The plan Tarnmoor turned down.

In our invented case, the test arrived at Tarnmoor in Group Year Two, a few months after the Brackenwell deal. The idea came from Calder's own finance team, whose results were measured on Calder's profit after tax. Tarnmoor plc would sell Brackenwell to Calder for twenty four million pounds, and Calder would borrow the price from Tarnmoor Finance at six per cent.

The arithmetic looked attractive from Calder's desk. Interest of one point four four million pounds a year would cut Calder's taxable profits, and its corporation tax by three hundred and sixty thousand pounds a year.

Tom Hesketh wrote the analysis for the board. He made three points.

The first was commercial. Brackenwell already supplied sensors to Calder and to Tarnmoor Engineering, and owning it from Calder changed nothing about how that supply worked. Calder had no need of the money and no business reason to borrow it. On any honest reading, the only purpose of Calder becoming a debtor was the deduction.

The second was the group view. Tarnmoor Finance would receive the same one point four four million pounds as taxable interest. The group's tax bill would barely move. What would move was Calder's own bill, and that is still a tax advantage, for Calder. The law counts an advantage for the company or any other person, and Kwik-Fit shows that deductions made available to one company can be the advantage even where the receiving side is not better off. The rule asks what the company was trying to do, not whether the group came out ahead.

The third was the case law. On the reasoning of J T I and Kwik-Fit, a tribunal would very likely find that being party to the loan had a main tax avoidance purpose, and would attribute all the debits to it. Calder's deduction would be disallowed, while Tarnmoor Finance's interest income stayed taxable. The plan would turn a neutral position into a loss for the group.

The board declined, and minuted why. The episode also shows the tax function's quieter job. Purpose is a question of fact, decided years later from documents. Groups that borrow for good reasons should say so at the time, in board papers that explain the business case. Groups that are offered plans whose only case is tax should be able to show that they said no.

When the debits outrun the credits.

When a company's non-trading debits exceed its non-trading credits, it has a non-trading deficit. For deficits of accounting periods beginning on or after the first of April, twenty seventeen, the law offers four routes.

First, the company can claim to set the deficit against any profits of the same period. Second, it can claim to carry it back twelve months, but only against non-trading loan relationship profits of that earlier period. Both these claims must be made within two years after the end of the deficit period.

Third, a deficit of the current period can be surrendered as group relief to another group company. Chapter fifteen covers the mechanics.

Fourth, whatever is not claimed or surrendered is carried forward. In a later period, the company can claim to set it against its total profits, within two years after the end of that later period. Carried-forward deficits can also be surrendered to the group, and they count towards the restriction that limits carried-forward reliefs to the five million pound allowance plus half of the profits above it. If the company has ceased its investment business, carried-forward deficits are confined to non-trading profits.

Two warnings follow. The carry-back is narrow. A deficit carried back cannot touch trading profits of the earlier year. And even the current-year set-off needs a claim. A deficit nobody claims simply rolls forward. The examiners set a twenty mark question in November twenty twenty five on a new subsidiary that included the options for relieving non-trading deficits, so these routes are live examination material.

Deficits from before April twenty seventeen were stuck under the old rules, usable only against the company's own later non-trading profits. That is exactly the kind of trapped deficit that sat behind Kwik-Fit.

Tarnmoor's numbers show the routes at work. In Group Year One, Tarnmoor plc's non-trading debits were eight point eight million pounds. They were seven point two million pounds of interest to Tarnmoor Finance, one point three five million pounds of interest on the bank facility, and the two hundred and fifty thousand pound fee. The corporate interest restriction disallowed two point two one million pounds of them, and the default order hits non-trading debits first. Chapter twenty eight explains why. That left a deficit of six point five nine million pounds, which Tarnmoor plc surrendered to Tarnmoor Engineering. In Group Year Two, the debits were nine point nine seven million pounds, the disallowance two point three million, and the surrender seven point six seven million.

Tarnmoor Estates is the other pattern. Its four point two million pounds of non-trading interest exceeds its non-trading credits, so it claims to set the deficit against its own rental profits in the same period.

Debts that are not loans.

Part six of the two thousand and nine Act extends the loan relationship rules to things that are not loans in the ordinary sense, and it is non-core for this paper. The idea behind it is simple. The same economic return should get the same tax treatment, whatever its legal form.

The most practical chapter covers relevant non-lending relationships. A money debt that does not arise from lending, a trade debt for example, is brought into the loan relationship rules where interest is payable on it, where exchange gains or losses arise on it, or where an impairment loss or release debit arises on certain unpaid business payments. So interest charged on an overdue customer balance, or an exchange gain on a dollar trade debt, is a loan relationship credit.

The second is disguised interest. Where a company is party to an arrangement that produces a return economically equivalent to interest, the return is taxed as a loan relationship profit on an amortised cost basis. A return is economically equivalent to interest if it is reasonable to regard it as a return on an investment of money at a reasonably commercial rate, reflecting the time value of money, and there is no practical likelihood that it will cease. There are exclusions, including where the return is otherwise taxed and where there is no tax avoidance main purpose. The target is a share or contract dressed up to turn interest into an untaxed or capital receipt.

Three more chapters can be named in a line each. Transferred income streams catch a company that sells a right to income, such as interest, for a lump sum, and tax the lump sum as the income it replaces. Alternative finance arrangements give finance structured to comply with Islamic principles the same treatment as interest. And shares with guaranteed returns treat shares that are designed to produce an interest-like return as if they were a creditor relationship.

In the examination, Part six usually appears as a recognition point. A scenario mentions an overdue debt with interest, a preference share with a fixed redemption premium, or a sale of a stream of payments, and the marks come for naming the rule and saying it brings the return into the loan relationship regime.

Derivatives and the hedging rules.

Derivatives are the third bouncer's territory, and on this paper they are non-core. The rules are in Part seven of the two thousand and nine Act. A derivative contract is, broadly, an option, a future or a contract for differences that is accounted for as a derivative, or would be if fair value accounting applied. Contracts whose underlying subject matter is excluded, for example certain shares in the company's own group, fall outside. Interest rate swaps and currency contracts are contracts for differences and are within the rules.

The default is familiar. Credits and debits are the amounts recognised in profit or loss. Trading derivatives go into trading profits. Non-trading derivatives join the non-trading loan relationship pot. Derivatives are usually at fair value in the accounts, so a company that holds them without hedge accounting is taxed on fair value movements as they occur.

The courts have patrolled the edges. In Union Castle Mail Steamship Company versus H M R C, decided by the Court of Appeal in twenty twenty, a group used a scheme to produce a tax debit of about thirty nine million pounds on derivative contracts, from amounts recognised in equity rather than in profit or loss. The appeal failed. Since twenty sixteen the rules have focused firmly on amounts in profit or loss.

Now the Disregard Regulations, made in two thousand and four. The problem they solve is a mismatch. A company hedges a risk, say the price of a commodity it buys, with a derivative. The derivative is at fair value and its movements hit profit or loss now. The hedged purchase affects profit only later. Tax that follows the accounts would tax the derivative's swings before the matching cost arrives.

Since twenty fifteen, the regulations no longer apply automatically. HMRC's guidance says the default is to follow the accounts. A company that wants the hedging rules elects into them, and the election can cover currency contracts, commodity and debt contracts, and interest rate contracts. Where an election applies, the fair value movements on the hedging derivative are left out. For currency, commodity and debt contracts they are brought back into account in line with the hedged item. For interest rate contracts they are brought in on an appropriate accruals basis. A company new to fair value accounting has an extended window to elect, and an election then locks in for a period.

HMRC's guidance identifies one main automatic case. Where the hedged item is not itself taxed in line with the accounts, connected company debt being the usual example, the hedging rules apply without an election. There is also an anti-avoidance rule where a main purpose of arranging matters is to avoid the regulations.

Tarnmoor shows both sides. In our invented case, Tarnmoor Engineering buys copper and hedges the price with copper futures. When it first used derivatives, before the story begins, it made the election. Suppose, for this example only, that at a year end its futures showed a fair value gain of four hundred thousand pounds. With the election, that gain is left out and brought in when the hedged copper purchases reach profit or loss. Without it, the gain would be taxed now, adding one hundred thousand pounds to that year's tax.

Tarnmoor Finance's swap is different. It is designated as a fair value hedge of the external notes. In a fair value hedge the accounts already adjust the notes for the hedged risk, so the two movements offset in profit or loss. Tarnmoor Finance has made no election, and none is needed. Tax simply follows the accounts.

The examiner set this in November twenty twenty three. A company held a fuel future without hedge accounting, and the examiners reported that only a minority of candidates mentioned the Disregard Regulations. The marks came for saying that fair value movements are taxed as they arise unless the company has elected.

Financing the group.

Step back from the rules to the job. A group tax function lives with financing decisions every week, and the examiner's narrative syllabus names common financing transactions as part of this paper. Here is how the pieces fit.

Debt or equity is the first choice. Interest on debt is, in principle, deductible, while dividends on shares are not. That asymmetry is why every regime in Part Four exists. The corporate interest restriction in chapter twenty eight limits net interest to a share of taxable earnings. Transfer pricing in chapter twenty seven asks whether an independent lender would have lent that much on those terms. The hybrid rules in chapter twenty nine deny deductions where the other side is not taxed. And the unallowable purpose rule sits underneath them all. Each looks at the same interest from a different angle, and a debit can survive one and fall to another. BlackRock won on transfer pricing and lost on purpose.

Acquisition finance is the second. The arrangement fee is a loan relationship debit, spread as the accounts spread it. The interest follows the accounts, subject to the purpose test. Where the target owes debt to outsiders, the group must decide before completion whether to buy that debt, refinance it, or release it, because the deemed release rule and its sixty-day rescue window turn on the timetable.

Bonds are the third. Listed bonds that pay interest are quoted Eurobonds and can pay interest gross. Interest paid on other debt to lenders abroad may need income tax deducted. For the financial year twenty twenty six the rate on yearly interest is twenty per cent. Coming next, enacted by the Finance Act twenty twenty six, is twenty two per cent from twenty twenty seven to twenty eight. Chapter twenty three covers the withholding rules in Part fifteen of the Income Tax Act two thousand and seven.

Cash pooling is the fourth. In a physical pool, balances are swept into a header account each day, so each participating company has a loan relationship with the header company. Those are connected companies relationships. The pool's interest must be at arm's length where a border is crossed, and the balances can generate exchange movements when currencies differ.

Then the calendar. Claims to use non-trading deficits are due within two years. A hedging election must be made in its window. A rescue release must happen within sixty days. And the board minutes must show purpose at the time.

One trap from the examiners deserves a last word. In May twenty twenty three, candidates facing a loan from an American company wasted time on diverted profits tax. The examiners reported that it does not apply to loan relationships. That tax has since been repealed and replaced, as chapter thirty explains, but the lesson stands. On a financing question, think loan relationships, transfer pricing, the interest restriction and withholding first.

What to take away.

The loan relationship code, since the Finance Act nineteen ninety six, treats company debt as income. Interest, discounts, fees, profits and losses on loans, and exchange movements are credits and debits. The amounts are those in profit or loss under generally accepted accounting practice. Amounts in other comprehensive income wait until they are recycled. Trading debits arise on borrowing for the trade, and everything else goes into the non-trading pot.

The first bouncer is the connected companies rules. Connection means control at any time in the accounting period. Connected relationships must use amortised cost. The creditor gets no impairment or release debit, except in a debt-for-equity swap or if the creditor is insolvent. The debtor brings in no release credit. The result is symmetry, and reversing an intra-group write-off is a favourite examination point.

Buying a debt cheaply and becoming connected triggers a deemed release, taxed on the debtor, unless the equity-for-debt exception or the corporate rescue exception applies. The rescue exception needs an arm's length acquisition, a release within sixty days, and a material risk of insolvency within twelve months. Outside a group, a released debt is normally taxable on the debtor, but five conditions lift the credit, including corporate rescue. Brackenwell's notes, bought for one point eight million pounds against three million, would have produced a deemed release of one point two million pounds. The release inside sixty days and the board's evidence meant no credit, and Tarnmoor plc got no debit.

Late-paid interest now matters only for close company participators, with corporate creditors caught only in non-qualifying territories, and for pension scheme loans. Tarnmoor is not close.

The second bouncer is section four four one. Debits are disallowed so far as attributable to an unallowable purpose, and a tax advantage for any person counts. Fidex gave us but for. BlackRock, Kwik-Fit and J T I, all decided in twenty twenty four, showed that a commercial acquisition does not save a loan whose own purpose is tax, and that all the debits can be disallowed. Syngenta is still pending. Tarnmoor turned down a twenty four million pound debt pushdown that would have saved Calder three hundred and sixty thousand pounds a year, because it had no purpose but the deduction.

Non-trading deficits since April twenty seventeen can be set against current profits, carried back twelve months against non-trading profits only, surrendered to the group, or carried forward against total profits. Each route needs a claim within two years.

The third bouncer is the Disregard Regulations. Derivatives follow profit or loss. Since twenty fifteen, the hedging rules apply only by election, or automatically where the hedged item is not taxed in line with the accounts. Tarnmoor Engineering's copper futures are under an election. Tarnmoor Finance's fair value hedge simply follows the accounts.

And the grades are these. Loan relationships are core. Derivatives and the relationships treated as loans are non-core.

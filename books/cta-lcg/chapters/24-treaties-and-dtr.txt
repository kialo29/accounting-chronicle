Chapter twenty four: Treaties and double tax relief.

On the fourteenth of April, nineteen twenty six, Winston Churchill, then Chancellor of the Exchequer, signed a short agreement with the government of the Irish Free State. Ernest Blythe signed for the Irish side. Until a few years before, the two countries had been one tax system. Now they were two, and the same people and the same profits were being taxed twice, once in London and once in Dublin. The agreement promised two things. A person resident in one country but not the other would be exempt in the other. A person resident in both would get relief. It would work only if both parliaments confirmed it. One historian of Irish tax describes it as the first international double taxation agreement the United Kingdom ever made.

A century later the United Kingdom has one of the largest treaty networks in the world, and a large group's tax function reads treaties every week. Which country may tax a royalty? How much may the paying country withhold? Is a construction project abroad a taxable presence there? And when two countries both tax the same profit, how much of the foreign tax will the United Kingdom give back as a credit?

This chapter answers those questions. It sits in Part Four, across borders, after residence in chapter twenty two and inbound permanent establishments and withholding in chapter twenty three. Both of its topics are core for the paper, grade one. They are the application of double tax treaties, including supplied extracts, and double tax relief. The Living Law, chapter twenty six, taught the basics of credit relief, the source by source limit, the allocation of deductions, underlying tax and expense relief. Here we read the treaties themselves, then take the credit rules to Advanced Technical depth, with the anti-avoidance and the cases. Everything is on the law for the financial year twenty twenty six.

Two statutes carry most of the chapter. The relief rules live in Part two of the Taxation, International and Other Provisions, Act twenty ten, which I will call the International Act. The Patent Box lives in the Corporation Tax Act twenty ten, which I will call the twenty ten Act.

Why two countries tax the same profit.

Every country taxes on two footings. It taxes its own residents on what they earn, wherever they earn it. And it taxes anyone, resident or not, on what they earn inside its borders. Each footing is reasonable on its own. Put them together across a border and the same pound is taxed twice. The country of residence says the company is ours. The country of source says the profit was made here. Both are right, and the company pays both.

Lawyers call this juridical double taxation, meaning the same income taxed in the hands of the same person by two states. Its cousin is economic double taxation, where the same profit is taxed in the hands of two different persons. A subsidiary pays tax on its profit, and its parent is taxed again on the dividend. Treaties deal mainly with the first kind. The second kind turns up later in this chapter, with underlying tax, and again in chapter twenty seven, with transfer pricing adjustments.

The fix was worked out between the wars. In October nineteen twenty eight, representatives of twenty seven countries met under the League of Nations and produced model tax treaties. The line runs from those models to the Model Tax Convention of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, which I will call the O E C D Model. Most of the United Kingdom's treaties follow it closely.

Think of a treaty as a peace agreement between two tax authorities. Each side gives up some territory in return for certainty. The country of source agrees to tax business profits only if the foreign company has a permanent establishment there, and to cap its withholding on dividends, interest and royalties. The country of residence agrees to relieve whatever tax the source country is still allowed to take, either by exempting the income or by giving a credit for the foreign tax.

Now the precise rule. A treaty allocates taxing rights and limits them. It does not create a charge. If United Kingdom law does not tax something, no treaty can make it taxable. What a treaty can do is stop the United Kingdom taxing something its own law would otherwise tax, or require it to give relief. So the method is always two steps. First, does domestic law charge the income? Second, does a treaty take the charge away or limit it?

In our invented group, Tarnmoor, the treaty that matters most is with Vallaria. Vallaria, like everything else in Tarnmoor's story except Ireland, is invented. The United Kingdom and Vallaria have a modern treaty on the O E C D pattern. Vallaria taxes companies at twenty per cent. Under the treaty it may withhold ten per cent on interest, fifteen per cent on dividends and five per cent on royalties. A building site or construction project is a permanent establishment only if it lasts more than twelve months. Marrovia, the group's other invented country, has no treaty with the United Kingdom at all.

How a treaty reaches a tax return.

A treaty is signed by governments, but a company cannot rely on it until Parliament has given it effect. Section two of the International Act does that. Arrangements made with another territory have effect if they are specified in an Order in Council declaring that they were made with a view to affording relief from double taxation. The draft Order must first be approved by the House of Commons. Section six then sets out the effect the arrangements have. Chapter two of this book described that route.

Chapter two also told the story of Fowler versus H M R C, decided by the Supreme Court in twenty twenty. A diver resident in South Africa was deemed by United Kingdom law to be trading, but the Supreme Court held that the deeming did not change which treaty article applied. Treaty words take their ordinary international meaning. And the O E C D Commentaries are persuasive, in Lord Briggs's words, as the cogency of their reasoning deserves.

Treaties now change in bulk. The Multilateral Instrument, a single convention that modifies many treaties at once, came into force for the United Kingdom on the first of October, twenty eighteen. It does not replace a treaty. It edits it, and only where both countries have chosen the same options. So H M R C publishes synthesised texts showing how each covered treaty reads after the edits. The United Kingdom and Ireland, for example, apply the Instrument's articles on transparent entities, dual resident companies, the preamble, the principal purpose test, the anti-fragmentation rule for permanent establishments, the definition of a closely related person, mutual agreement and corresponding adjustments, together with arbitration. They do not apply its article on commissionaires and dependent agents. Reading a modern treaty therefore means reading the treaty, then the Instrument, then the synthesised text that puts them together.

Three practical points follow. First, treaty relief is not automatic. A company must claim it, either from the paying country at source or from H M R C. Second, a treaty only binds the two states that signed it. Vallaria's treaty with the United Kingdom tells Vallaria what it may withhold on a royalty paid to a United Kingdom company. It says nothing about a payment to Marrovia. Third, in the examination you are given the O E C D Model as an on-screen document, the twenty seventeen articles. When a question supplies an extract from a particular treaty, that extract is the treaty for the question. After the November twenty twenty three sitting the examiners reported that some candidates ignored the supplied treaty article and used the Model text instead. Read the extract. Quote its words. Apply them.

Reading the Model, article by article.

The Model has about thirty articles, and H M R C's International Manual describes them in a sentence. The first five set the scope, the middle ones deal with each type of income, and the rest are general. You need a working map, because the examiner will hand you an article and expect you to know where it sits.

Articles one and two say who and what is covered, meaning persons resident in one or both states, and taxes on income and capital. Article three defines terms, and sends you back to domestic law for any term the treaty leaves undefined, unless the context requires otherwise. Article four is residence. It starts from whoever is liable to tax in a state by reason of residence, place of management or a similar test. Then it breaks ties. Older treaties send a dual resident company to its place of effective management. The modern approach, adopted through the Multilateral Instrument where both countries agree, leaves the two competent authorities to settle it by agreement. Chapter twenty two told that story with Tarnmoor Ireland.

Article five defines a permanent establishment as a fixed place of business, including a branch, office or factory. A building site or construction project counts only if it lasts more than a stated period. The Model says twelve months, some treaties say six. Preparatory or auxiliary activities are excluded, and dependent agents may create one. A subsidiary is not a permanent establishment of its parent just because the parent controls it. Chapter twenty three compared this treaty test with the new domestic test.

Article seven is the heart of the business provisions. The profits of an enterprise are taxable only in its home state, unless it carries on business in the other state through a permanent establishment. Then the other state may tax the profits attributable to that establishment, worked out as if it were a separate and independent enterprise dealing at arm's length. Article nine is its twin for associated companies. Where connected enterprises deal on terms that independent parties would not have agreed, a state may adjust the profits, and the other state should make a corresponding adjustment to remove the double taxation. Chapter twenty seven owns that.

Articles ten, eleven and twelve deal with dividends, interest and royalties. The pattern is the same in each. The country where the recipient is resident may tax. The country of source may also tax, but only up to a capped rate, and only if the recipient is the beneficial owner. Royalties are defined widely, covering patents, trade marks, designs, secret formulas or processes, and know-how. Article thirteen gives gains on immovable property to the country where the property is, gains on the movable property of a permanent establishment to that establishment's country, and most other gains to the country of residence.

Article twenty three is the relief article. Its two versions are labelled twenty three A, exemption, and twenty three B, credit. The United Kingdom's treaties give credit. Article twenty four is non-discrimination, and article twenty five is the mutual agreement procedure. Both have their own section shortly.

Here is the method to use on any treaty question. Classify the income. Find the article that governs it. Ask which state may tax, and how much. Then go to the relief article to see how the residence state removes the double charge.

Try it on our invented case. In August of Group Year Two, Calder Valve Engineering, the valve maker that Tarnmoor bought in chapter one, began installing process valves at a refinery in Vallaria. The work ran to September of Group Year Three, fourteen months. Under Vallarian law, which our story does not describe, Vallaria wanted to tax the profit. Under the invented treaty, a construction project is a permanent establishment only if it lasts more than twelve months. Fourteen months is more. So Calder had a Vallarian permanent establishment, Vallaria could tax the profits attributable to it, and the United Kingdom, which taxes Calder on its worldwide profits, had to give credit. Had the job finished in eleven months, article seven would have left the profit to the United Kingdom alone.

Who really owns the income.

The caps on withholding in articles ten to twelve are valuable, and that makes them a target. If a United Kingdom company is entitled to a low withholding rate on interest from a country, and a company in a third country is not, the obvious idea is to route the interest through the United Kingdom. The treaty's answer is the phrase beneficial owner. The cap applies only if the recipient is the beneficial owner of the payment.

The leading English case is Indofood International Finance versus J P Morgan Chase Bank, decided by the Court of Appeal in two thousand and six. It was not an appeal against a tax assessment. It was a commercial dispute between the issuer of some loan notes and the bank, and it turned on what a tax treaty meant. An Indonesian group had raised money through a finance company in Mauritius. The Mauritian company issued the notes and lent the money on to the Indonesian parent, which guaranteed them. The point of Mauritius was the treaty between Indonesia and Mauritius, which cut the Indonesian withholding on interest from twenty per cent to ten. The question for the court was whether a newly created Dutch company could be put in the middle instead, to claim the same ten per cent rate under the treaty between Indonesia and the Netherlands.

The Court of Appeal held that it could not. The Dutch company would have to pay on what it received, and it would have no real benefit from the interest. Its position would be like that of an administrator of the income. The court also held that beneficial owner in a treaty has an international fiscal meaning, not a narrow domestic legal one. A company that receives income only to pass it on is not its beneficial owner, whatever its legal title.

Notice what the court did not need. It did not need a purpose test. It asked who, in substance, enjoyed the income. That is why Indofood is often described as the forerunner of the principal purpose test, which now does the same work more directly.

In our invented case the question is easy. Calder licenses its Ashlar patents to Tarnmoor Vallaria, the group's Vallarian manufacturer. From Group Year Four, Tarnmoor Vallaria pays a royalty of six per cent of its Ashlar valve sales, about one point two million pounds a year. Calder developed the invention itself, owns the patents, keeps the royalty and bears the cost of maintaining the patents. It is the beneficial owner. So Tarnmoor Vallaria withholds at the treaty rate of five per cent, sixty thousand pounds a year, not at any higher Vallarian domestic rate. The group's practice is to give the paying company whatever evidence of United Kingdom residence it asks for, such as a certificate of residence from H M R C. How Vallaria administers the reduced rate is Vallarian law, which our story ignores.

The principal purpose test.

Beneficial ownership catches conduits. It does not catch every abuse. A company can be the genuine owner of income and still have been placed where it is mainly to win a treaty benefit. The O E C D's work on base erosion and profit shifting produced a broader answer, and the Multilateral Instrument carries it into existing treaties.

It comes in two parts. Article six of the Instrument adds a preamble saying the treaty is meant to eliminate double taxation without creating opportunities for non-taxation or reduced taxation through evasion or avoidance, including treaty shopping. Article seven adds the principal purpose test. In the words of the United Kingdom and Ireland synthesised text, a benefit is denied if it is reasonable to conclude, having regard to all relevant facts and circumstances, that obtaining that benefit was one of the principal purposes of any arrangement or transaction. There is an escape. The benefit is still given if granting it would be in accordance with the object and purpose of the relevant provisions of the treaty.

Three features make the test sharper than it first sounds. It needs only one of the principal purposes, not the main purpose. It looks at any arrangement or transaction, not at the company as a whole. And the starting point is what it is reasonable to conclude, which in practice means the documents. A board paper that says the structure was chosen to get the five per cent rate is evidence against you.

In our invented case, the United Kingdom and Vallaria treaty contains the same preamble and principal purpose test. Here is a labelled hypothetical to test it. Suppose the group had proposed moving the Ashlar patents to Tarnmoor Capital, its finance company in Marrovia, where the tax rate is nine per cent. Marrovia has no treaty with Vallaria in our story, so the royalty from Vallaria would suffer full Vallarian withholding. Suppose a planner then suggests a holding company in some third country that does have a good treaty with Vallaria, to receive the royalty and pass most of it on. That is Indofood's conduit, and beneficial ownership may defeat it. But even if the third country company were given real functions, the principal purpose test would ask why it was there. If one of the principal purposes was to secure the treaty rate, and nothing in the treaty's object supports giving it, the rate goes. The group never proposed it, and this book only uses it to show the test.

The same habit of mind runs right through this book. Chapter four showed the general anti-abuse rule and chapter twelve the unallowable purpose rule. Purpose tests are now the main way the law separates commerce from tax engineering, in treaties as at home.

Non-discrimination and the mutual agreement procedure.

Article twenty four protects foreign enterprises from being taxed more heavily than local ones. It has several limbs. Nationals of one state must not be taxed more burdensomely than nationals of the other in the same circumstances. A permanent establishment must not be taxed less favourably than a local enterprise carrying on the same activities. And a company owned by residents of the other state must not be subjected to more burdensome taxation than other similar local companies.

That last limb won a famous case. H M R C versus F C E Bank was decided by the Court of Appeal in October twenty twelve. Under the group relief rules of the time, before the year two thousand, a loss could pass between two United Kingdom companies only if the group link ran through a United Kingdom resident parent. F C E Bank and its United Kingdom sister company shared a parent in the United States. The Court of Appeal dismissed H M R C's appeal. The non-discrimination article of the nineteen seventy five treaty between the United Kingdom and the United States meant that group relief could not be refused simply because the common parent was American. The group relief rules have since been widened, and chapter fifteen explains how non-resident companies can now link a group. The lesson for the examination is the method. When a supplied treaty has a non-discrimination article, ask whether a United Kingdom rule treats a foreign-owned company worse than a domestic one in the same circumstances.

Article twenty five is the safety valve. If a company considers that the actions of one or both states will result in taxation not in accordance with the treaty, it may ask its own competent authority to intervene, even while domestic appeals remain open. In the United Kingdom the competent authority is H M R C. The authorities then try to resolve the case by agreement.

The time limits matter. The O E C D Model and the Instrument's article sixteen allow three years from the first notification of the action that causes the problem. United Kingdom law sets a fallback of six years after the end of the chargeable period, or any longer period the treaty allows. Once the authorities agree, United Kingdom law gives effect to the solution despite anything in any enactment, and consequential claims, such as group relief, can be made within twelve months of notification of the agreement even if the normal time limits have passed.

The old weakness of the procedure was that two authorities could simply fail to agree. H M R C's International Manual still says the treaty has no provision for that situation. That text is out of date wherever the Instrument's arbitration provisions apply, as they do between the United Kingdom and Ireland. There, unresolved cases can go to binding arbitration.

Tarnmoor will need this procedure. In Group Year Six, H M R C and Calder settle a transfer pricing dispute about the price at which Calder sold its process valve business to Tarnmoor Vallaria, increasing Calder's profit by two million pounds. Vallaria had computed Tarnmoor Vallaria's profits on the original price. Under the invented treaty's associated enterprises and mutual agreement articles, the group asks for a corresponding adjustment in Vallaria, and in Group Year Seven it gets one. Chapter twenty seven tells that story.

From treaty to credit.

Treaties tell the residence state to relieve double taxation. Part two of the International Act says how. Relief comes in two forms, and from two sources.

The two forms are credit and deduction. Credit relief reduces United Kingdom tax on the foreign income by the foreign tax on it, pound for pound. Expense relief lets the company instead deduct the foreign tax in computing its income. A company may elect that credit is not to be given, and then deduct the foreign tax. Credit is usually far better, because a pound of credit saves a pound, while a pound of deduction saves at most twenty five pence. Expense relief earns its place where credit would be useless, for example where the company has no United Kingdom tax to set the credit against because of losses. A deduction then at least increases the loss carried forward.

The two sources are treaty relief and unilateral relief. Where a treaty applies, credit is given under its relief article, as the International Act gives it effect. Where there is no treaty, the International Act gives unilateral relief on broadly the same footing, for foreign tax that corresponds to United Kingdom income tax or corporation tax. So a royalty from Marrovia, with no treaty, can still earn a credit for Marrovian withholding. The examiners' November twenty twenty five computation question turned on exactly that, overseas royalties with no treaty, relieved unilaterally.

Several rules apply to every credit. The foreign income is brought in gross. A company taxed on a royalty of one hundred, from which ten was withheld, is taxed on one hundred, not ninety, and then credits the ten. Credit is given only for foreign tax on the same income that the United Kingdom is taxing. Anson versus H M R C, decided by the Supreme Court on the first of July, twenty fifteen, shows how that question can be fought. Mr Anson, a United Kingdom resident, was a member of a Delaware limited liability company. The United States taxed him on his share of its profits as they arose. H M R C said the United Kingdom was taxing something different, his distributions from a separate company. Lord Reed, giving the judgment, held that Mr Anson was entitled to the profits as they arose, so the two countries were taxing the same income, and credit was due. H M R C later said the decision turned on its facts.

The company must also minimise the foreign tax. The credit is limited to the foreign tax that would have been payable if the company had taken all reasonable steps to minimise it, including claiming any treaty rates. If Calder had let Tarnmoor Vallaria withhold at a higher domestic rate instead of claiming the five per cent, the excess would not be creditable.

There are time limits. A company must claim credit within four years after the end of the accounting period in which the income is taxed, or, if later, within one year after the end of the accounting period in which the foreign tax is paid. Where a later adjustment in either country makes a credit too small or too large, H M R C's guidance says the time limit becomes six years from the adjustment.

Finally, one bar to signpost. Where a company has elected for the foreign branch exemption, its foreign permanent establishments' profits are exempt, and foreign tax on them is not creditable. Chapter twenty five owns that election.

The credit limit, source by source.

Here is the rule that wins and loses most marks. The credit cannot exceed the United Kingdom corporation tax attributable to the foreign income. The International Act puts it as a product, R times I G. R is the rate of corporation tax at which the company is charged for the period, before any credit. I G is the income or gain, after the deductions allocated to it. For a company paying the main rate in the financial year twenty twenty six, R is twenty five per cent.

Think of the credit as a refund capped at what the United Kingdom would have charged on the same slice of profit. The United Kingdom will give back foreign tax up to that cap and not a penny more. It will not use United Kingdom tax on other income to absorb a foreign tax rate higher than its own.

The limit is applied source by source. Each item of foreign income is tested on its own, against the United Kingdom tax on that item. A high-taxed source cannot shelter behind a low-taxed one. There was once a way round that for dividends, a system of onshore pooling of foreign tax, but it was repealed for dividends paid on or after the first of July, two thousand and nine, when foreign dividends became generally exempt. Never pool foreign taxes across sources in a current period answer.

What counts as a single source? For trade income the International Act gives a precise test. The credit cannot exceed the corporation tax attributable to the income from the particular transaction, arrangement or asset concerned, after a just and reasonable share of the trade's deductions. Those deductions can include expenses of connected companies that are reasonably attributable to the income. So you cannot claim that a royalty bears no costs because the research was paid for elsewhere in the group.

There is an examination wrinkle here. In the November twenty twenty five question, the examiners reported that nearly all candidates failed to realise that the royalties could be treated as one source and the foreign tax credits aggregated. Their suggested answer treated the royalties together, and the International Act supports it. Section forty seven says that where treaty royalties are paid for one asset in more than one foreign country, the royalties are treated as income from a single asset, so the credits are added together and tested against the United Kingdom tax on the combined royalty income. The November question involved no treaty, and this book could not confirm that section forty seven reaches unilateral relief, but the examiners took the same approach. Where several payers pay royalties for the same asset, treat them as one source, say why, and show the aggregated credit.

Now the misconception. Many people believe that foreign tax is always fully credited, so double taxation simply disappears. It does not. Credit stops at United Kingdom tax on that income, source by source. Foreign tax above the limit is lost. There is one exception, for the profits of a foreign permanent establishment. Unrelieved foreign tax on a permanent establishment's profits can be carried forward without time limit, or back to accounting periods beginning in the three years before, but only against United Kingdom tax on profits of the same establishment, and only by claim. H M R C's guidance gives four years for the claim. For anything else, the excess is gone.

Calder's refinery.

Back to the invented refinery contract. Calder's accounting periods matter. Its year to the thirty first of March, Group Year Three, contained the first eight months of the Vallarian project, and the permanent establishment made a loss of three hundred thousand pounds. Calder was taxed on its worldwide profits, so the loss simply reduced its United Kingdom profits. There was no Vallarian tax in a loss period, and nothing to credit.

Calder then changed its year end to the thirty first of December, with a nine month accounting period from the first of April to the thirty first of December, Group Year Three. In that period the project ended, and the permanent establishment made a profit of nine hundred thousand pounds, measured on United Kingdom principles. Vallaria taxed it at twenty per cent. As a story simplification, we ignore any Vallarian loss relief for the earlier loss. So the Vallarian tax is one hundred and eighty thousand pounds.

Now the United Kingdom. Calder pays the main rate, twenty five per cent. United Kingdom tax on the nine hundred thousand pounds is two hundred and twenty five thousand pounds. The credit limit is therefore two hundred and twenty five thousand pounds. The foreign tax, one hundred and eighty thousand, is below it, so the whole one hundred and eighty thousand is credited. Calder pays a United Kingdom top-up of forty five thousand pounds on the permanent establishment's profit. Twenty per cent abroad, plus five per cent at home, makes twenty five per cent overall. Where the foreign rate is below the United Kingdom rate, credit relief always lands you on the United Kingdom rate.

Two lessons sit inside the numbers. First, I G is measured by United Kingdom rules. It is the profit the United Kingdom is actually taxing, not the profit Vallaria measured. Second, the order of profits and losses matters. The United Kingdom relieved the early loss at twenty five per cent, then collected only a top-up on the later profit. That asymmetry, losses relieved at home and profits credited, is the heart of the branch exemption decision in chapter twenty five, where Calder decides not to elect.

Here is a labelled variation, not part of the story. Suppose Vallaria had measured the profit at one point two million pounds, perhaps by refusing a head office charge the United Kingdom allows. Vallarian tax would have been two hundred and forty thousand pounds. The limit stays at two hundred and twenty five thousand, so fifteen thousand pounds would be unrelieved. Could it be carried? Only against United Kingdom tax on the same permanent establishment. The establishment had made a loss in the earlier period, and it ceased when the project ended. There would be nothing to carry it to. The fifteen thousand pounds would be lost. The full working is in the reading edition.

Calder's royalty and the Patent Box.

The royalty from Tarnmoor Vallaria shows how a domestic relief can shrink a credit. From Group Year Four, Calder receives one point two million pounds a year, less Vallarian withholding at the treaty rate of five per cent, sixty thousand pounds. The royalty is income of Calder's trade. In our invented case, the costs attributable to the licence, the patent fees, the licence management and a just and reasonable share of overheads, come to three hundred thousand pounds, leaving a royalty profit of nine hundred thousand pounds.

Calder has also elected into the Patent Box, as chapter eleven explained. The Patent Box, in Part eight A of the twenty ten Act, gives a deduction that brings the effective rate on relevant intellectual property profits down to ten per cent. Keeping chapter eleven's simplification, the whole nine hundred thousand pounds is treated as relevant intellectual property profits. The deduction is three fifths of it, five hundred and forty thousand pounds, leaving three hundred and sixty thousand pounds taxable, and ninety thousand pounds of corporation tax.

Now the credit. H M R C's guidance on the Patent Box says the deduction is brought into the double tax relief calculation, and by reducing the corporation tax chargeable it may restrict the relief. That follows from the credit limit. The United Kingdom tax on this income is now ninety thousand pounds, not two hundred and twenty five thousand. The foreign tax is sixty thousand, which is still within the limit, so it is credited in full. Calder pays thirty thousand pounds of United Kingdom tax on the royalty. Add the sixty thousand withheld in Vallaria, and the total tax is ninety thousand pounds, ten per cent of the royalty profit. The Patent Box rate survives, and the treaty rate keeps the withholding inside it.

Now a labelled variation that shows the danger. Suppose the same licence had been granted to an unconnected company in Marrovia, which has no treaty with the United Kingdom and withholds thirty per cent on royalties. The withholding would be three hundred and sixty thousand pounds. Unilateral relief would allow a credit, but only up to the United Kingdom tax on the income. With the Patent Box that is ninety thousand pounds, so two hundred and seventy thousand pounds would be wasted. Without the Patent Box the limit would be two hundred and twenty five thousand pounds and the waste one hundred and thirty five thousand. A low effective United Kingdom rate makes high foreign withholding hurt more. The tax function models both before advising on where to license, and treaty access is often worth more than the headline rate.

Allocating deductions.

The credit limit depends on I G, the foreign income after deductions. So the question of which income bears which deductions matters. The International Act gives the company a choice. Where an amount is deductible from profits of more than one description, the company may allocate it in such amounts, and to such of its profits, as it thinks fit. Deductions from total profits, such as group relief and qualifying charitable donations, can therefore be steered. Loan relationship deficits and intangibles debits have their own detailed rules, which the reading edition lists.

The aim is to protect the credit. Set the deductions first against United Kingdom profits, which carry no foreign tax. Then set them against the foreign source with the lowest rate of foreign tax, because that source has the most room between its foreign tax and the United Kingdom tax on it. Keep the highest-taxed sources as large as possible.

Here is a labelled example, not part of the story. For this example only, suppose Calder had not elected into the Patent Box, and suppose that in one year it had United Kingdom trading profits of five hundred thousand pounds, the Vallarian royalty profit of nine hundred thousand pounds with sixty thousand pounds withheld, and a second royalty from an unconnected Marrovian licensee. That Marrovian royalty is five hundred thousand pounds gross, with one hundred and fifty thousand pounds withheld at thirty per cent, and a profit after its own costs of four hundred thousand pounds. Suppose Calder also has one million pounds of group relief and charitable donations to deduct. Taxable total profits are eight hundred thousand pounds, and corporation tax is two hundred thousand.

Allocate badly, putting four hundred thousand of the deductions against the Marrovian royalty, and its I G falls to nothing, so it gets no credit at all. The Vallarian royalty still credits its sixty thousand. Total credit, sixty thousand pounds. Tax payable, one hundred and forty thousand.

Allocate well. Five hundred thousand against United Kingdom profits, then five hundred thousand against the Vallarian royalty. The Vallarian I G falls to four hundred thousand, its limit to one hundred thousand, still well above the sixty thousand withheld, so its full credit survives. The Marrovian royalty keeps its four hundred thousand, and credits one hundred thousand pounds, its limit. Total credit, one hundred and sixty thousand pounds. Tax payable, forty thousand. The allocation alone is worth one hundred thousand pounds. And even then, fifty thousand pounds of Marrovian tax is lost, because thirty per cent is more than the United Kingdom's twenty five.

Dividends and underlying tax.

For most of the twentieth century, the big credit computations were on dividends. A United Kingdom parent received a dividend from a foreign subsidiary, and the United Kingdom taxed it, with credit for the withholding on the dividend and for the foreign tax the subsidiary had paid on the profits behind it. That second credit is underlying tax. It deals with economic double taxation, the same profit taxed in two different companies.

Today most foreign dividends received by a United Kingdom company are exempt, and chapter twenty five owns the exemption. So underlying tax matters only when a dividend is taxable. That happens when the dividend falls outside every exempt class, or is caught by the anti-avoidance rules, or is deductible for the payer abroad, or when the company chooses to elect for a dividend to be taxed. A dividend that was deductible abroad gets no underlying tax credit at all, only credit for any withholding.

The mechanics, in outline. Underlying tax is available where the United Kingdom company controls, directly or indirectly, at least ten per cent of the voting power in the payer. The credit is the foreign tax on the profits out of which the dividend is paid, in proportion to the dividend. The dividend is grossed up by the underlying tax, so the United Kingdom taxes the dividend plus the underlying tax.

Then comes a cap, often called the mixer cap. It limits the underlying tax to the dividend plus the underlying tax, multiplied by the United Kingdom rate. Its name is a relic of the days when groups mixed high-taxed and low-taxed dividends through intermediate companies to average out their foreign tax. A trap follows from the order of the steps. The dividend is grossed up by the full underlying tax, even the part the cap disallows.

Do not confuse the mixer cap with the credit limit. The mixer cap belongs to underlying tax, inside section fifty eight of the International Act. The credit limit, R times I G, applies to every source, and applies to the total of withholding and underlying tax together.

Here is a labelled example, not part of the story. Suppose Tarnmoor Vallaria paid Tarnmoor plc a dividend of one million pounds, and the parent elected to have it taxed. Vallaria withholds fifteen per cent, one hundred and fifty thousand pounds. Tarnmoor Vallaria paid tax at twenty per cent, so the underlying tax behind a one million pound dividend is two hundred and fifty thousand pounds. The gross income is one point two five million pounds and the United Kingdom tax on it, at twenty five per cent, is three hundred and twelve thousand, five hundred pounds. The mixer cap on underlying tax is the same three hundred and twelve thousand, five hundred, so all two hundred and fifty thousand passes it. But the total foreign tax, four hundred thousand pounds, meets the credit limit of three hundred and twelve thousand, five hundred. The United Kingdom collects nothing, and eighty seven thousand, five hundred pounds of foreign tax is wasted. Electing to tax brings no United Kingdom tax saving here. Chapter twenty five explains when an election might still make sense.

Double tax relief anti-avoidance.

A credit is worth more than a deduction, so it attracts schemes. Bayfine U K versus H M R C, decided by the Court of Appeal in twenty eleven, shows why. A United States parent owned two United Kingdom subsidiaries. They entered into forward contracts with matching results, a large profit in one and a large loss in the other. The transactions had no real commercial purpose beyond exploiting the different treatment of the profit in the two countries. The United States treated the profitable subsidiary as a disregarded entity, so it taxed the profit in the parent's hands. In the United Kingdom, the loss-making company surrendered its loss as group relief, and the profitable company claimed credit of nearly thirty six million pounds for the American tax its parent had paid on the same profit.

The High Court allowed the claim. The Court of Appeal reversed it. It read the treaty's relief article purposively, observing that the treaty's primary purposes are to eliminate double taxation and to prevent the avoidance of taxation. Granting the credit would have let the American tax wipe out United Kingdom tax on the profit, while the matching loss was relieved in the United Kingdom as well. Nor did the United Kingdom have to give unilateral relief for tax that had failed to qualify under the treaty.

Parliament has its own rules as well, in sections eighty one to eighty eight of the International Act. They apply where four conditions are met. Credit would be allowable. There is a scheme or arrangement whose main purpose, or one of whose main purposes, is to cause foreign tax to be taken into account. The scheme falls into one of the listed types, which cover, in outline, how foreign tax is attributed to income, the effect of paying the foreign tax, deemed foreign tax, claims or elections, reductions in someone's tax liability, and payments that are deductible for tax. And the total credits involved are more than minimal. Where those conditions are met, the effects are counteracted by whatever adjustments are necessary. Since the Finance Act twenty eighteen, the counteraction applies automatically, as part of self assessment, instead of waiting for H M R C to issue a notice. So the company must make the adjustment in its own return.

Two smaller rules complete the picture. Underlying tax at a rate above the United Kingdom rate, boosted by an avoidance scheme, is restricted to the United Kingdom rate. And where a foreign permanent establishment's loss has been used abroad against other people's profits, foreign tax on the establishment's later profits is reduced for credit purposes by the tax saved.

The tax function, and how the examiner tests it.

For Tarnmoor's tax team, led by Tom Hesketh in our invented case, treaty and credit work is a cycle. Before money moves, the team checks which treaty applies, in its synthesised form, and claims the reduced rate at source, with residence certificates where the paying country wants them. It documents beneficial ownership and the commercial purpose of any structure, with the principal purpose test in mind. When the foreign tax is paid, it collects the receipts, because the credit is for tax actually paid. In the return it computes the credit source by source, allocates deductions to protect the credit, and records the Patent Box effect on the royalty credit. It diarises the claim deadlines, and it watches for foreign adjustments, which reopen the credit. And when two countries tax the same profit after a transfer pricing adjustment, it goes to the mutual agreement procedure within the time limits.

Planning sits on top of the cycle. Where foreign tax will exceed the United Kingdom tax on a source, the excess is a pure cost, so the team asks whether the source tax can be reduced by a treaty claim, whether the income can be earned through a country with a better treaty for genuine commercial reasons, and whether a loss year makes expense relief better than credit. Where a domestic relief such as the Patent Box will cut United Kingdom tax on a foreign stream, it checks that the cut does not simply convert United Kingdom tax into wasted foreign credit. And it never builds a structure whose only story is the treaty rate, because the principal purpose test reads the board papers too.

The examiner tests this material in three ways. First, treaty extracts. A question supplies an article, for residence, a permanent establishment or withholding, and wants you to apply its words to the facts. Use the extract, not the Model. Second, double tax relief inside a computation. The November twenty twenty five question had overseas royalties under withholding with no treaty, so unilateral relief. The May twenty twenty five question had royalties at different withholding rates. The May twenty twenty six question asked about a branch abroad, with double tax relief as one strand. Third, discussion, where you explain the credit limit, the allocation of deductions or the choice between credit and exemption.

The traps are consistent. Apply the limit source by source. Gross up the foreign income. Do not pool. Remember minimisation. Allocate deductions to United Kingdom profits first. Do not call the credit limit the mixer. And do not spend time on dividend credits when the dividend is exempt.

What to take away.

A double tax treaty allocates taxing rights between two countries and limits the tax of the country of source. It never creates a charge. It reaches a United Kingdom tax return through section two of the International Act and an Order in Council, and since the first of October, twenty eighteen, many treaties are edited by the Multilateral Instrument, so read the synthesised text.

Read any treaty with one method. Classify the income, find the article, decide which state may tax and how much, then apply the relief article. In the examination, use the supplied extract, not the Model.

Business profits are taxed in the source country only through a permanent establishment. Under our invented treaty with Vallaria, a construction project counts only after twelve months. Calder's fourteen month refinery contract created one.

Withholding caps on dividends, interest and royalties depend on beneficial ownership. Indofood, decided by the Court of Appeal in two thousand and six, denied a conduit company that status. The principal purpose test now denies a benefit where obtaining it was one of the principal purposes of an arrangement.

Non-discrimination protects foreign-owned companies, as F C E Bank showed for group relief. The mutual agreement procedure resolves taxation not in accordance with a treaty, and arbitration now backs it up where the Instrument applies.

Double tax relief is usually a credit, by treaty or unilaterally. Foreign income is brought in gross, the foreign tax must be on the same income, and it must be minimised. Claims are due within four years, or one year after the period in which the foreign tax is paid if later.

The credit cannot exceed R times I G, United Kingdom tax on that income after its share of deductions, source by source. For trade income, that means the particular transaction, arrangement or asset. Excess foreign tax is lost, except on a foreign permanent establishment's profits, where it can be carried forward, or back three years, against the same establishment.

In our invented case, Calder's refinery profit of nine hundred thousand pounds bore one hundred and eighty thousand pounds of Vallarian tax, all credited, with a United Kingdom top-up of forty five thousand pounds. Its royalty from Tarnmoor Vallaria, one point two million pounds, bore sixty thousand pounds of withholding, all credited even after the Patent Box cut United Kingdom tax on it to ninety thousand pounds.

Allocate deductions to United Kingdom profits first, then to the lowest-taxed foreign source. In our labelled example that was worth one hundred thousand pounds.

Underlying tax matters only for taxable dividends. It needs a ten per cent holding, the dividend is grossed up by the full underlying tax, and the mixer cap limits it to the gross dividend at the United Kingdom rate. Onshore pooling ended on the first of July, two thousand and nine.

And credits attract schemes. Bayfine read the treaty purposively, and sections eighty one to eighty eight of the International Act counteract schemes automatically.

All of this is the law for the financial year twenty twenty six.

A credit is one way to relieve foreign profits. Chapter twenty five, on going abroad through a branch or a subsidiary, looks at the other way, exemption, and asks when a group should choose it.

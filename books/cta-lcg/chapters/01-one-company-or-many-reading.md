# Part One: The Larger Company

## Chapter 1: One company or many?

On 1 April, at the start of what our story will call Group Year One (GY1), a regional engineering business owned by one family stopped being a family firm. Calder Valve Engineering Ltd woke up that morning as it had gone to bed: same works, same 300 staff, same order book. By lunchtime its shares belonged to Tarnmoor plc, an invented listed engineering group, which had paid £32m for them.

Readers of *The Living Law* (TKS) have met Calder before. It is invented too, like every company and person in Tarnmoor's story. In *The Living Law*, chapter 5, Calder was a company on its own with no associated companies: taxable total profits (TTP) of £2,000,000 and corporation tax of £500,000, paid in four quarterly instalments of £125,000. One company, one return, one bill.

Nothing in the purchase changed Calder's legal identity. It still computes its own profits, files its own return and owes its own tax. And yet, from that 1 April, almost every number around it moves. The point at which it must pay tax early moves. The rules on how it prices its dealings with sister companies switch on. A board in Leeds now answers for its tax governance. Its interest costs are tested against a group it has never met.

So here is the question this chapter answers. **When a company joins a large group, is it still one taxpayer among many, or has it become a small part of one large taxpayer?** The short answer is both, and the skill of this paper lies in knowing which rule takes which view.

This is the first chapter of Part One, on the larger company. It sets up the whole book and builds on *The Living Law*, chapter 20 (corporation tax foundations) and chapter 24 (groups). No single syllabus row belongs to it. Instead it gives you two tools used in every later chapter, a **threshold ladder** and a **group-definitions matrix**, and introduces the invented group whose story runs through the book.

## Why one company stays one taxpayer

*The Living Law*, chapter 20, told how a company became a person in law. The leading case is *Salomon v A Salomon and Co Ltd* [1897] AC 22 (HL): a properly formed company is a legal person distinct from its members, however its shares are held. Corporation tax, created by the Finance Act 1965, was built on that foundation. The taxpayer is the company.

This is the **separate entity principle**. Each company in a group computes its own TTP for its own accounting periods, on its own CT600, and pays its own tax. Each has its own losses, capital allowance pools and base costs. If TES sells a building to TEL, two taxpayers have dealt with each other.

Why does the law insist on this when a group plainly runs as one business?

- **Company law** gives each company its own assets and debts; its creditors look only to it.
- **Groups change shape** constantly. A system based on the separate company follows acquisitions and disposals without reopening a consolidated position.
- **Minority shareholders** (Greyfell and Northlight in Helmside) need each company's profit to be its own.
- **A buyer inherits the company's tax history**, which only makes sense if that history belongs to the company.

But the principle has costs: a loss in one company and a profit in a sister would be taxed as if strangers held them, and an asset moved between sisters would trigger a gain though nothing has left the group. So Parliament has built a **toolkit of bridges**: group and consortium relief (CTA 2010 Parts 5 and 5A), no gain, no loss transfers within a gains group (TCGA 1992 s 171), group roll-over (s 175), group relief from stamp duty (FA 1930 s 42) and SDLT (FA 2003 Sch 7).

The UK never abandons the separate entity; it bridges it, rule by rule, each bridge with its own conditions. Only in its newest regimes, the **corporate interest restriction** (TIOPA 2010 Part 10) and **Pillar Two** (F(No.2)A 2023 Parts 3–4), does the law begin to treat the group as a single economic unit. That tension is the book's first debate (L1), developed in chapters 15, 28 and 30.

## The group's tax charge is not its tax bill

> **Misconception.** *The Living Law*, chapter 24, tackled "a group pays tax as one". The grown-up version is: **"the tax charge in the consolidated accounts is the group's tax bill."**

People believe it because it is the number they see. Tarnmoor's annual report shows one income tax expense, and analysts quote the effective tax rate that comes from it.

But the consolidated charge is an accounting figure:

- it adds current tax across **every** company, UK and overseas, so it includes Vallarian and Marrovian tax that HMRC never sees;
- it includes **deferred tax**, which is not a payment but a recognition of tax that will fall due, or be saved, in later years (chapter 6);
- it reflects consolidation adjustments that no tax return contains.

The UK tax bill is a set of **separate liabilities, company by company**, each computed on that company's TTP and paid on its own timetable. In GY1, TEL's CT after group and consortium relief is **£2,633,750** (TTP £10.535m × 25%); Calder, in its first group accounting period, computes CT of **£750,000** (TTP £3.0m × 25%) before setting off its RDEC. There is **no group return** for corporation tax.

There is a second reason the two numbers differ. The accounts measure profit under accounting standards; tax law then adjusts it, adding back some costs, deducting others, replacing depreciation with capital allowances. Some differences reverse later and some never do. The consolidated charge captures the reversing ones as deferred tax; the return simply taxes this year's adjusted profit.

The practical lesson is that a tax function **reconciles**: it explains the consolidated charge to the board, auditors and market, builds up the company returns underneath it, and makes the two stories agree. "The tax charge in the accounts" and "the tax payable" are different requirements with different answers, and tax-accounting tails to computation questions are now common (N25 Q1, M25 Q2, M24 Q2; a standalone 10-mark deferred tax question in M26 Q6).

## Applying the guest lists to Tarnmoor

Run Tarnmoor through the lists in GY1 and the family changes shape.

**Associated companies.** TPLC controls everything it owns outright: TPLC, TEL, TES, TFL, TWS, TVS, TCM, TIL, and Calder from 1 April. **Nine.** The two overseas companies count (association turns on control, not residence). Tarnmoor Pumps does not count (dormant). Helmside does not count: 45% is not control, and no single shareholder controls it. For marginal relief a company counts if associated **at any time in the AP**, so each company's limits are divided by **9** at once (itself plus eight associates). The QIP count is taken on a different day (see "Tarnmoor on the ladder").

**Group relief.** Every wholly owned company passes the 75% tests, but losses pass only between UK related companies. TVS and TCM are outside the charge, so their losses stay abroad. **TIL**, though incorporated in Ireland, is UK resident while its board meets in Leeds, so it can surrender and claim like any other UK company. **Helmside** is not in the group relief group at all: it is a **consortium company**, and its losses flow in proportion to the members' holdings (chapter 15).

**Gains group.** Almost the same list, overseas companies included, with TPLC as principal company.

**Worldwide group (CIR).** TPLC and every subsidiary in its consolidated accounts, including TVS, TCM and the dormant Pumps company. Helmside sits outside it as an unconsolidated joint venture.

| Company | Associated (marginal relief divisor 9) | 75% group relief group | Gains group | Worldwide group | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TPLC | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ (principal) | ✓ (ultimate parent) | |
| TEL, TES, TFL, TWS | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | |
| Calder (from 1 April GY1) | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | Joins every list at once |
| TIL | ✓ | ✓ (UK resident) | ✓ | ✓ | Irish-incorporated |
| TVS, TCM | ✓ | ✗ (not UK related) | ✓ | ✓ | Overseas |
| Tarnmoor Pumps | ✗ (dormant) | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | Nothing to surrender |
| Helmside | ✗ (45%) | ✗ (consortium) | ✗ | ✗ | Consortium company |

So which is the real Tarnmoor group? There is no single answer, and that is the lesson. Move a building from TES to TEL, and the gains group and the SDLT group matter. Lend from TFL to TVS, and the transfer pricing participation test matters. Borrow from a bank, and the worldwide group matters.

Calder's purchase shows why. On 1 April it joins every list at once, and each membership switches on a different consequence. The rest of this chapter is about the consequences that come from size.

## What makes a company larger: the threshold ladder

The paper's title speaks of larger companies; this book uses **"larger company"** as a working term, not a legal one. No statute defines it. Instead, dozens of rules each draw their own line.

Why should size matter? Three reasons recur:

1. **Revenue.** A small number of large businesses pay a large share of corporation tax, so the state wants their money sooner and their risks visible.
2. **Capability.** A group with a tax function, auditors and systems can carry burdens that would crush a small company.
3. **International consensus.** Working through the OECD, governments have agreed common lines, most famously **€750m** of revenue, above which country-by-country reporting and the global minimum tax apply.

Every line is a **cliff edge**: one pound or euro either side changes a company's obligations, and groups cross thresholds by accident through acquisitions, mergers, currency movements or one good year.

Two further points. HMRC's Large Business directorate works with most UK businesses whose turnover exceeds £200m (GOV.UK, "HMRC's compliance approach for large businesses"). And the thresholds use **different numbers, from different accounts, over different periods**: profits of the current accounting period (QIPs), the previous financial year (SAO, tax strategy, UTT), two of the previous four periods (Pillar Two). Applying one test to all of them is a classic error.

> **Exam lens: thresholds and groups**
> - **Grades (2026 grid):** CT self-assessment 1; SAO 1; tax strategy 1; group relief (consortia included for LCG) 1; CIR 1; TP and APAs 1; CFCs 1; notification of uncertain tax treatment 3; MTT and DTT 3. No row is "thresholds": they are tested inside these rows.
> - **Past appearances:** **N24 Q5 (20 marks)**: a US multinational buys a UK SME; the marking guide gave 4 marks for filing and QIPs in a large group (thresholds divided across the group, payment dates), 3 for CbC reporting, 6.5 for TP now applying (not an SME) with master and local file contents, 3.5 for CIR (file to protect the position, reporting company, abbreviated return) and 3 for stamp duty on the share purchase. Examiners: most found TP, but some wrote at length on it and ignored easier points; some raised hybrids with no trigger. **N23 Q5 (10 marks)**: SAO thresholds known but misapplied (which companies count). **M25 Q3 (15 marks)**: tax strategy thresholds with different year ends; candidates apportioned subsidiaries' figures instead of using the accounts for the year ending in the parent's financial year. **M25 Q2**: very large company instalments with changing forecasts done badly.
> - **Style:** technical prose; list the obligations a transaction switches on, with the threshold and the authority; 0.5–1 mark per point.
> - **Traps:** dividing by the wrong number (associated companies, counting the company itself), or counting on the wrong day (QIPs: the day before the AP begins; marginal relief: any time in the AP); forgetting that overseas companies count as associates; SAO applies to **UK-incorporated** companies; very large companies have no first-year exception; mixing the tests' measurement periods; treating TP as optional once a target joins a large group.
> - **Layout:** a short table or numbered list of obligations, each with threshold, deadline and penalty.

## Who does the work

A larger company's tax is done by a system, and the paper expects you to know the parts.

- **The group tax function** (Tom Hesketh's team in our invented case): prepares or reviews every company's return; manages instalments; runs TP documentation; prepares the CIR return and the tax numbers in the accounts; keeps the threshold and group-list map.
- **The board**: approves the published tax strategy; owns the group's tax risk appetite.
- **The CFO as senior accounting officer** (Nadia Kerr): takes reasonable steps to ensure appropriate tax accounting arrangements and certifies each year (FA 2009 Sch 46).
- **The audit committee and external auditors**: test the tax numbers in the accounts.
- **HMRC**: the Large Business directorate works with around 2,000 of the UK's largest businesses; each has a **Customer Compliance Manager** who learns the business and coordinates HMRC's specialists. HMRC's Tax Compliance Risk Management manual (TCRM1000) describes rating customers' tax risk on a spectrum from low to high. A good relationship means issues raised early and resolved in real time.
- **Specialists**: TP economists, valuers, R&D claim teams, stamp taxes lawyers, IFRS specialists.

The work runs to a calendar: instalments through the year; year-end tax numbers for the annual report; returns within 12 months, with the CIR return; the SAO certificate by the end of the accounts filing period (6 months after the year end for a public company, 9 months otherwise). Each deadline belongs to a particular company, and each miss carries its own penalty (chapters 3 and 4).

The CIOT's description of the paper explains why this matters. Candidates "are not expected to have deep knowledge of all tax areas but should be able to identify what the potential tax issues are", and should know specialist areas well enough to "coordinate the work of the firm's specialists". Read that as a job description: the examiner rewards the adviser who sees every issue a transaction raises, says precisely what it means, and knows who to call.

## Mapping the thresholds every year

> **Going further: the tax function's threshold map**
>
> A tax function maps every threshold and every group list **every year**, and again whenever the group changes shape.
>
> - **Acquisitions** are the usual trigger. A group just under €750m can cross it by buying one company; CbC reporting, TP records and Pillar Two follow.
> - **Each new subsidiary changes the associated company count**, lowering every company's thresholds. When Tarnmoor buys Brackenwell Sensors Ltd (invented) on 1 July GY2, the marginal relief count rises to 10 at once; the QIP thresholds fall to £2,000,000 / £150,000 (£20m and £1.5m ÷ 10) from GY3.
> - **Disposals and demergers** work the other way (the marginal relief count is 11 in GY5 and GY6 and 10 in GY7; the QIP count stays at 10).
> - **Currency**: the highest line is drawn in euros, so a weaker pound can carry a sterling group across it without a single extra sale.
>
> **A joiner's checklist** (picked up in chapters 3, 4, 10, 14 and 20): different year end (Calder keeps 31 March until GY3); returns and instalments to take over, on an earlier timetable once the QIP count catches up (for Calder, one period later); losses restricted after a change in ownership; loss of SME status (an R&D-intensive SME loses ERIS and falls back on the merged RDEC); a new SAO answerable for its arrangements; TP switching on.
>
> **Professional judgement.** Thresholds are measured on different numbers, from different accounts, over different periods. Write each test down with the source of each figure, so the SAO can rely on it, and review the map whenever the deal team is busy: the tax function is often the last to hear about a purchase and the first to be blamed for a missed deadline.

## What to take away

A company that joins a large group is still a separate taxpayer. *Salomon* confirmed that a company is a person distinct from its members, and corporation tax taxes that person: each company computes, files and pays its own tax, and there is no group return for corporation tax.

The law bridges the gap between the many companies and the one business with a toolkit of group regimes, **each with its own guest list**: associated companies (control; overseas companies count; dormant and passive holding companies ignored); the 51% group (GPAs, SSE, s 190 recovery, tax strategy and UTT); the 75% group with equity holder tests (group relief), and the consortium (5% members owning 75%); the gains group (75% plus effective 51%); stamp duty and SDLT groups (75% of shares, profits and assets, with arrangements tests); the worldwide group (CIR); consolidated groups for Pillar Two and CbC reporting.

**The consolidated tax charge is not the tax bill.** It includes overseas and deferred tax; the UK bill is a set of company liabilities.

**The ladder, for FY2026:** profits over £1.5m make a company large for QIPs, and over £20m very large, both divided by 1 + associated companies; net interest over £2m brings the CIR into play; £5m is the deductions allowance; £200m turnover or £2bn balance sheet switches on SAO, tax strategy and UTT; €750m switches on CbC reporting, TP records and Pillar Two; leaving the SME category switches on transfer pricing.

For Tarnmoor in GY1: revenue £1,180m (about €1,357m at the invented rate); a marginal relief divisor of 9 at once (limits £5,556 / £27,778); a QIP divisor of 8 for the 31 December companies (£187,500 / £2,500,000), because HMRC's manual counts associates on the day before the AP begins; and Calder still **large** in its first group AP (divisor 1: four instalments of £187,500 in months 7, 10, 13 and 16), becoming very large (divisor 9, £2,222,222) one period later.

And the job: identify the issues, explain them precisely, and coordinate the specialists.

To answer this chapter's question: Calder is **one company among many** for computing and paying its tax, and **part of one large group** for almost every threshold that decides how. Next, chapter 2 asks where all of these rules actually live, and how an adviser finds them.

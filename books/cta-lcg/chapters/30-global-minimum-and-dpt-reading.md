# Chapter thirty: The global minimum and the end of diverted profits tax.

## A floor under the world.

In October 2021, more than 135 countries agreed something that tax policy had never managed before. They agreed a minimum. The largest multinational groups would pay at least **15%** on their profits in every country where they operate, or someone, somewhere, would collect the difference. The deal was struck through the OECD and its wider Inclusive Framework. The UK wrote its share of the deal into the **Finance (No. 2) Act 2023, Parts 3 and 4** ("the Pillar Two legislation").

Seven years earlier, the UK had reached for a blunter weapon. In his Autumn Statement on **3 December 2014**, the Chancellor, George Osborne, announced a **diverted profits tax** (DPT). The press called it the "Google tax". It sat outside corporation tax, charged a higher rate than corporation tax, and made the company pay before it could argue. For accounting periods beginning on or after **1 January 2026**, it is gone. Its work now belongs to a charge inside corporation tax, on what the law calls **unassessed transfer pricing profits** (UTPP).

Both changes share a single idea: profit should be taxed somewhere, at a fair rate, and a group that shifts it away should gain nothing. So this chapter asks two linked questions. **When a large group's profits end up somewhere taxed below the floor, who collects the difference? And now that DPT has gone, what stops a group from pricing its own dealings so that profit quietly leaves the UK?**

This is chapter 30, the last chapter of Part Four (profits that cross borders). Everything in it is **awareness (grade 3)** on the LCG Advanced Technical paper: multinational top-up tax, domestic top-up tax and diverted profits tax. None was examined between May 2023 and May 2026. *The Living Law* (TKS) only signposted these rules, so we start from first principles, but keep to the depth an awareness topic deserves. Its close neighbours are chapters 26 (CFCs) and 27 (transfer pricing). Throughout, the law is that for **FY2026**, under Finance Act 2026.

## Why a minimum tax.

Start with the problem. Profit is easy to move and hard to pin down. A group can put its patents, brands or lending in a subsidiary in a low-tax country and pay that subsidiary royalties or interest from everywhere else. Transfer pricing asks whether the price is right (chapter 27). CFC rules ask whether the parent's country should tax the subsidiary's profit (chapter 26). But neither touches the low rate itself, and for decades countries competed on that rate, each cutting a little to attract the mobile profit of large groups.

The minimum tax does not forbid a low rate. A country may still tax at 9%, or at nothing. What changes is that the low rate stops working for the largest groups, because the difference up to 15% is collected anyway. **Picture a floor fitted under every country's tax rate: if a country leaves a gap below the floor, another country fills it.** The precise rule: for each country (territory) where a group operates, the group works out an effective tax rate on a common measure of profit; where that rate is below 15%, a top-up tax brings it up to 15%.

In December 2021 the OECD published model rules, the **Global Anti-Base Erosion (GloBE) rules**. As commentators describe the arrangement, countries were not obliged to adopt them, but those that did were expected to follow the model so that the pieces would fit together across borders. A floor only works if every country measures the room the same way. The whole project is usually called **Pillar Two**. (A separate "Pillar One" is not on this paper.)

**The debate (L5).** Is it a good idea? The paper will not ask you to settle it. Supporters say it ends a race to the bottom that drained public revenue and rewarded paper structures over real investment. Critics say it erodes a country's right to set its own taxes, blunts the incentives small economies use to attract real activity, and buries large groups in compliance. Both points have force, and economists still argue about the evidence. The law, for now, has chosen the floor, and the UK has built it into two taxes.

## The United Kingdom's two taxes, and who is in.

| | Multinational top-up tax (MTT) | Domestic top-up tax (DTT) |
|---|---|---|
| Where | F(No.2)A 2023 Part 3 (ss 121–264) | F(No.2)A 2023 Part 4 (s 265 ff) |
| From | APs commencing on or after **31 December 2023** (s 264) | APs commencing on or after **31 December 2023** |
| Who pays | UK parent members of qualifying groups, on low-taxed members **abroad** (income inclusion rule); from **31 December 2024** also the undertaxed profits rule (Part 3 Ch 9A, ss 229A–229J, inserted by FA 2025 Sch 4) | UK entities of qualifying groups (and of large UK-only groups), on low-taxed **UK** profits |
| Nature | IIR = main collection mechanism; UTPR = backstop (HMRC, MTT01100) | The UK's qualified domestic minimum top-up tax (QDMTT) (HMRC, MTT01200) |

**The income inclusion rule (IIR)** makes a parent pay top-up tax on the low-taxed profits of its members abroad. **The undertaxed profits rule (UTPR)** is the backstop: where low-taxed profit escapes every IIR, perhaps because the parent's country has none, other countries collect a share. **Domestic top-up tax** applies the same arithmetic to the group's UK entities, so that any top-up on UK profit is collected by the UK, not by a parent's country abroad.

So the **order of collection** is: (1) the country where the profit arises, through its own QDMTT, which HMRC's guidance says is credited against GloBE top-up tax in the group calculation (with any shortfall still payable under the GloBE rules); (2) the parent's country, through the IIR; (3) the UTPR, which mops up anything left.

**Who is in (F(No.2)A 2023 s 129).** A group is "qualifying" if the revenue in the ultimate parent entity's consolidated financial statements **exceeds €750m** in **at least two of the four** previous accounting periods (periods other than 12 months scaled by days/365) (Condition A), and at least one member is located in the UK (Condition B). Notice "exceeds": the statute says it, and so does HMRC's manual (MTT01100). HMRC's online guidance "Check if you need to report Pillar 2 top-up taxes" says "750 million euros **or more**". The difference matters only for a group at exactly €750m; there, the statute governs.

> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

In our invented case Tarnmoor's revenue exceeded €750m before GY1 (chapter 1), so TPLC registered long ago as the group's **filing member**.

## Measuring the rate.

The GloBE rules do not use any country's own tax computation. They start from the **accounting profit** of each group entity, as used for the consolidated accounts, and make a set of adjustments of their own: the result is **GloBE income**. Against it they set **covered taxes**: broadly taxes on income, including the rules' own deferred tax adjustments and some taxes paid by other members on that entity's profit (such as a parent's CFC charge: next section).

The **effective tax rate (GloBE ETR)** is worked out **territory by territory**, not company by company. All the group's entities in one territory are blended:

> **Jurisdictional ETR = total covered taxes of the territory ÷ total GloBE income of the territory**
>
> If the ETR is below 15%, **top-up percentage = 15% − ETR** (HMRC, MTT01100).

The top-up percentage is not applied to the whole profit. First the rules deduct the **substance-based income exclusion** (SBIE): a deduction in proportion to the payroll costs and tangible assets the group has in the territory, which HMRC's manual describes as corresponding to the return the group might expect on those assets and employees (MTT32000 ff). What remains is the **excess profit**, and the top-up percentage applies to that. A territory with many staff and factories may owe little or nothing even at a low rate; a territory holding only patents or loans gets almost no exclusion. (The SBIE percentages are not taught here; they are in the legislation and MTT32000–32030.) In the Tarnmoor figures below, **we leave the SBIE out**, which overstates the top-up a little, and **treat accounting profit as GloBE income**.

**Misconception: "Under Pillar Two every company must pay at least 15%."** People believe it because the headline is "a 15% global minimum". It is wrong in three ways:
1. It reaches only groups over the **€750m** threshold.
2. The test is a **blended rate for each territory**: one company at 10% and its sister at 20% may together clear the floor.
3. The measure is **GloBE income and covered taxes**, not taxable profit and tax paid. A UK company whose current tax is low because of full expensing does not by that alone create a top-up, because the GloBE rules count deferred tax as well.

As chapter 6 explained, the accounts themselves keep Pillar Two out of deferred tax under the temporary exception in **IAS 12 para 4A** (IASB, 23 May 2023), and show any Pillar Two current tax **separately** (para 88B).

So the rule is a floor for each territory of a large group, measured on its own base. It is not a minimum rate for each company.

## Tarnmoor's Marrovian test.

In our invented case, the group's finance company, **Tarnmoor Capital Ltd (TCM)**, is resident in **Marrovia**, an invented country with a corporation tax rate of **9%** and, in the story, **no domestic minimum tax** of its own. In **GY5** its profit is **£5.1m** (interest £5.4m less expenses £0.3m). Marrovian tax at 9% is **£459,000**.

The UK also taxes some of that profit. TCM is a CFC (chapter 26). After the finance company (Chapter 9) exemption, 25% of its profit is chargeable: **£1,275,000**. TPLC pays a **CFC charge of £204,000** (25% × £1,275,000 = £318,750, less creditable Marrovian tax 9% × £1,275,000 = £114,750).

The Pillar Two step: **F(No.2)A 2023 s 179** allocates a parent's tax under a CFC tax regime (the UK CFC charge included) **down to the CFC** for MTT purposes, so it counts as a covered tax of Marrovia. For **mobile income** the amount pushed down is **capped at (15% − local ETR) × that income**. Here: 6% × £5.1m = **£306,000**. The CFC charge of £204,000 is within the cap, so all of it moves.

> **Worked example 30.2: Marrovia, GY5 (invented; simplified: SBIE ignored; accounting profit = GloBE income; no other GloBE adjustments)**
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | GloBE income (TCM: interest 5,400,000 − expenses 300,000) | 5,100,000 |
> | Covered taxes: Marrovian tax at 9% | 459,000 |
> | Covered taxes: UK CFC charge pushed down (s 179) | 204,000 |
> | *Cap on push-down for mobile income: (15% − 9%) × 5,100,000 = 306,000; 204,000 within it* | |
> | **Total covered taxes** | **663,000** |
> | **ETR** (663,000 ÷ 5,100,000) | **13.0%** |
> | Top-up percentage (15% − 13%) | 2.0% |
> | Excess profit (no SBIE in this simplification) | 5,100,000 |
> | **Top-up tax (2% × 5,100,000)** | **102,000** |
> | Less QDMTT paid in Marrovia (none: invented) | — |
> | **MTT payable by TPLC under the IIR** (inclusion ratio 100%) | **102,000** |
>
> **Check:** Marrovian tax £459,000 + CFC charge £204,000 + top-up £102,000 = **£765,000 = 15.0% × £5.1m**. Without the push-down, the ETR would be 9% and the top-up 6% × £5.1m = £306,000, the same total that the CFC charge and the top-up make together. The floor holds whichever route the money takes.
>
> **Why the ETR is always 13% here.** The CFC charge is 25% × (25% − 9%) × profit = **4% of profit**, so ETR = 9% + 4% = 13%, whatever the profit. In **GY1–GY4** (TCM profit £3.3m; CFC charge £132,000; cap £198,000) the same arithmetic gives ETR 13.0% and a top-up of **£66,000 a year** (chapter 6's GY2 rate reconciliation leaves it out as a simplification).

**Elsewhere the floor does not bite on the story's figures.**
- **Vallaria** taxes Tarnmoor Vallaria SA (TVS) at **20%**: above 15%, no top-up (and, where available, the transitional safe harbour avoids the computation: below).
- The **UK** companies pay **25%**: on the story's figures, no DTT.
- **Tarnmoor Ireland Ltd** (TIL), Irish resident from 30 June GY4, pays Ireland's **12.5%** trading rate, below the floor. Who collects any Irish top-up depends on Irish law, which this paper does not examine (and this book has not researched); the story leaves it aside.

## Safe harbours.

A full GloBE computation for every territory every year is a heavy task. **Safe harbours** treat a territory's top-up as nil without the full calculation.

**Transitional CbCR safe harbour (F(No.2)A 2023 Sch 16 Part 2, paras 3, 7–9).** It borrows figures from the group's (qualifying) country-by-country report. A group may elect for it for APs **commencing on or before 31 December 2026 and ending on or before 30 June 2028**. A territory passes if it meets **any one** of three tests:

| Test | Condition |
|---|---|
| De minimis | Revenue **< €10m** and profit before tax **< €1m** |
| Simplified ETR | Simplified covered taxes ÷ profit before tax ≥ **15%** (periods beginning before 2025), **16%** (beginning in 2025), **17%** (beginning on or after 1 January 2026) |
| Routine profits | Profit before tax ≤ the SBIE |

And there is a sting: **"once out, always out"**. If a group does not use the safe harbour for a territory in one year, it cannot return to it for that territory later.

**Real-calendar illustration (not the story).** A group with a calendar year end can use the harbour for its year to 31 December 2026, at the 17% test: its last year. A group whose year runs 1 July 2026 – 30 June 2027 also gets that year (commencing before 31 December 2026; ending before 30 June 2028). A group with a 1 January 2027 start does not.

> **Worked example 30.3: the transitional safe harbour tests for Tarnmoor (invented; we run the numbers on this year's rules)**
>
> | Territory | De minimis | Simplified ETR (17%) | Routine profits | Result |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Vallaria (TVS profit £30.0m in GY2; tax 20%) | Fails (far above €10m / €1m) | **Passes** (20% ≥ 17%) | Not needed | Top-up nil without full calculation |
> | Marrovia (TCM, GY5) | Revenue about €6.2m (< €10m) but profit about €5.9m (> €1m): **fails** | 9% (or 13% with the CFC charge): **fails** | Finance company with a small office: SBIE minimal: **fails** | Full GloBE computation (worked example 30.2) |
>
> Euro figures at the invented €1.15 per £: revenue £5.4m ≈ €6.21m; profit £5.1m ≈ €5.87m.

**Two other harbours to recognise.**
- **UTPR transitional safe harbour (Sch 16 para 12A):** protected the UPE's territory where its nominal corporate rate is **at least 20%**, for APs commencing on or before 31 December 2025, ending before 31 December 2026, and not longer than 12 months.
- **QDMTT safe harbour (Sch 16A Part 1):** where a group pays an **accredited** QDMTT in a territory, it may elect (annually, for all members there) to treat the top-up there as nil under everyone else's rules, so one calculation does (HMRC, MTT15110–15120).

FA 2026 Sch 8 adds **simplified calculations for non-material members** (Sch 16A Part 3).

The administration is where an awareness question may well find its marks.

|---|---|---|

| GloBE information return (GIR) | **15 months** after the end of the AP; **18 months** for the first; no deadline before **30 June 2026** (FA 2025 Sch 4 para 52); or an overseas return notification where the GIR is filed with a qualifying authority abroad | Sch 14 para 10 |

| Payment | First AP ending on or before 31 December 2024: 30 June 2026; first AP ending later: 18 months after its end; later APs: the later of 30 June 2026 and 15 months after the AP end | GOV.UK "Pay Pillar 2 top-up taxes" |

**Tarnmoor.** TPLC is the filing member. For **GY5** (AP ending 31 December GY5) its GIR, its UK return and payment of the **£102,000** are due by **31 March GY7** (15 months). In the group accounts the top-up is **current tax**, disclosed separately, with no deferred tax (IAS 12 paras 4A, 88B; chapter 6).

**The rulebook keeps moving: FA 2026 s 50 and Sch 8.** Changes include elective qualifying domestic top-up taxes; application of the IIR to permanent establishments; flow-through UPEs; deferred tax exclusions (APs ending on or after 21 July 2025); a **qualifying undertaxed profits tax** (APs beginning on or after 31 December 2025); DTT for REITs; exchange rates; **group relief payments as covered taxes**; CFC mobile income for DTT (cap at 15% of the CFC entity's adjusted profits); the **simplified calculations for non-material members**; and a 12-month extension for pre-2026 elections. **General commencement: APs beginning on or after 31 December 2025**, or from 31 December 2023 by an irrevocable "retrospection election" (FA 2026 Sch 8 para 53).

> **Coming next: proposed, not law.** In January 2026 the OECD released the **"side-by-side" package**, designed, as its name suggests, to let some countries' own minimum tax systems operate alongside the GloBE rules. A ministerial statement (7 January 2026) said the UK would implement it through the Finance Bill 2026-27, and on **13 July 2026** the government published draft legislation (policy paper "Pillar 2: side-by-side package and further amendments to Multinational Top-up Tax and Domestic Top-up Tax"). The side-by-side measures would apply to APs beginning on or after **1 January 2026** and include elective safe harbours, one of which, according to professional commentary, would remove MTT for groups with US parents; further technical amendments would apply mainly from APs beginning on or after 31 December 2026. **Not law, and not examinable on FA 2026.**

> **Exam lens: Pillar Two (MTT and DTT)**
> - **Grade:** Multinational Top Up Tax **3**; Domestic Top Up Tax **3** (2026 grid; added at 3 for 2026; also 3 in the 2028 grid). Check the 2027 grid when published.
> - **Past appearances:** none, M23–M26.
> - **Style:** if it appears, expect a few recognition marks inside a larger group question (an acquisition taking a group over €750m; a low-tax subsidiary in a CFC question; a tax accounting tail), not a computation.

> - **Traps:** saying every company pays 15%; attempting a full GloBE computation unasked; forgetting that a CFC charge is pushed down; stating the side-by-side package as law.

## Diverted profits tax, and pay now, argue later.

Now the other half of the chapter. **DPT** was in **FA 2015 Part 3**, for accounting periods beginning on or after **1 April 2015** (s 116; straddling periods split). It had two limbs:
1. **Insufficient economic substance** (ss 80–81): a UK company (or UK PE) whose arrangements with a related party involved entities or transactions lacking economic substance, producing an effective tax mismatch.
2. **Avoided PE** (s 86, with an exception for limited UK-related sales or expenses in s 87): a non-UK company arranging its affairs to avoid a UK taxable presence.

The rate was **25%** (s 79 as enacted), set above corporation tax on purpose; for APs beginning on or after **1 April 2023** it rose to **31%** (FA 2021 s 8), six points above the new 25% main rate.

The design mattered as much as the rate. HMRC issued notices, and the company had to **pay before it could argue**; review and appeal came later (ss 101–102). The aim was plain: a group facing an immediate cash bill at a premium rate had every reason to agree its transfer pricing on the ordinary CT basis instead.

**The case: *Glencore Energy UK Ltd v HMRC* [2017] EWHC 1476 (Admin).** HMRC issued Glencore Energy UK Ltd with a **charging notice under FA 2015 s 95** for **£21,129,349 plus interest**, for its nine-month AP **1 April – 31 December 2015**. Glencore did not wait for the statutory review and appeal; it asked the High Court for judicial review of the notice. **Green J** (judgment 29 June 2017) **refused permission**: the statute's own route, a review under s 101 and an appeal to the First-tier Tribunal, was an adequate alternative remedy, and judicial review is a remedy of last resort; he also refused permission on wider discretionary grounds. A separate ruling held that the High Court could not grant permission to appeal its own refusal ([2017] EWHC 1587 (Admin)). Glencore took the matter to the Court of Appeal (*R (Glencore Energy UK Ltd) v HMRC* [2017] EWCA Civ 1716, 2 November 2017; Gloster, Sales and Singh LJJ); later commentary cites that decision for the same principle, that a suitable alternative remedy will usually exclude judicial review.

Glencore's lesson is the one the design intended: **pay now, argue later**. The tax would be argued, if at all, after the money had moved.

The weakness lay elsewhere. A free-standing tax, outside corporation tax, sat awkwardly with double tax treaties and the mutual agreement procedure (MAP). That is the problem the replacement solves.

## From diverted profits tax to unassessed transfer pricing profits.

**FA 2026 s 46 and Sch 5** repealed FA 2015 Part 3 for **accounting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2026** (Sch 5 para 1(3)). In its place is a new **Part 4A of TIOPA 2010, ss 217A–217T** (with Sch A1 for partnerships and Lloyd's).

**Commencement in practice (real-calendar illustration, not the Tarnmoor story):**

| Company's accounting period | Regime |
|---|---|
| 1 January – 31 December 2026 | UTPP for the whole period |
| 1 April 2025 – 31 March 2026 | DPT for the whole period; UTPP from the AP beginning 1 April 2026 |
| 1 October 2025 – 30 September 2026 | DPT for the whole period, although most of it falls after 1 January 2026 |

Old periods keep the old tax, so an enquiry into an earlier year can still involve DPT, and an adviser must check the period before naming the charge.

**Misconception: "DPT still exists."** For current periods it does not. For any AP beginning on or after 1 January 2026, the answer is **UTPP**, and on a paper examined on FA 2026, that is the name to use.

**What UTPP taxes (s 217B).** Profits that a **transfer pricing requirement** brings into charge but that the company has not reflected in its own return. Chapter 27 explains the TP rules themselves. The new charge has no measure of its own: it borrows the arm's length price and taxes the gap between that price and the return. The charge is **corporation tax**, so (HMRC's TIIN, 26 November 2025) treaties and MAP apply to it. The **UTPP rate** is the underlying CT rate **plus 6%** (s 217A(2)–(4)): **31%** where the main rate of 25% applies (HMRC, INTM489155). DPT's separate notification requirement has not been kept.

**And the avoided-PE limb?** It has no direct successor in Part 4A. On this book's reading, that work now falls to the rewritten PE definition in FA 2026 Sch 7 (CTA 2010 s 1141(1)(b): the "principal role" test for dependent agents; chapter 23). The government has not said so in exactly those words (reasoned inference).

## Three gates.

UTPP is not charged at the premium rate just because a price was wrong. **Three conditions** must all be met (s 217C, with s 217D on the mismatch; HMRC, INTM489115–489150):

| Gate | Test | HMRC guidance |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **Effective tax mismatch outcome** (ETMO) | The **corresponding amount** (tax payable by the other party, in any period, on profits corresponding to the UTPP; may be nil) is **less than 80%** of the **underlying CT amount** (UK CT at the underlying rate on the UTPP) | INTM489130–489135 |
| 2. **Tax design condition** (TDC) | Reasonable to assume the arrangements were **designed to reduce, eliminate or delay UK tax** (income tax by withholding, or CT) | INTM489140–489145: not the same as a "main purpose" test; UTPP does not apply merely because a group moves a whole activity to a lower-tax territory; the target is separating UK economic activity from the income it produces |
| 3. **Not excepted loan relationships** | The UTPP must not arise **wholly** from excepted loan relationship arrangements (CTA 2009 Part 5 and Part 6 deemed loan relationships, or a loan relationship with a hedging derivative under Part 7) | INTM489150: a loan inside a wider arrangement does not automatically make the outcome excepted |

HMRC's own ETMO example (INTM489135): a UK company pays £20m for services with an arm's length price of £15m. UTPP £5m; underlying CT amount £1.25m; the overseas recipient pays £1m of tax on the £5m. **£1m is exactly 80% of £1.25m, so there is no ETMO.** HMRC adds that the test can be revisited: if the foreign tax is later refunded (for example by loss relief), the corresponding amount falls to nil and the ETMO is met.

The third gate echoes an old trap: the M23 examiners said DPT "does not apply to loan relationships", and UTPP keeps an equivalent exception.

> **Worked example 30.4: the premium (labelled hypothetical, not Tarnmoor)**
>
> For this example, suppose a UK distributor in another group overpays a royalty by **£4,000,000** to a related company taxed at **5%**.
>
> | | £ |
> |---|---|
> | UTPP (excess over arm's length) | 4,000,000 |
> | Underlying CT amount (25%) | 1,000,000 |
> | Corresponding amount (5% × 4,000,000) | 200,000 |
> | Ratio (200,000 ÷ 1,000,000) | 20%: **< 80%: ETMO met** |
> | If the TDC is also met: UTPP at 31% | **1,240,000** |
> | CT if the company had self-assessed the adjustment (25%) | 1,000,000 |
> | **Premium for not self-assessing** | **240,000** |

> **Worked example 30.5: Tarnmoor's provisions against the 80% gate (invented; simplified)**
>
> | Counterparty territory | Local rate | ÷ UK 25% | ETMO possible? |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Vallaria | 20% | **80%** | **No**: 80% is not less than 80% |
> | Marrovia | 9% | 36% | Yes |
>
> **Calder → TVS (GY3).** Calder sold its process-valve customer contracts and know-how to TVS for £6.0m; the price was later raised by **£2.0m** in the transfer pricing settlement (chapter 27). UK CT on £2.0m at 25% = **£500,000**; Vallarian tax on the matching profit at 20% = **£400,000** = **80%**: no ETMO, so UTPP could not apply (simplification: Vallarian deductions for TVS's own amortisation and timing are ignored). The sale also moved a real business, which on HMRC's guidance points away from the TDC.
>
> **UK → Marrovia.** A Marrovian provision would open gate 1. The only one ever proposed, **Project Undertow** (TFL's £80m perpetual notes to TCM, GY4; chapter 29), was a loan relationship (gate 3 would likely have shut UTPP out anyway, and the hybrid rules were the real problem), and the board rejected it.

## Pay first, and what the examiner wants.

The procedure keeps the old pay-first design, now inside corporation tax (ss 217F–217S):

| Step | Rule |
|---|---|
| Preliminary notice | By a **designated HMRC officer**, **no later than 4 years** after the end of the AP |
| Representations | Within **30 days** (60 for transparent entities), on limited grounds |
| Assessment | Within **60 days** of the preliminary notice (90 for transparent entities), or 30 days after a closure notice if later |
| Period for amendments | **15 months**: the company may amend its return to bring the profits into ordinary CT (not in the last 21 days); HMRC may correct or increase the assessment; no closure notice before the period ends |
| Payment | **Pay first**: postponement only for amounts equal to tax already paid on the corresponding profits (s 217K) |
| Appeal | Only after paying and after the period for amendments, within 30 days; the tribunal may reduce the charge to the non-UTPP rate if a condition is not met (s 217M) |
| No reliefs | "No relief, deduction or set-off of any description" against UTPP profits or UTPP-rate tax; no TMA s 59DA repayment claims (ss 217Q–217S) |

The lesson for a tax function: **the charge is a lever, not a destination.** Correct transfer pricing in the company's own return removes the exposure, and even after a notice, an amendment within the period brings the profits back to the ordinary rate.

**How the examiner tests it.** These topics are **awareness, grade 3**. None was examined M23–M26. At awareness level the marks go to recognition: a sentence or two showing the rule exists and when it applies.

DPT has a history on this paper, as a **trap**. After **M23 Q3** (15 marks: a loan from a US company that might take 60% control; TP, CIR, withholding), the examiners reported that "time was wasted on DPT, which does not apply to loan relationships". After **N23 Q1** (20 marks: residence and a CFC finance company) they noted that some candidates "strayed into DPT and CIR". The same discipline holds now: for any period beginning on or after 1 January 2026, say **UTPP, not DPT**; and do not raise it unless the question gives you a low-taxed related party, a pricing problem and a hint of design.

## What to take away.

**Pillar Two** puts a **15%** floor under the profits of large multinational groups, measured **territory by territory**. More than 135 countries agreed it in October 2021, and the UK enacted it in **F(No.2)A 2023 Parts 3–4**.

**Multinational top-up tax**, from 31 December 2023, makes a UK parent pay top-up on low-taxed members abroad through the **income inclusion rule**, with the **undertaxed profits rule** as backstop from 31 December 2024. **Domestic top-up tax** collects any top-up on UK profits first.

A group is in if consolidated revenue **exceeds €750m in at least two of the previous four periods** (s 129). The statute says "exceeds"; some HMRC guidance says "or more".

**ETR** = covered taxes ÷ GloBE income for each territory; **top-up percentage = 15% − ETR**, applied to profit after the **substance-based income exclusion**.

In our invented case, Marrovia's 9% plus the pushed-down CFC charge of **£204,000** gives **13.0%** on **£5.1m** in GY5; TPLC pays **£102,000** of MTT; everything together is exactly 15%.

The **transitional CbCR safe harbour** runs out with periods commencing in 2026, when its simplified ETR test is **17%**; once out, always out.

Register within **6 months** of the end of the first qualifying AP; file the GIR and UK return within **15 months** (18 for the first). The **side-by-side package is proposed, not law**.

**DPT** ran from 1 April 2015 at **25%**, later **31%**, with a pay-now, argue-later design that survived the *Glencore* challenge. For APs beginning on or after **1 January 2026** it is repealed. **UTPP** is charged as **corporation tax at 31%** (main rate + 6%), through three gates: the other party's tax is **under 80%** of the UK tax; the **tax design condition** is met; the profits do not arise wholly from **excepted loan relationships**. Pay first; no reliefs; correct pricing in the return removes the charge.

So, **who collects the difference?** The country where the profit arises, if it chooses to; then the parent's country; then everyone else. **And what stops profit leaving through mispricing?** Transfer pricing, backed now by a premium rate of corporation tax that a group can avoid simply by getting the price right.

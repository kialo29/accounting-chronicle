Chapter sixteen: Company gains, property and leases.

On the thirtieth of April in Group Year Three, Tarnmoor Estates exchanged contracts to sell its depot for six million pounds. Tarnmoor, its companies, its people and its numbers are invented for teaching, as they are throughout this book. Tarnmoor Estates is the group's property company. It had bought the depot in June two thousand and four for two and a half million pounds, and for all those years it had let the building to Tarnmoor Engineering, which used it for its trade.

A gain of three and a half million pounds, on the face of it. Yet the group's tax function knew that the corporation tax due on that sale could be anything from about two hundred thousand pounds to more than eight hundred thousand pounds, depending on three things. What the law lets the company deduct. What the group does with the money next. And when the sale is treated as happening.

This chapter works through those questions for land, buildings and leases. It is the second chapter of Part Three. Corporation tax on chargeable gains, leases of land, part disposals, roll-over relief, property income and the transactions in land rules are all core topics on the paper. Wasting assets, deferred consideration and indexation are non-core. The chapter builds on The Living Law, chapter twenty three, which taught company gains at the first level. It covered indexation, the matching rules, the shareholding exemption and roll-over relief in outline. Here we take the property side to Advanced Technical depth.

The law is mostly in the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act nineteen ninety two, which I will call the Gains Act. Property income sits in the Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, the two thousand and nine Act, and the transactions in land rules in the Corporation Tax Act twenty ten, the twenty ten Act. We apply the law for the financial year twenty twenty six.

A gain in the corporation tax computation.

A company does not pay capital gains tax. Its chargeable gains are computed under the Gains Act and then fed into its corporation tax computation, where they become part of total profits and are taxed at the company's ordinary rate. For a large company in the financial year twenty twenty six, that is twenty five per cent. There is no annual exempt amount for a company and no separate rate for gains.

The territorial rule is simple for a United Kingdom resident company. It is chargeable on gains on assets wherever they are, subject to the foreign branch exemption that chapter twenty five explains. A non-resident company is chargeable on gains on United Kingdom land, on assets that derive at least seventy five per cent of their value from United Kingdom land where it has a substantial indirect interest, and on assets used for a United Kingdom permanent establishment. Finance Act twenty twenty six rewrote the link between those assets and the establishment, for chargeable periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six. Chapter twenty three owns those rules.

The mechanics follow a fixed order. For each accounting period, take the company's chargeable gains, deduct its allowable losses of the same period, then deduct capital losses brought forward. The net figure enters total profits. If losses exceed gains, the excess carries forward, without time limit, against future gains only.

That last word is the first trap. A capital loss can never be set against income. Not against trading profits, not against rents, not against interest. The examiners reported after the May twenty twenty six sitting that some candidates still set a capital loss against income. And the reverse is not true either. A trading loss can be set against total profits, which include gains, so trading losses can shelter gains, but capital losses can never shelter trading profits.

Two more rules complete the frame. A capital loss must be quantified and notified to H M R C, in practice through the company's return, before it counts. And since the first of April, twenty twenty, capital losses carried forward fall within the loss restriction that chapter fourteen explained. Above the five million pound deductions allowance, they can shelter only half of a company's chargeable gains. In a large group, with the whole allowance used elsewhere, a capital loss brought forward may be worth much less than its face value.

Inside a group, there is one more tool. A gain in one company and a loss in another can be matched by a joint election, without moving any asset. Chapter seventeen owns that election, and the no gain, no loss transfers that sit beside it. In this chapter we stay with a single company and its property.

Disposals, dates and connected persons.

A chargeable gain needs a disposal of an asset. All forms of property are assets, including options, debts and rights, and anything a company has created. A disposal includes a sale, a gift, a part disposal and the receipt of a capital sum derived from an asset, such as compensation or an insurance payout. If an asset becomes of negligible value, a company can claim to be treated as selling it and buying it back at that value, which crystallises the loss.

When does the disposal happen? For an asset sold under a contract, the law says the disposal is made when the contract is made, not when the asset is conveyed. For a conditional contract, it is made when the condition is satisfied. That is why Tarnmoor Estates' depot was sold on the thirtieth of April in Group Year Three, the day contracts were exchanged unconditionally, even though completion came later. The date decides the accounting period, the instalment payments and, as we will see, the roll-over window.

Next comes the price. Normally the consideration is what the buyer pays. But where a disposal is not a bargain made at arm's length, or is a gift, the law substitutes market value. And between connected persons, a transaction is always treated as not at arm's length, so market value applies whatever the contract says. Companies under common control are connected with each other, and a company is connected with the person who controls it.

Connected parties bring a second rule that catches large groups. A loss on a disposal to a connected person can be set only against gains on other disposals to that same person, at a time when they are still connected. Practitioners call it a clogged loss. Within a gains group the no gain, no loss rule overrides market value altogether, so the clogged loss rule matters mostly for disposals to companies that are connected but outside the gains group, such as a sister company held at sixty per cent, or a company controlled by the same outside investor.

Then there is the series rule. Suppose a company sells a site to a connected person in three separate plots, each worth less on its own than its share of the whole. The law says that where two or more linked transactions with connected persons fall within six years, each disposal is valued as a proportionate part of the aggregate market value of everything transferred. Each later transaction reopens the earlier ones. The rule stops a group breaking a valuable whole into cheap pieces.

What it cost, and the frozen thermometer.

The gain is the consideration less the allowable deductions in section thirty eight of the Gains Act. There are three kinds. First, the cost of acquiring the asset, with the incidental costs of acquisition, such as legal fees, surveyors' fees and stamp duty land tax. Second, enhancement expenditure. Third, the incidental costs of disposal, such as agents' fees and legal costs on the sale.

Enhancement is where the exam lives. The law allows expenditure incurred to enhance the value of the asset only if it is reflected in the state or nature of the asset at the time of the disposal. A company that spent money on an extension and then pulled it down cannot deduct that money when it sells. The examiners set exactly that in May twenty twenty six, with a workshop that had been built, then demolished, before a factory was sold. Spending on establishing, preserving or defending title, such as legal costs in a boundary dispute, is also allowed.

Two exclusions matter for companies. Nothing is allowed that was deductible in computing trading or property income, so repairs that were deducted against rents cannot be deducted again. And capital allowances never reduce a gain. They only restrict a loss, so that a company cannot get relief twice for the same fall in value. Chapter nine showed how that works for fixtures.

Then comes indexation. Companies alone kept indexation allowance after it was abolished for individuals. It increases the base cost in line with the retail prices index, from the month the expenditure was incurred. But it is frozen. For disposals on or after the first of January, twenty eighteen, indexation runs only to December twenty seventeen. The Living Law, chapter twenty three, compared it to a thermometer that stopped. Every company asset bought before twenty eighteen carries indexation to that frozen date, and nothing after it. Assets bought later carry none.

Indexation has one more limit. It cannot create a loss, and it cannot increase one. If a company sells for less than cost, indexation is nil. If the gain before indexation is smaller than the indexation, the result is no gain and no loss. The examiners reported after May twenty twenty six, and earlier after May twenty twenty five, that candidates still used indexation to enlarge a loss.

Now the depot. Tarnmoor Estates paid two and a half million pounds in June two thousand and four. The indexation factor from June two thousand and four to December twenty seventeen is nought point four eight nine. So indexation is one million, two hundred and twenty two thousand, five hundred pounds. Proceeds of six million pounds, less cost of two and a half million, less indexation, leave a chargeable gain of two million, two hundred and seventy seven thousand, five hundred pounds. Hold on to that number. Without any relief, tax at twenty five per cent would be five hundred and sixty nine thousand, three hundred and seventy five pounds.

That figure may sound familiar. In chapter three, Tarnmoor Estates paid its instalments for Group Year Two on a forecast that included this gain, then found that the sale had slipped into Group Year Three. It overpaid by exactly that amount and surrendered part of the refund to Tarnmoor Engineering. The date of disposal moved the tax by a whole year.

Selling part of an asset, and proving the value.

Property is often sold in pieces. A strip of land goes to a highway authority, a plot is sold off a larger site, a lease is carved out of a freehold. Each is a part disposal, and the question is how much of the original cost goes with the part sold.

The law answers with a fraction. The cost attributed to the part disposed of is the whole cost multiplied by A over A plus B, where A is the consideration for the part disposed of and B is the market value of the part retained. The rest of the cost stays with the retained part, ready for its own disposal later. Where expenditure relates wholly to one part, such as a road built only on the part sold, it is allocated directly and the fraction is not needed.

Here is a short example, invented and outside the story. Suppose a company bought a site for one point two million pounds, after December twenty seventeen, so there is no indexation. It sells a strip for five hundred thousand pounds, and the land it keeps is worth one and a half million. The fraction is five hundred thousand over two million, one quarter. So three hundred thousand pounds of cost goes with the strip, the gain is two hundred thousand pounds, and nine hundred thousand pounds of cost remains for the land retained.

Notice what drives the answer. B, the value of what is kept, is not a price anyone paid. It is a valuation. The same is true of market value on a connected party disposal, and of the value of a reversion when a lease is granted. Large groups therefore live with valuation risk, and the law gives them a way to manage it.

H M R C offers post-transaction valuation checks. After the disposal, and before filing, the company sends form C G thirty four with its valuation. H M R C's guidance asks for the form at least three months before the filing date. If H M R C agrees the value, it will not challenge that value when the return is filed, provided the facts given were complete. Shares and other assets are valued by H M R C's Shares and Assets Valuation team. Land is valued by the Valuation Office Agency. It is a simple, free step that removes one of the commonest causes of enquiry into a gains computation, and a good tax function builds it into the timetable for every material disposal that rests on a valuation.

Wasting assets, chattels and money paid later.

Some assets wear out. The law calls an asset with a predictable life of fifty years or less a wasting asset, and it treats plant and machinery as always having such a life. For most wasting assets the cost is written off on a straight line over the life, so a buyer of a ten-year asset who sells after five years deducts only half the cost. Leases of land follow their own curve, which we come to next.

For companies the important wasting assets are plant and machinery, and here capital allowances take over. Movable plant is a chattel. Wasting chattels are normally exempt, but not if they qualified for capital allowances. Where the plant qualified for allowances, the straight-line write-off is switched off, and any loss is restricted by the allowances given. The practical result is easy to state. Plant sold for less than cost gives no gain and, after the restriction, no allowable loss, because the capital allowances system has already dealt with the fall in value. Plant sold for more than cost gives a chargeable gain on the excess, while the capital allowance disposal value is capped at cost.

There is one small relief left. A chattel sold for six thousand pounds or less is exempt, even if it qualified for allowances, and above that limit the gain is capped at five thirds of the excess over six thousand pounds. Fixed plant that forms part of a building is not a chattel at all, because it is part of the land. The examiners mixed these rules into a property question in May twenty twenty six, and the best answers sorted each asset quickly into building, fixed plant or movable plant before computing anything.

Now money paid later. If part of the price is deferred but fixed in amount, the law brings the whole of it into the computation at the date of disposal, with no discount for waiting and no allowance for the risk of non-payment. If it later proves irrecoverable, the computation can be adjusted. For a company there is a twist. Where the deferred price takes the form of a debt owed to the seller, which is a creditor relationship, the law brings it in at its fair value instead, and from then on the debt is taxed under the loan relationship rules of chapter twelve.

Where the deferred sum cannot be ascertained, because it depends on future profits, the right to it is a separate asset. Chapter eighteen tells that story, with the earn-out on the sale of Tarnmoor Actuators.

Finally, where a price is paid in instalments over more than eighteen months, the law lets the tax be paid by instalments too, over up to eight years, as H M R C allow. It is a cash-flow relief, not a reduction of the gain.

Leases as a melting block of ice.

A lease is an asset like any other, but it has one unusual feature. It ends. A lease with nine hundred and ninety nine years to run is, for practical purposes, as good as the freehold. A lease with two years to run is nearly worthless. So the law treats a lease of land as a wasting asset, but only once fifty years or less remain. A lease with more than fifty years to run is a long lease and is not a wasting asset at all.

Think of a lease with fifty years left as a block of ice. It melts slowly at first, then faster and faster towards the end. A lease with twenty five years left is not worth half of a fifty-year lease. It is worth much more than half, because most of the value of a lease lies in its early years, and the value collapses in the last few. The law captures that curve in a table, Schedule eight to the Gains Act, reproduced in the tax tables you will have in the exam. Fifty years scores one hundred. Twenty five years scores eighty one point one. Ten years scores forty six point six nine five. One year scores five point nine eight three.

On the disposal of a short lease, the allowable cost is restricted to the cost multiplied by the table percentage at disposal, divided by the table percentage at acquisition. Indexation, where it applies, is then computed on that restricted figure, not on the original cost.

Two rules decide whether the table applies. First, short-lease status is judged at the date of the transaction. A lease bought with sixty years to run and sold with forty five left is a short lease when sold, and H M R C's guidance confirms that the short-lease rules apply even though it was long when acquired. The examiners stressed this after the November twenty twenty five sitting. Second, the distinction between assignment and grant. An assignment is the sale of an existing lease. The whole lease passes, the seller drops out, and the computation is a simple disposal of a wasting asset. A grant is the creation of a new lease out of a larger interest, which is a part disposal and, for a short lease, brings in an income charge on the premium. The examiners reported after November twenty twenty four that candidates treated assignments of short leases as grants.

That second mistake is our misconception. People assume that any premium received for a short lease is partly income. It is not. Only a premium on the grant of a lease of fifty years or less has an income element. A price received on an assignment is pure capital.

Now the story. Tarnmoor Estates also held a leasehold office as an investment, not used in any group trade. It had bought the lease, with exactly fifty years to run, for eight hundred thousand pounds. In Group Year Three it assigned the lease, with exactly twenty five years left, to an unconnected buyer for one million pounds.

The allowable cost is eight hundred thousand pounds times eighty one point one over one hundred, which is six hundred and forty eight thousand, eight hundred pounds. Half the term has gone, yet less than a fifth of the cost has melted away. The gain before indexation is three hundred and fifty one thousand, two hundred pounds.

But a lease bought with fifty years to run and sold with twenty five left has been held for twenty five years, so Tarnmoor Estates bought it long before December twenty seventeen, and indexation applies. In our invented case, take the indexation factor from the month of purchase to December twenty seventeen as nought point two five, a round figure chosen for the story. In the exam you would compute it from the retail prices index in the tax tables. Indexation is a quarter of the restricted cost, one hundred and sixty two thousand, two hundred pounds, and the chargeable gain is one hundred and eighty nine thousand pounds. The full working is in the reading edition.

Granting a lease for a premium.

Why should a premium ever be income? Because rent and premium are two ways of being paid for the same thing. A landlord can let a warehouse for thirty years at a full rent, or at a lower rent plus a large payment up front. If the up-front payment were all capital, landlords would convert rent into premium and pay less tax, or none. So the two thousand and nine Act treats part of a premium on a short lease as property income.

The formula is easy to carry in your head. Where a lease of fifty years or less is granted for a premium, the amount taxed as property income is the premium multiplied by fifty minus the number of complete years of the lease less one, all over fifty. For a lease of exactly thirty years that is fifty minus twenty nine, twenty one fiftieths, forty two per cent. Each extra year of term takes two per cent off the income element, so a premium for a lease of one year is taxed wholly as income, and one for a lease of fifty years only two per cent. The rules reach further than cash premiums, to work a tenant is required to do, sums paid instead of rent and payments for the surrender of a lease.

The rest of the premium is capital, and the grant is a part disposal of the landlord's interest. Here H M R C's guidance on Schedule eight adjusts the usual fraction. The amount taxed as income is left out of the consideration. In the fraction, the top line uses only the capital part of the premium, but the bottom line uses the full premium plus the value of the reversion that the landlord keeps. Many textbooks and candidates use the full premium on top, or the capital part on both lines. Neither matches H M R C's worked example.

Now the story. In Group Year One, Tarnmoor Estates bought a run-down freehold warehouse for one and a half million pounds. Values in the area rose fast. In Group Year Four it granted a thirty-year lease to an unconnected logistics operator for a premium of two million pounds plus a yearly rent. After the grant, the freehold reversion was worth four million pounds.

The income element is forty two per cent of two million pounds, eight hundred and forty thousand pounds, taxed as property income of Tarnmoor Estates' property business. The capital part is one point one six million pounds. The cost of the part disposed of is one and a half million times one point one six million over six million, which is two hundred and ninety thousand pounds. So the chargeable gain is eight hundred and seventy thousand pounds, with no indexation because the warehouse was bought after twenty seventeen. One point two one million pounds of cost stays with the reversion. Altogether, one point seven one million pounds of a two million pound premium is taxed in the year of grant, corporation tax of four hundred and twenty seven thousand, five hundred pounds.

The tenant's side completes the picture. A trading tenant can deduct the part of the premium taxed on its landlord, spread over the term of the lease. The logistics operator deducts eight hundred and forty thousand pounds over thirty years, twenty eight thousand pounds a year. Rent is deductible in full each year. For a tenant, rent beats premium on timing.

Two variations complete the topic. If the lease granted had been longer than fifty years, there would be no income element at all. The whole premium would be consideration for an ordinary part disposal, with the full premium in the fraction. That was the shape of a twenty-mark question in May twenty twenty six, which also tested that indexation cannot create a loss. And where a short sub-lease is granted out of a short head lease, the law uses the lease table again to measure the cost used up, and deducts the income element from the gain rather than the proceeds, though never so as to create a loss.

Property income for a company.

Tarnmoor Estates' rents and its premium income fall into one United Kingdom property business. For a company, all its lettings of United Kingdom land form a single business, and its profits are computed on the same principles as trading profits, following the accounts. Rents are taxed as they accrue. Repairs, insurance, agents' fees and the running costs of the buildings are deducted. Capital allowances are available on plant used in the business, such as lifts and heating systems in a let building, though not on plant in a dwelling.

One difference from an individual landlord matters a great deal. A company's interest on borrowings to buy or improve property is not an expense of the property business. It is a loan relationship debit, as chapter twelve explained. Tarnmoor Estates' interest on its loan from Tarnmoor Finance therefore sits in its non-trading loan relationship deficit, which it sets against its property profits under the loan relationship rules, not as a property expense.

Timing differs from the accounts in one important place. A landlord's accounts usually spread a premium over the life of the lease. The tax rules do not. The income element of a premium is taxed in the accounting period in which the lease is granted, so the accounts must be adjusted, and the gap between tax now and profit later is a timing difference for deferred tax, as chapter six explained. Tarnmoor Estates' eight hundred and forty thousand pounds is taxed in Group Year Four, whatever its accounts show for that year.

Money can also flow the other way. A landlord sometimes pays a tenant to take a lease, a reverse premium. The two thousand and nine Act treats a reverse premium received by a company as a revenue receipt. If the company takes the premises for its trade, it is a trading receipt. Otherwise it is a receipt of its property business. It is normally taxed as the accounts spread it, over the lease, but where the parties are connected and the terms are not at arm's length, the whole amount is taxed at once.

When a property business makes a loss, the twenty ten Act lets the company set it against its total profits of the same period. Any excess carries forward against total profits of later periods, within the loss restriction, or can be surrendered as group relief, but only so far as it exceeds the company's gross profits, as chapter fifteen explains.

Why roll-over relief exists, and how it works.

A business that sells its factory to move to a better one has not cashed out. It has swapped one productive asset for another. Taxing the gain on the old factory would take cash out of the business at the very moment it is reinvesting, and would discourage firms from moving to better premises. Roll-over relief, in sections one hundred and fifty two to one hundred and fifty eight of the Gains Act, lets a trader postpone the gain by deducting it from the cost of the replacement. The tax is deferred, not forgiven. It comes back when the new asset is sold, unless that gain is rolled over again.

The conditions are strict, and the examiners award marks for stating them. The old asset must have been used only for the purposes of the trade throughout the period of ownership. If it was not, the gain is apportioned and only the trade part qualifies. The new asset must be acquired for the trade and taken into use for it on acquisition. Both assets must fall within the qualifying classes, though not necessarily the same class. For property, the class that matters is land and buildings occupied and used only for the trade, together with fixed plant and machinery that is not part of a building. For a company, goodwill is outside roll-over altogether, because goodwill and other intangibles are dealt with under the corporate intangibles rules, which have their own reinvestment relief, as chapter eleven explained.

Then the window. The new asset must be acquired within the period beginning twelve months before the disposal and ending three years after it. H M R C can extend the window, but should not be relied on to do so.

The relief must be claimed, within the usual four-year time limit for company claims. If the company has not yet reinvested when it files, it can make a provisional claim, declaring that it intends to reinvest within the window. For a company, a provisional claim lapses on the fourth anniversary of the end of the accounting period of the disposal, and the tax becomes due with interest from the normal date, as if the relief had never been claimed.

Now the rule at the heart of the topic. Where only part of the proceeds is reinvested, the gain cannot all be rolled over. The amount chargeable now is the amount of the proceeds not reinvested, capped at the gain. The rest of the gain is rolled over. So if the proceeds not reinvested are equal to the gain or more, no relief is available at all.

This is the misconception the examiners keep meeting. People say roll-over taxes the part of the gain you did not reinvest. It does not. It taxes the part of the proceeds you did not reinvest. The law assumes that the cost comes back first and the gain last. Only once you have reinvested at least the original cost and indexation does any relief begin, and only when you reinvest the whole proceeds is the whole gain rolled over. The examiners reported after November twenty twenty four, and again after May twenty twenty six, that candidates computed the chargeable amount from the gain instead of from the proceeds not reinvested.

The depot, the distribution centre and the proceeds not reinvested.

Back to Tarnmoor Estates. On the first of September in Group Year Three, Tarnmoor Engineering bought a new distribution centre from a developer for five point two million pounds, four months after the depot sale and well inside the window. It took the building straight into use for its trade.

Two companies are involved. Tarnmoor Estates made the gain and Tarnmoor Engineering bought the new asset. Roll-over works across a group because the law treats the trades of group members as a single trade, and lets a landlord company that does not trade claim where its asset was used only for group trades. Both companies sign the claim. Chapter seventeen sets out those group conditions. Here we do the arithmetic, which is the same as for a single company.

The proceeds were six million pounds. Five point two million pounds was reinvested, so eight hundred thousand pounds was not. That is less than the gain of two million, two hundred and seventy seven thousand, five hundred pounds, so eight hundred thousand pounds is chargeable now and one million, four hundred and seventy seven thousand, five hundred pounds is rolled over. Tarnmoor Engineering's base cost for the distribution centre becomes five point two million pounds less the rolled-over gain, three million, seven hundred and twenty two thousand, five hundred pounds.

Look at the tax. Corporation tax on eight hundred thousand pounds is two hundred thousand pounds, against five hundred and sixty nine thousand, three hundred and seventy five pounds with no relief. Chapter seventeen moves part of the eight hundred thousand pounds again, to Tarnmoor Engineering, where a capital loss is waiting.

Now test the rule. Suppose, as a variation outside the story, Tarnmoor Engineering had spent only four million pounds. Two million pounds of proceeds would be unreinvested, so two million pounds would be chargeable and only two hundred and seventy seven thousand, five hundred pounds rolled over. Had it spent three and a half million pounds, the two and a half million pounds not reinvested would exceed the whole gain, and there would be no relief at all.

Three consequences follow, each a favourite of the examiner. First, the rolled-over gain is not lost. When Tarnmoor Engineering one day sells the centre, its gain is measured from three million, seven hundred and twenty two thousand, five hundred pounds. Second, the structures and buildings allowance that Tarnmoor Engineering claims on the building, one hundred and two thousand pounds a year, is added to its sale proceeds on that later disposal, as chapter nine explained. Third, in the group accounts the deferral does not remove deferred tax. As chapter six explained, roll-over postpones tax and does not cancel it.

Depreciating assets and holdover.

Roll-over into a new asset that wears out would let a gain disappear as the asset is depreciated and sold for little. So where the new asset is a depreciating asset, the law does not reduce its cost. Instead the gain is held over, frozen outside the computation, and comes back into charge on the earliest of three dates. When the new asset is sold. When it stops being used for the trade. Or ten years after it was acquired.

A depreciating asset is a wasting asset, or one that will become a wasting asset within ten years, which means an asset with an expected life of sixty years or less. Fixed plant and machinery is always a depreciating asset. So is a lease with sixty years or less to run. A freehold is not. Notice the gap between the two tests. A lease with fifty five years to run is not yet a wasting asset, so its own cost is not written down, but it will become one within ten years, so a gain reinvested in it is held over, not rolled over. Count the years at the date of acquisition.

The held-over gain is the gain on the old asset, computed in the usual way, and when it comes back it is charged in the accounting period of the triggering event, at the rate for that period. The examiners set exactly that in November twenty twenty four, in a question where a gain held over years earlier came back into charge. The new asset's own gain or loss on its sale is computed separately, on its full cost.

There is an escape route. If, before the held-over gain crystallises, the company buys a non-depreciating qualifying asset, it can switch the held-over gain into a full roll-over against that asset.

Suppose, outside the story, the depot proceeds had gone into fixed plant for a new production line, costing six million pounds. Nothing would reduce the plant's cost, so the capital allowances would be computed on the full price. The whole gain of two million, two hundred and seventy seven thousand, five hundred pounds would be held over, and would come back no later than ten years after the plant was bought, unless a freehold was bought in time to take it over.

Holdover is a relief to recognise, not to reach for by reflex. The examiners reported after May twenty twenty six that candidates spent time on holdover in a question that asked only about roll-over, and earned nothing for it.

Land that becomes stock.

A property company can change its mind. Land bought as an investment may later be developed and sold, and land built as stock may later be kept and let. The tax system has to decide where the investment gain ends and the trading profit begins. Section one hundred and sixty one of the Gains Act draws the line at the moment of appropriation.

Where a company appropriates a capital asset to trading stock, it is treated as selling the asset at market value at that moment. Any gain up to then is a chargeable gain, and the stock enters the trade at market value. Alternatively, the company can elect, within two years of the end of the accounting period, to have no chargeable gain or loss and instead to bring the asset into stock at market value less the gain, or plus the loss. Then the whole profit, including the earlier growth in value, is taxed later as trading profit.

Which is better? Suppose, outside the story, a company holds a field bought for four hundred thousand pounds after twenty seventeen, and decides to build and sell houses on it when it is worth one million pounds. Without the election, it has a gain of six hundred thousand pounds now, and the stock enters at one million. That is attractive if the company has capital losses to cover the gain, because capital losses can never be used against trading profit. With the election, there is no gain now, the stock enters at four hundred thousand pounds, and the six hundred thousand pounds is taxed as trading profit when the houses are sold. That is attractive if the company has trading losses, or wants to defer the tax until the sale. The rate is twenty five per cent either way, so the choice turns on losses and timing.

The movement can also run the other way. When a developer appropriates a building from stock to keep as an investment, the trade is treated as selling it at market value, and for gains purposes the company acquires it at the value brought into the trading accounts. The examiners built a twenty-mark question around exactly that in May twenty twenty three, and reported that many candidates missed the market value appropriation.

Inside a group, the transfer itself can do the appropriating. Where a group company acquires as trading stock an asset another member held as a capital asset, it is treated as appropriating it on arrival. Chapter seventeen covers that rule.

Transactions in United Kingdom land.

Some companies prefer their land profits to look like capital, or to arise somewhere outside the United Kingdom charge. Until twenty sixteen, offshore developers could build in the United Kingdom through companies resident abroad, claim that they had no permanent establishment here, and pay no United Kingdom tax on their development profits. Finance Act twenty sixteen closed that door with Part eight Z B of the twenty ten Act, in force for disposals on or after the fifth of July, twenty sixteen. Arrangements made between the Budget on the sixteenth of March and that date were caught by an anti-forestalling rule.

The rule works by recharacterisation. A profit or gain on a disposal of United Kingdom land is treated as a trading profit of the company that realises it if any one of four conditions is met. Condition A, a main purpose of acquiring the land was to realise a profit or gain from disposing of it. Condition B, the same purpose, for property deriving its value from the land. Condition C, the land is held as trading stock. Condition D, where the land is developed, a main purpose of developing it was to realise a profit or gain from disposing of it once developed. The whole profit, including what would otherwise be a capital gain, becomes a trading profit. A non-resident company caught by the rule is treated as carrying on a trade of dealing in or developing United Kingdom land, and is charged to corporation tax whether or not it has a permanent establishment here.

Three refinements matter. Indirect disposals are caught too. Where a company disposes of property, such as shares, that derives at least fifty per cent of its value from United Kingdom land, and it is party to an arrangement whose main purpose is to deal in or develop the land, the profit can be taxed as trading profit. Under Condition D, the part of the gain fairly attributable to the period before the intention to develop was formed is carved out, and stays a capital gain. And the rule does not apply where the profit is already taxed as income.

Apply that to the field. If the company bought it as a long-term investment, and only later decided to develop, Condition D catches the development profit but not the growth in value before the decision. Section one hundred and sixty one reaches the same dividing line from the other side. Had it bought the field intending to obtain planning consent and sell, Condition A would make the whole profit trading from day one, whatever its accounts called the land.

For Tarnmoor Estates, which buys and holds sites to let to the group, the rule is a reminder to document intention. Board papers that say a site is held for the group's own use, and valuations at the time of any change of plan, are the evidence that protects capital treatment, and the pre-intention carve-out, if H M R C later asks.

Planning, and the examiner's view.

Pull the threads together and a property tax plan for a large group looks like this. Time the reinvestment, because roll-over turns on a three-year window, measured from contract dates. Reinvest the whole proceeds if relief matters, because the cost comes back first. Choose between assigning and granting a lease, and between rent and premium, knowing that a short grant brings an income charge for the landlord and a slow deduction for a trading tenant. Decide on any appropriation to stock while losses are available, and make the election within two years if it helps. Get valuations of retained interests and reversions checked through form C G thirty four before the return is filed. And remember that every land transaction has a stamp duty land tax dimension, which chapter twenty one covers.

The tax function turns that plan into a calendar. Forecasts for instalment payments include expected gains, and they move when a completion date moves, as Tarnmoor Estates learned in Group Year Two. Every disposal of land gets a memo recording the contract date, the cost and indexation, any relief claimed and the claim deadline. Provisional roll-over claims go on a list with their lapse dates, the fourth anniversary of the end of the period of disposal. Appropriation elections are diarised for two years after the period end, and roll-over claims for four. And the post-transaction valuation check goes in at least three months before the return is due. None of this is exotic. It is the routine that stops a good result on a sale turning into an enquiry two years later.

For the paper, these topics are among the most frequently examined. A question built on property gains, leases and roll-over has appeared five times in four sittings between May twenty twenty three and May twenty twenty six, at fifteen or twenty marks, usually alongside capital allowances, fixtures elections, no gain, no loss transfers and stamp duty land tax. The examiners' complaints repeat. Chargeable amounts computed from the gain instead of the proceeds not reinvested. Assignments treated as grants. Short-lease status tested at the wrong date. Indexation used to increase a loss. Market value used where a group transfer carried cost. Holdover discussed when only roll-over was asked. The requirements say calculate, with explanations. Lay out each disposal in the conventional order, proceeds, cost, indexation, relief, chargeable gain, and keep pounds and thousands of pounds apart. From twenty twenty eight, gains on leases leave the syllabus, but your sitting is under the current grid, where they are core.

What to take away.

A company's chargeable gains are part of its total profits, taxed at its ordinary rate, twenty five per cent for a large company in the financial year twenty twenty six. Capital losses are set against gains of the same period, then carried forward against future gains only, within the loss restriction. They never touch income.

The disposal happens when the contract is made, or when a condition is satisfied. Market value replaces the price for gifts, bargains not at arm's length and every disposal to a connected person, and a loss on a disposal to a connected person can be used only against gains on disposals to that same person.

Allowable costs are the acquisition cost, enhancement expenditure still reflected in the asset when it is sold, and the costs of disposal. Indexation runs only to December twenty seventeen, and it can never create or increase a loss. Tarnmoor Estates' depot, bought for two and a half million pounds in June two thousand and four with an indexation factor of nought point four eight nine, and sold for six million pounds, gave a gain of two million, two hundred and seventy seven thousand, five hundred pounds.

A part disposal takes cost in the fraction A over A plus B, and B is a valuation. Plant that qualified for capital allowances gives no allowable loss, and a gain only if sold above cost.

A lease is a wasting asset once fifty years or less remain, and it melts along the table. Fifty years scores one hundred, twenty five years eighty one point one. Status is tested at the date of the transaction. An assignment has no income element. The grant of a lease of fifty years or less taxes the premium times fifty minus the years less one, over fifty, as property income. On Tarnmoor Estates' thirty-year grant, that was eight hundred and forty thousand pounds of income and a gain of eight hundred and seventy thousand pounds, using the capital part over the full premium plus the reversion. Its lease assignment gave a gain of one hundred and eighty nine thousand pounds after indexation.

Roll-over relief needs trade use, qualifying classes, the window from twelve months before to three years after, and a claim. It taxes the proceeds not reinvested, never more than the gain. The depot gave eight hundred thousand pounds chargeable, one million, four hundred and seventy seven thousand, five hundred pounds rolled over, and a base cost for the distribution centre of three million, seven hundred and twenty two thousand, five hundred pounds. Into a depreciating asset, the gain is held over for up to ten years instead.

Appropriation to stock is a deemed sale at market value unless the company elects within two years to fold the gain into stock. And Part eight Z B of the twenty ten Act turns profits on United Kingdom land acquired or developed with a main purpose of selling at a profit into trading profits, for residents and non-residents alike.

So the depot's six million pounds cost Tarnmoor Estates tax on eight hundred thousand pounds, not on three and a half million, because the law deducted what the depot had cost in real terms to December twenty seventeen, and because the group put most of the money back into a building it used for its trade.

Chapter seventeen: Gains inside the group.

On the twenty third of July, two thousand and eight, the Court of Appeal gave judgment in Johnston Publishing North Limited versus H M R C. The taxpayer had begun life as a company bought off the shelf, and it had joined a large group. An asset had come to it from another company in that group, free of tax, as assets moving inside a group usually do. Later the two companies left the group together. H M R C said that leaving triggered a charge on the asset's gain, and its provisional figure for that gain, as reported at the time, was two hundred and eighty million pounds.

The law then had an exception for associated companies leaving together. The two companies were associated when they left. But they had not been associated when the asset moved between them. The court decided for H M R C. Both moments mattered. In the words of Lord Justice Tuckey, one must start by trying to give meaning to all the words used.

Three years later Parliament rewrote that exception. In the same Finance Act it changed what the charge itself does for most groups, and turned what had been a tax bill into something that is usually exempt. This chapter explains how that happened, and the larger question behind it. When the companies in a group pass assets, gains and losses among themselves, when does the law look through the walls between them, and what happens when a company walks out of the house?

This is the third chapter of Part Three. Every topic in it is core on the paper. Groups and transactions within groups, depreciatory transactions, anti-gain buying, companies leaving groups, recovery of tax from other group companies and value shifting all carry the top grade. It builds on The Living Law, chapter twenty four, which introduced gains groups, the reallocation election, group roll-over, pre-entry losses and the degrouping charge. Here we take each of them to Advanced Technical depth, and add the anti-avoidance rules that chapter only signposted.

Nearly all the law is in the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act nineteen ninety two, which I will call the Gains Act. Practitioners say its group sections digit by digit, and so will I. Section one seven oh defines the group, section one seven one moves assets at no gain, no loss, section one seven one A moves gains and losses, and section one seven nine is the degrouping charge. We apply the law for the financial year twenty twenty six. Finance Act twenty twenty six made no change to these rules.

Why a group needs its own gains rules.

The Living Law, chapter twenty four, pictured a group as a family of separate wallets living under one roof. For gains, a slightly different picture helps. Think of a family that owns several houses and moves furniture between them as its needs change. Nobody pays rent, and nobody sells anything to anybody. The furniture simply goes where it is useful.

Tax law starts from the opposite place. Each company is a separate person. When one company hands a building to its sister, that is a disposal. The two are connected, so the market value rule would normally apply, and the transferor would be taxed on a gain it never turned into cash. A group that wanted to put its property into one company, or move a factory to the subsidiary that uses it, would pay tax for rearranging its own furniture.

So the Gains Act lets assets move inside a group at no gain, no loss. The relief is automatic, and it is generous. And it creates an obvious game. If a building can move into a subsidiary without tax, why sell the building at all? Move it into a new subsidiary, then sell the subsidiary's shares. The buyer gets the building, the group gets the cash, and the gain on the building is never taxed as such.

H M R C's own manual describes that game. It says the arrangement was commonly called enveloping, or the envelope trick, and that it was used extensively after the Finance Act nineteen sixty five brought companies' gains into tax. The degrouping charge in section one seven nine is the answer. If a company leaves the group within six years of receiving an asset at no gain, no loss, it is treated as having sold and bought back the asset at its market value on the day it received it.

The rest of the group gains code follows the same pattern. Parliament gives a relief because the group is one business, then adds a rule to stop the relief being used as a tool. The reallocation election lets a group match its gains and losses without moving anything. Group roll-over treats all the trades as one. Against those reliefs stand the pre-entry loss rules and the anti-buying rules, which stop groups buying companies for their losses or gains. The depreciatory transaction and value shifting rules stop groups draining value out of a company before they sell it. And section one nine oh lets H M R C collect unpaid tax from other members.

Keep the family picture in mind. The family discount is real. But when a house leaves the family, it may have to settle up for the furniture it took with it.

Who belongs to the gains group.

Section one seven oh builds the group from the top down. There is a principal company. Its seventy five per cent subsidiaries are members. So are their seventy five per cent subsidiaries, and theirs, down the chain. Seventy five per cent here means beneficial ownership of three quarters of the ordinary share capital, directly or through other companies.

Then comes a second test, which is easy to forget. Every member must also be an effective fifty one per cent subsidiary of the principal company. That means the principal company must be entitled to more than half of the subsidiary's profits available for distribution, and more than half of its assets on a winding up. The test looks at the economic interest of the principal company, multiplied down the chain.

Take a simple chain. A parent owns seventy five per cent of a subsidiary, which owns seventy five per cent of a sub-subsidiary. The parent's effective interest in the bottom company is fifty six point two five per cent. That is more than half, so the bottom company is in. Add a third tier, also at seventy five per cent, and the effective interest falls to about forty two point two per cent. The third tier is out, even though every link in the chain is a seventy five per cent link.

A company can belong to only one gains group, and the Act has tie-breaking rules for the rare case of a company that could belong to two. A seventy five per cent subsidiary cannot be the principal company of a group of its own. And companies resident anywhere in the world can be members. Residence matters for what a member can do with an asset, as the next section shows, but not for membership.

Our invented group, Tarnmoor, shows how the list works. Tarnmoor plc, the listed parent, is the principal company. Every company it owns outright is a member. That means Tarnmoor Engineering, Tarnmoor Estates, Tarnmoor Finance, Tarnmoor Water Systems, the dormant Tarnmoor Pumps, the Vallarian and Marrovian subsidiaries in our invented countries, and Tarnmoor Ireland. Calder Valve Engineering joins on the first of April in Group Year One, when Tarnmoor buys it. Brackenwell Sensors joins on the first of July in Group Year Two. Helmside Energy, the joint venture in which Tarnmoor plc holds forty five per cent, is outside until the first of March in Group Year Five. On that day Tarnmoor plc takes its holding to eighty five per cent, and Helmside joins.

Do not confuse this list with the others in the book. Chapter one set out the guest lists. The shareholding exemption aggregates holdings across a fifty one per cent group. Recovery of unpaid tax under section one nine oh also uses a fifty one per cent group, so collection reaches further than relief. Group relief uses seventy five per cent with economic tests of its own, and only between companies within the charge to United Kingdom tax. The gains group is its own list, wider than group relief because overseas members count, and narrower than the fifty one per cent groups.

One more feature matters in practice. A company does not leave the gains group merely because it, or another member, goes into liquidation. Chapter fourteen showed that this is why a liquidating holding company can still pass assets around its group before it is dissolved.

No gain, no loss.

Section one seven one is the heart of the code. When one member of a gains group disposes of an asset to another, both are treated as if the consideration were whatever amount gives the transferor neither a gain nor a loss. The rule is automatic. There is no claim and no election. It also overrides the market value rule, so it does not matter that the two companies are connected or that the price on the transfer documents is something else.

There is a condition about the tax net. The asset must stay within the charge to United Kingdom corporation tax. In practice, each company must be United Kingdom resident, or be a non-resident company chargeable on that asset, for example because the asset is used by its United Kingdom permanent establishment or is United Kingdom land. A transfer to an overseas member that takes the asset out of the charge is not at no gain, no loss. When Calder sold its process valve plant to the Vallarian subsidiary in Group Year Three, the sale was taxed on the ordinary rules, for the same reason chapter eleven gave for the know-how that went with it.

Some disposals are excluded even inside the group. They include a transfer in satisfaction of a debt, the redemption of redeemable shares, capital distributions, disposals to or by investment trusts, venture capital trusts and real estate investment trusts, and a transfer to a dual resident investing company. Section one seven one also gives way to the share exchange rules, so an exchange of shares inside a group is dealt with under those rules, which chapter eighteen covers.

What does the transferee inherit? It inherits the transferor's cost. For an asset held before twenty eighteen, that cost includes indexation up to December twenty seventeen, carried in through the no gain, no loss rule. But H M R C's manual makes the point that indexation rolled up in this way cannot later create or increase a loss when the transferee sells.

Now the story. On the first of April in Group Year Three, Calder transfers its works to Tarnmoor Estates, the group's property company, which leases the works straight back to Calder. Calder had bought the works in March twenty nineteen for four point two million pounds. That was after December twenty seventeen, so there is no indexation. The works are worth five point six million pounds on the day they move. Tarnmoor Estates' base cost is four point two million pounds, not five point six million. One point four million pounds of gain sits unrealised inside the building. Stamp duty land tax group relief saves two hundred and sixty nine thousand, five hundred pounds, as chapter twenty one explains.

Six months later, on the first of October in Group Year Three, Tarnmoor Estates transfers the water systems factory to Tarnmoor Water Systems, which uses it. Tarnmoor Estates had bought it in Group Year One for four point six million pounds. It is now worth six million pounds. Again the transferee takes the transferor's cost, four point six million pounds, and again one point four million pounds of gain waits inside the asset.

This is where the examiners keep finding mistakes. After the November twenty twenty five sitting they reported that many candidates used market value instead of the no gain, no loss cost. After May twenty twenty three, they noted that few saw that a no gain transfer means a lower base cost later. The transferee's cost is the transferor's history, not today's value.

Trading stock on the move.

The no gain, no loss rule is built for capital assets. Trading stock is taxed as income. So what happens when an asset crosses the line between the two as it moves inside the group? Section one seven three answers by borrowing the appropriation rules in section one six one, which chapter sixteen explained for a single company.

Suppose, for this example, that Tarnmoor Estates holds a plot of land as an investment, with a cost of two million pounds and a value of three million pounds. Suppose it transfers the plot to a development subsidiary, which will build on it and sell it as trading stock. The transfer itself is at no gain, no loss, so the developer takes over the two million pound cost. But the developer is then treated as appropriating the land to trading stock at market value. Under section one six one that is a disposal at three million pounds. The developer has a chargeable gain of one million pounds.

The developer can elect instead to bring the land into its stock at its cost. Then there is no chargeable gain now. The one million pounds turns up later as trading profit, when the developer sells the finished buildings. For a company, the election must be made within two years after the end of the accounting period of the appropriation. Which is better depends on the group's position, and the choice belongs to the developer alone. A group with capital losses to use may prefer the gain. A group without them may prefer to keep everything in the trade and take the profit when the cash arrives.

The rule also runs the other way. Suppose a group developer holds a building as stock and transfers it to an investment company in the group, which will let it. The developer is treated as appropriating the building out of its stock just before the transfer. For the trading computation that appropriation is at market value, so the developer's trading profit includes the uplift, and the investment company's capital gains cost then starts from that value.

The examiners tested exactly this in May twenty twenty three, in a twenty mark property question, and reported that candidates missed the market value appropriation. When an asset changes character as it moves, look for the appropriation first, and only then apply the no gain, no loss rule.

Moving the gain, not the asset.

Before the year two thousand, a group that wanted to set a loss in one company against a gain in another had one tool. It physically transferred the asset to the loss-making company just before the sale to the outside buyer. That cost legal fees, sometimes stamp duty, and occasionally a degrouping problem. The Finance Act two thousand introduced an election to treat the asset as if it had been transferred, without the paperwork. For gains and losses accruing on or after the twenty first of July, two thousand and nine, the rule was rewritten as section one seven one A, so that a group can simply reallocate a gain or a loss from one member to another.

The conditions are short. Both companies must be members of the same gains group when the gain or loss accrues. The receiving company must be one to which the asset could have been transferred at no gain, no loss, so in practice United Kingdom resident or trading here through a permanent establishment. The two companies make a joint election within two years after the end of the accounting period of the company in which the gain or loss accrued. The election can cover all or part of the gain or loss, but never more than the whole. H M R C says it has no statutory power to extend the two-year limit.

The reallocated gain is treated as accruing to the receiving company at the same time as it accrued to the original company. And any payment between the companies for the election, up to the amount of the gain or loss, is ignored for corporation tax and is not a distribution.

In our invented case, Tarnmoor Engineering realises a capital loss of five hundred thousand pounds in Group Year Three, on a portfolio of listed shares. Tarnmoor Engineering has no gains of its own that year. Without help, the loss would be carried forward, usable only against Tarnmoor Engineering's own future gains, and then only within the loss restriction that chapter fourteen explained.

Meanwhile Tarnmoor Estates has a chargeable gain of eight hundred thousand pounds from the depot it sold in April, after the group roll-over claim in the next section. Tom Hesketh, the group's head of tax, proposes the obvious match. Tarnmoor Estates and Tarnmoor Engineering jointly elect to treat five hundred thousand pounds of the gain as Tarnmoor Engineering's. The current-year loss absorbs it in full. Current-year capital losses are outside the loss restriction, which bites only on losses carried forward.

The arithmetic is simple. Without the election, Tarnmoor Estates pays tax at twenty five per cent on eight hundred thousand pounds, which is two hundred thousand pounds. With it, Tarnmoor Estates pays tax on three hundred thousand pounds, which is seventy five thousand pounds, and Tarnmoor Engineering pays nothing on the gain it has received. The group saves one hundred and twenty five thousand pounds now. Tarnmoor Estates pays Tarnmoor Engineering one hundred and twenty five thousand pounds for the loss, and because that is less than the five hundred thousand pounds reallocated, the payment is ignored for corporation tax.

The deadline is the thirty first of December in Group Year Five, two years after the end of Tarnmoor Estates' accounting period. Tom files it with the Group Year Three returns, well inside the limit. Tarnmoor Estates is left with net gains of four hundred and eighty nine thousand pounds for the year, the three hundred thousand pounds it kept plus the gain on its lease assignment from chapter sixteen.

One trade for roll-over.

Chapter sixteen taught roll-over relief for a single company. Sell a business asset, buy another within the window, and the gain can be rolled into the new asset's cost. The window runs from twelve months before the disposal to three years after it. And the part that stays taxable is the proceeds not reinvested, not some slice of the gain.

Section one seven five takes that relief into the group. For roll-over purposes, all the trades carried on by members of a gains group are treated as a single trade. A disposal by one member and an acquisition by another can be matched, as if one person had made both. Both companies must make the claim. Each must be United Kingdom resident, or the asset must be used in a United Kingdom permanent establishment's trade.

Two details make the rule work in real groups. The first is for property companies. Many groups hold their land in a company that does not trade. It lets the buildings to the trading companies. Section one seven five lets a non-trading member claim, as long as the assets it disposes of or acquires are used only for the trades of other group members. The second detail is a limit. Roll-over is not available where the new asset was acquired from another group member at no gain, no loss. Otherwise a group could create a reinvestment simply by moving an asset it already owned. A dual resident investing company is also kept out.

Here is the Tarnmoor story. Tarnmoor Estates, which does not trade, has owned a depot since June two thousand and four, when it paid two point five million pounds. In our invented case it has let the depot to Tarnmoor Engineering throughout, and Tarnmoor Engineering has used it only for its trade. On the thirtieth of April in Group Year Three, Tarnmoor Estates sells the depot for six million pounds. Indexation up to December twenty seventeen adds one point two two two five million pounds to the cost. The gain is two million, two hundred and seventy seven thousand, five hundred pounds. Chapter sixteen does that computation in full.

Four months later, on the first of September, Tarnmoor Engineering buys a new distribution centre from a developer for five point two million pounds, for its own trade. A different company, but the same single trade in the eyes of section one seven five, and well inside the window. Tarnmoor Estates and Tarnmoor Engineering claim together.

Six million pounds came in. Five point two million pounds went out on a qualifying asset. The proceeds not reinvested are eight hundred thousand pounds, and that is the amount that stays chargeable in Tarnmoor Estates. The rest of the gain, one million, four hundred and seventy seven thousand, five hundred pounds, is rolled over. It reduces Tarnmoor Engineering's base cost in the distribution centre, from five point two million pounds to three million, seven hundred and twenty two thousand, five hundred pounds. The gain has not gone away. It has moved house, into Tarnmoor Engineering's building, and will be taxed when that building is sold, unless it is rolled again.

Notice what the group's structure did. The depot was a landlord's asset, but because it was used only for a group trade, the landlord could claim. Had Tarnmoor Estates let the depot to an outside tenant, or to a fifty per cent joint venture, there would have been no group trade to roll into. That was the trap in November twenty twenty four, when the examiners reported that candidates wrongly deferred a gain on property leased outside the group against reinvestment elsewhere in the group. They also taxed the gain rather than the proceeds not reinvested.

The two claims then fit together with the reallocation election from the last section. Roll-over is applied first, and leaves a chargeable gain of eight hundred thousand pounds in Tarnmoor Estates. Five hundred thousand pounds of that is then reallocated to Tarnmoor Engineering to meet its loss.

Losses that arrive with a new member.

Capital losses are valuable to a group with gains. So for as long as groups have been able to reallocate gains, there has been a market in companies whose main asset is an unused capital loss. Schedule seven A to the Gains Act stops a group buying that kind of relief.

Since the Finance Act twenty eleven the rule has been simple. A pre-entry loss is an allowable loss that accrued to a company before it joined the group. Before twenty eleven the definition also reached part of any later loss on an asset the company already held when it joined, worked out by time apportionment or by market value. Parliament cut all that away. Now only losses actually realised before entry are ring-fenced.

A pre-entry loss can still be used, but only against three kinds of gain. First, gains on disposals the company made before it joined. Second, gains on assets the company already held when it joined. Third, gains on assets it acquires afterwards from outside the group, if it uses them in a trade it carried on before joining and has carried on ever since. The Schedule also sets the order in which pre-entry and other losses are used, and fits that order into the corporate loss restriction.

Brackenwell Sensors is the example. Before Tarnmoor bought it, Brackenwell realised a capital loss of four hundred thousand pounds on its shares in a failed spin-out. When it joined the group on the first of July in Group Year Two, that loss became a pre-entry loss.

Run through the three permitted uses. Brackenwell made no gains between the first of January and the thirtieth of June in Group Year Two, before it joined. It held no chargeable asset likely to produce a gain. Its patents and know-how fall under the intangibles code, not the Gains Act, and its laboratory equipment is plant on which it claims capital allowances. So the loss will be used only if Brackenwell one day buys a gains asset from outside the group, a building for example, uses it in its sensor trade, and sells it at a gain.

What the group cannot do is feed the loss. It cannot move a gain-bearing asset into Brackenwell at no gain, no loss and sell it there, because that asset came from inside the group after entry. Nor, on this book's reading of the reallocation rules, can it reallocate another member's gain to Brackenwell. The reallocation works by assuming the asset passed to Brackenwell from inside the group just before the sale, so the gain is not on an asset Brackenwell held at entry. If the loss is ever used, it also counts against the deductions allowance, and the group has allocated the whole allowance to Tarnmoor Engineering, so only half of any qualifying gain could be relieved.

Tom Hesketh valued the loss at nil in the price, and the group accounts recognise no deferred tax asset for it. The pattern matches chapter fourteen's. There, Chapters two B and two D of Part fourteen stop a company's pre-change trading losses sheltering gains on assets moved into it after a change in ownership. Here, Schedule seven A does the same job for capital losses.

Buying gains and buying losses.

Schedule seven A is mechanical. It asks one question, when did the loss accrue, and ignores motive. For the cases where a deal is really about using losses, the Gains Act has sharper tools. Sections one eight four A to one eight four I were inserted by the Finance Act two thousand and six. They replaced an older Schedule that ring-fenced pre-entry gains, and they turn on purpose.

Both main rules start from a qualifying change of ownership. That means a company joining a group, leaving one, or coming under someone's control. There are exceptions for a new holding company inserted over an existing group with the same shareholders, and for moving a seventy five per cent subsidiary around inside a group.

Section one eight four A is the loss-buying rule. If a company with losses, or with assets standing at a loss, changes hands in connection with arrangements whose main purpose, or one of whose main purposes, is to secure a tax advantage by using those losses, the losses cannot be deducted.

Section one eight four B is the mirror, the gain-buying rule. Suppose a group with spare capital losses buys a company that owns an asset standing at a large gain, precisely so that the gain can be realised inside the buying group and covered by those losses. If the change of ownership is connected with arrangements having that main purpose, no loss can be deducted from the gain, other than the company's own losses from before the change. H M R C's manual gives an example in which a seller sells a subsidiary to a buyer with surplus capital losses, the buyer then buys the subsidiary's business, and the buyer hopes its losses will absorb the gain. H M R C's view is that section one eight four B stops the gain being sheltered.

Where both regimes could apply, H M R C's manual says the anti-avoidance rule takes priority, and Schedule seven A applies to ordinary mergers and acquisitions with no such purpose. That fits Brackenwell. Tarnmoor bought it for its sensors, not its losses, so its four hundred thousand pound loss falls under Schedule seven A and not under section one eight four A.

A third targeted rule, in sections one eight four G and one eight four H, is aimed at schemes that convert income into capital gains so that capital losses can cover them, or that secure income deductions alongside the use of capital losses. It works by H M R C giving the company a notice.

These rules belong to the same family as the purpose tests in chapters four, twelve and fourteen. The law no longer tries to list every way a loss might be bought. It asks why the deal was done. That is why Tarnmoor's board minutes on each acquisition record the commercial reasons, and why Tom Hesketh's due diligence asks a buyer's question about every target. Which of its assets carry gains, and which of its losses could we use, and would anyone think that was why we bought it?

Depreciatory transactions and dividend stripping.

Now to the rules that protect the value of shares. A group can make a subsidiary's shares worth less without selling anything. It can move the subsidiary's best asset to a sister company at a low price. It can cancel share capital. Then it sells the shares at a loss and sets that loss against real gains elsewhere. Nothing has left the group, yet a capital loss has appeared.

Section one seven six of the Gains Act deals with that. It applies when a company disposes of shares or securities in another company, and their value has been materially reduced by a depreciatory transaction. A depreciatory transaction includes a disposal of assets between members of a group at other than market value, and any other transaction to which the company, or its seventy five per cent subsidiary, and other group members were parties, including a cancellation of share capital. Only transactions since the thirty first of March, nineteen eighty two count. A negligible value claim counts as a disposal.

Where it applies, any loss on the shares is reduced to the extent that is just and reasonable. Two features matter. The first is that there is no motive test. It does not matter why the transaction happened. The second is that it touches only losses. A gain on the shares is never increased. And if the loss is reduced, a later gain on shares in another company that was party to the transaction, made within six years, can be reduced by up to the amount of the loss disallowed.

Suppose, for this example, that a group subsidiary whose shares cost ten million pounds sells a building worth five million pounds to its sister company for two million pounds. The transfer is at no gain, no loss for gains purposes, but three million pounds of value has left the subsidiary. The parent later sells the subsidiary for seven million pounds, a loss of three million pounds. Section one seven six can reduce that loss by up to three million pounds, here to nil, whatever the reason for the transfer. If the parent then sells the sister's shares at a gain within six years, that gain can be reduced by up to three million pounds.

Section one seven seven extends the idea to dividend stripping. Where a company holding at least ten per cent of a class of shares in another company, and not dealing in them, receives a distribution that materially reduces the value of its holding, section one seven six applies as if the distribution were a depreciatory transaction. That works even if the two companies are not in a group.

In a modern group these rules matter less often than they once did, because many share disposals fall within the substantial shareholding exemption, under which a loss is not allowable at all. They matter most where the exemption does not apply. That means sales of investment companies, holdings that fail the twelve-month test, and companies that are not trading.

Value shifting.

Depreciatory transactions catch losses. Value shifting catches the other half of the game, where value is drained out of a company so that a gain on its shares is smaller than it should be.

Section two nine is the old general rule. If a person with control of a company uses it so that value passes out of shares owned by that person, or someone connected, into other shares, that is a disposal at market value. Section three oh deals with schemes that reduce an asset's value while handing someone a tax-free benefit. But for corporation tax, section three oh does not apply to a company's disposal of shares or securities in another company. Section three one applies instead.

Section three one was put in by the Finance Act twenty eleven, replacing four older sections that were notoriously complex. It applies when a company disposes of shares or securities, and arrangements have materially reduced the value of those shares, or of an asset owned by a group member at the time of the disposal. The arrangements must have as a main purpose obtaining a tax advantage, meaning avoiding corporation tax on chargeable gains, for anyone. And they must not consist solely of making an exempt distribution. H M R C's manual explains that the exempt distribution limb points to the exempt classes in the Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, which I will call the two thousand and nine Act, and that the test works the same way for every company, whatever its size.

Where it applies, the consideration for the shares is increased by a just and reasonable amount. That amount takes account of the whole arrangements, including any tax charge or relief they trigger. H M R C's manual gives an example. If arrangements reduce the consideration for a share sale by two hundred, but also trigger a degrouping gain of fifty, an adjustment of one hundred and fifty may be appropriate.

The classic target is a drain-out. Suppose, for this example, that a group owns a property investment subsidiary whose shares cost twenty million pounds and are worth fifty million pounds. Because it does not trade, the shareholding exemption will not apply to a sale. Before selling, the group moves one of the subsidiary's properties to a sister company at a value that creates twelve million pounds of distributable reserves. The subsidiary pays a twelve million pound dividend up to its parent, and the parent sells the subsidiary for thirty eight million pounds. The gain is eighteen million pounds instead of thirty million. The arrangements are not solely a dividend. They include the property transfer that made the dividend possible, and their main purpose is plain. Section three one can add a just and reasonable amount, up to twelve million pounds, to the consideration, putting back up to three million pounds of tax at twenty five per cent.

Compare an ordinary dividend paid out of profits the subsidiary had genuinely earned. That is the carve-out. Arrangements consisting solely of an exempt distribution are outside section three one, however much they reduce the value of the shares.

That contrast was tested in November twenty twenty four, when candidates confused the two, and the examiners reported that depreciatory transactions do not consider motive and only apply to losses, while value shifting is motive based. They also noted that candidates missed that value shifting does not apply where the reduction comes solely from an exempt distribution.

Tarnmoor meets the rule in Group Year Six. In December, Tarnmoor Actuators pays Tarnmoor Engineering a dividend of two million pounds out of profits it has made since the hive-down, before Tarnmoor Engineering sells it at the end of the month. The dividend is paid out of genuine profits and is not part of a wider value strip, so it is an exempt distribution and nothing more. And because the share sale falls within the substantial shareholding exemption, there is no gain tax for any arrangement to avoid. Section one seven six does not matter either, because there is no loss to reduce.

The degrouping charge.

Now back to the envelope trick, and the rule that stops it. Section one seven nine applies when a company, call it the transferee, acquired an asset from another member of the group at no gain, no loss, and then leaves the group within six years of that acquisition. It applies if, when it leaves, the transferee still owns the asset otherwise than as trading stock, or an associated company leaving with it owns it.

The charge is a deemed sale and reacquisition. The transferee is treated as having sold the asset and bought it back at its market value immediately after it acquired it. Notice the date. The value is taken at the time of the intra-group transfer, not the time of leaving. The degrouping charge recovers the gain that the no gain, no loss transfer deferred, and no more. Growth in value after the transfer belongs to whoever owns the company then.

Some departures are not leaving at all. A company does not leave the group just because another member ceases to exist. If the whole group is taken over, so that the principal company becomes a member of another group, there is no charge then. But the exposure continues. A charge arises if, within the six years, the transferee stops being a subsidiary of the new group in the required way.

Then comes the exception that Johnston Publishing was about, for associated companies leaving together. Suppose the asset passed from one company to another, and the two leave the group together, still associated. Then nothing has escaped the group that granted the relief, so there is no charge. Associated means one is a seventy five per cent subsidiary of the other, or both are seventy five per cent subsidiaries of a third company. After Johnston Publishing, the Finance Act twenty eleven rewrote the exception as two conditions. Condition A is met where both companies have been seventy five per cent and effective fifty one per cent subsidiaries of another company from the date of the acquisition until immediately after they leave. Condition B is met where one has been such a subsidiary of the other for that whole period. The statute now looks expressly back to the date of the transfer, which is the point the court decided in two thousand and eight. There are anti-avoidance rules for a pair that later separates inside the buyer's group.

Suppose, for this example, that Tarnmoor had sold Tarnmoor Engineering itself in Group Year Six, with Tarnmoor Actuators still underneath it. Tarnmoor Actuators received the actuators factory from Tarnmoor Engineering on the first of February and was Tarnmoor Engineering's subsidiary from that day until after the sale. Condition B is met, and no charge arises on that sale.

Finally, the case where a company leaves without any share sale by the group. A subsidiary might leave because it issues new shares to an outsider, diluting the group below seventy five per cent. Then the degrouping gain accrues to the leaving company itself, at the later of the start of its accounting period in which it leaves and the time of the deemed reacquisition.

Since twenty eleven, the bill moves into the sale price.

For most groups the bigger change in twenty eleven was a different one. Before it, a degrouping gain always accrued to the leaving company. A buyer of shares therefore inherited a tax bill hidden inside the target, and the price had to allow for it. Since the Finance Act twenty eleven, for companies leaving on or after the nineteenth of July, two thousand and eleven, the rule depends on why the company leaves.

If it leaves because a group member disposes of shares, in the leaving company or in a company above it, the degrouping gain does not accrue to the leaving company at all. Instead it is added to the consideration for the seller's share disposal. A degrouping loss is deducted from that consideration. Where several disposals cause the departure, the companies can elect how to allocate the adjustment.

The result is that if the seller's share disposal falls within the substantial shareholding exemption, the degrouping gain is exempt with it. H M R C's manual says the exemption then applies to the whole gain, including the degrouping element. And the leaving company still keeps the benefit of the deemed reacquisition. Its base cost in the asset becomes the market value at the date of the original transfer.

This is the chapter's misconception. People assume a degrouping charge is always a tax cost. Since twenty eleven, for a trading subsidiary sold within the shareholding exemption, it usually costs nothing at all, and it gives the buyer a higher base cost. The family picture needs one change. When a house leaves the family, the bill for the furniture is now folded into the house's sale price, and if that sale is tax free, so is the bill.

Tarnmoor's sale of Tarnmoor Actuators shows it. On the first of February in Group Year Six, Tarnmoor Engineering hives down its actuators business into the new company. The factory moves at no gain, no loss. Tarnmoor Engineering had bought it after twenty seventeen for five million pounds, so Tarnmoor Actuators takes a cost of five million pounds. The factory is worth seven point five million pounds on the day it moves. On the thirty first of December, eleven months later, Tarnmoor Engineering sells Tarnmoor Actuators to an outside buyer. It is inside six years and still holds the factory, so the degrouping gain is two point five million pounds.

Because Tarnmoor Actuators leaves on a share sale by Tarnmoor Engineering, the two point five million pounds is added to Tarnmoor Engineering's proceeds for the shares. Chapter eighteen explains why that sale is within the shareholding exemption, using the hive-down rule, and chapter twenty works through the sale itself. The degrouping gain is exempt with the rest. Had the exemption failed, the two point five million pounds would have added six hundred and twenty five thousand pounds of tax to the share gain. And Tarnmoor Actuators' base cost in the factory is now seven point five million pounds, which the buyer will value.

Compare the intangibles code in chapter eleven. There the degrouping charge stays on the leaving company, and an exempt share sale switches it off altogether. That means no tax, but also no uplift. The gains rule reaches a similar result by a different road, and gives the buyer an uplift.

Demergers have an exception of their own. When Tarnmoor Water Systems leaves the group on the first of July in Group Year Five, it is distributed to Tarnmoor plc's shareholders in an exempt demerger, which chapter nineteen explains. That departure is within six years of the factory transfer, but section one nine two of the Gains Act says the degrouping charge does not apply to a company leaving only because of an exempt demerger distribution. A chargeable payment within five years of the demerger would bring the charge back. So the one point four million pounds of deferred gain simply travels with Tarnmoor Water Systems in the factory's four point six million pound cost. Stamp duty land tax is less forgiving, as chapter twenty one shows. The relief on that transfer is clawed back.

Collecting the tax from the rest of the group.

A group can move an asset into one company, let that company realise the gain, and then leave it with no money to pay the tax. Section one nine oh of the Gains Act gives H M R C a way round that.

If corporation tax on a chargeable gain is still unpaid six months after it became payable, H M R C can serve a notice on other companies, requiring them to pay. The notice can go to the company that was the principal company of the group when the gain accrued. It can go to any company in the group that owned the asset, or part of it, in the twelve months before the gain. And where the taxpayer company is not United Kingdom resident, it can go to a controlling director. The notice must be served within three years of the date the tax was finally determined. The company served must pay within thirty days. It pays the lower of the unpaid tax and the tax on that gain. It can recover the amount from the taxpayer company, and gets no deduction for it. The intangibles code has a parallel power, which chapter eleven mentioned, so a group's unpaid tax on intangibles can be collected in the same way.

The group for this purpose is a fifty one per cent group, not the seventy five per cent gains group. So collection reaches further than relief. A company owned sixty per cent can never receive an asset at no gain, no loss, but it can be made to pay a fellow member's tax.

Suppose, for this example, that Tarnmoor Estates had failed to pay the tax on the three hundred thousand pounds of depot gain it kept, seventy five thousand pounds. Six months after the due date, H M R C could serve notice on Tarnmoor plc, the principal company when the gain accrued. Tarnmoor plc would pay within thirty days and recover the money from Tarnmoor Estates. In a solvent listed group this never happens. Where it does matter is on the sale of a subsidiary, where a buyer will ask for protection against the seller group's unpaid gains tax, and the seller will ask for the same in reverse.

Running the gains group, and how it is examined.

For a group tax function, the rules in this chapter turn into a short list of standing tasks.

First, keep a degrouping register. Every no gain, no loss transfer goes on it, with the date, the transferee, the transferor's cost and the market value on the day. That value is what a degrouping charge will use, possibly five years later, when nobody remembers it. Tarnmoor's register for Group Year Three already has two lines, Calder's works and the water systems factory, each carrying one point four million pounds of deferred gain, each exposed for six years.

Second, prefer the election to the lorry. If the aim is only to match a gain with a loss, section one seven one A does it without moving anything. That means no conveyancing, no stamp duty land tax to relieve, and no new degrouping exposure. Physical transfers are for when the asset really needs to be somewhere else.

Third, watch the six years. A transfer made more than six years before a sale never degroups. A transfer made inside six years usually ends in the seller's exempt proceeds, but only if the share sale qualifies for the shareholding exemption. Check that first.

Fourth, document purpose and sources. Before any sale, record where each pre-sale dividend comes from. A dividend of genuine profits is outside value shifting. A dividend made possible by a pre-sale reshuffle may not be. On every acquisition, minute the commercial reasons, so that the anti-buying rules have nothing to find.

Fifth, read the target's history. Does it hold assets it received intra-group within six years? Do they degroup on completion? Does it bring capital losses that Schedule seven A will ring-fence? Is anyone expecting section one nine oh to reach the buyer's group? The answers go into the price, the warranties and the tax deed, as chapter twenty explains.

There is an ethical thread here too. Most of these rules now turn on purpose. Tarnmoor's published tax strategy says the group does not use marketed tax avoidance schemes, and a gains plan that only works if nobody asks why it was done is the kind of plan that strategy rules out.

On the paper, gains inside groups appear in property questions and in sale of subsidiary questions, rarely on their own. In May twenty twenty four, a fifteen mark question on selling a subsidiary turned on an intra-group transfer, the degrouping charge added to proceeds, and the shareholding exemption. The examiners reported it was well answered. In November twenty twenty four, a fifteen mark question mixed depreciatory transactions, value shifting and pre-sale dividends, and candidates confused the two regimes. Group roll-over and holdover took fifteen marks in November twenty twenty four, and a twenty mark group property question in November twenty twenty five turned on the no gain, no loss cost.

The traps are consistent. Using market value instead of the transferor's cost. Measuring the degrouping gain at the date of leaving instead of the date of the transfer. Charging the degrouping gain on the leaving company when a share sale puts it into the seller's proceeds. Computing a loss on a sale that the shareholding exemption covers. Treating a depreciatory transaction as needing a motive, or value shifting as catching an ordinary dividend. And rolling over into property used outside the group.

The questions are technical prose with computations, at ten, fifteen or twenty marks, with half a mark to a mark for each point. Set out each transfer with its date and cost, then each consequence, in the order the questions arise.

What to take away.

The gains group is a principal company and its seventy five per cent subsidiaries down the chain, each an effective fifty one per cent subsidiary of the principal company. Overseas companies can be members, and a company can belong to only one gains group. Recovery of unpaid tax uses a wider fifty one per cent group.

Section one seven one moves assets between members at no gain, no loss, automatically, overriding market value, provided the asset stays within the United Kingdom charge. The transferee takes the transferor's cost, including indexation to December twenty seventeen, which still cannot create a loss. Calder's works passed to Tarnmoor Estates at four point two million pounds though worth five point six million. The water systems factory passed to Tarnmoor Water Systems at four point six million pounds though worth six million.

Section one seven three makes an asset that becomes trading stock an appropriation at market value, unless the company elects within two years to keep the gain in its trading profit.

Section one seven one A reallocates a gain or loss by joint election within two years of the end of the accounting period, and payments up to the amount are ignored. Tarnmoor Estates passed five hundred thousand pounds of gain to Tarnmoor Engineering's loss and saved one hundred and twenty five thousand pounds of tax.

Section one seven five treats the group's trades as one for roll-over. Both companies claim, a non-trading landlord can join in if the asset is used only for group trades, and nothing can be rolled into an asset bought inside the group. The depot left eight hundred thousand pounds chargeable and gave Tarnmoor Engineering a base cost of three million, seven hundred and twenty two thousand, five hundred pounds.

Pre-entry losses can be used only against gains before entry, on assets held at entry, or on outside assets used in the same continuing trade. Brackenwell's four hundred thousand pounds is likely never to be used. Sections one eight four A and one eight four B stop buying losses and gains where that is a main purpose.

Depreciatory transactions reduce losses only, with no motive test. Section three one value shifting is motive based, increases consideration, and ignores arrangements consisting solely of an exempt distribution.

The degrouping charge applies when a transferee leaves within six years holding the asset, at market value at the date of the transfer. Associated companies leaving together escape only if associated from the transfer onwards. Since twenty eleven, a departure on a share sale puts the gain into the seller's proceeds, usually exempt. Tarnmoor Actuators' two point five million pounds disappeared into an exempt sale, and an exempt demerger meant no charge at all on the water systems factory.

So the walls between the companies in a group are real, but the law opens doors in them, and then watches who walks out carrying what.

The doors in this chapter mostly lead to share sales, and the shareholding exemption kept appearing behind them. Chapter eighteen, on shares, the exemption, reorganisations and earn-outs, opens that door properly.

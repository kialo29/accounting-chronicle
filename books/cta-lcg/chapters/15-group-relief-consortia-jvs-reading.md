# Part Three: The Group.

# Chapter fifteen: Group relief, consortia and joint ventures.

On 27 October 2021, a Budget day, a set of rules written to satisfy European law quietly left the British statute book. For more than a decade a UK company had been able, in narrow circumstances, to claim relief for the losses of a subsidiary resident elsewhere in the European Economic Area (CTA 2010 Part 5 Ch 3, ss 111–128). Those rules were the long shadow of the *Marks and Spencer* litigation (*HMRC v Marks and Spencer plc* [2013] UKSC 30 and [2014] UKSC 11), which *The Living Law* (TKS), chapter 24, told. **Finance Act 2022 s 24(3)** repealed Chapter 3 with effect from that Budget day. In substance, HMRC's manual (CTM81502) now says that relief for the overseas losses of group companies resident in the EU, for periods after 26 October 2021, is no longer available.

So the border is back where it began. Losses move between companies inside the UK tax net, and almost nowhere else. And even inside it they do not flow freely. They move one company at a time, by claim and by consent, measured period by period, and they stop dead when the ownership tests fail or when someone has agreed to sell.

This chapter asks a simple question. **A group plans, borrows and reports as one business. Why does the law make its losses travel company by company, and what decides how far each one can go?**

This is the first chapter of Part Three. On the 2026 LCG grid, **group relief (including consortia) is core (grade 1)**; so are **joint ventures (1)**, **partnerships: company as a partner (1)** and **non-resident and dual resident companies (1)** (the group relief part is here; residence is chapter 22). The grid's "excluding consortia" note applies to the Awareness module, not to LCG: consortium relief is examinable, as November 2024 Q2 proved. The chapter builds on *The Living Law*, chapter 24 (75% test, basic group relief, non-coterminous periods, consortia in outline, *Marks and Spencer*, *Farnborough*).

Most of the law is in the **Corporation Tax Act 2010 (CTA 2010) Part 5** (ss 97–188) and **Part 5A** (ss 188AA–188FD); the partnership and JV rules are in the **Corporation Tax Act 2009 (CTA 2009)**; claims are governed by **FA 1998 Sch 18 Part 8**. We apply the law for **FY2026**.

## Why relief, and not one tax return.

*The Living Law*, chapter 24, made the basic point: the UK taxes each company separately. There is no consolidated return. A parent with 50 subsidiaries files 51 returns, and each company pays its own tax.

Some countries let a group file as one taxpayer. The UK chose a different bridge. **Group relief** lets one company surrender a current-year loss to another company in the same group, which claims it against its own profits. The effect looks like consolidation, but only for the amounts surrendered, only for the periods that overlap, and only between companies that pass the tests.

Picture a building with a separate water tank for every flat. Group relief is a set of pipes between the tanks. Each pipe has a valve, a gauge and a rule about which tanks it may join. The tanks are never merged.

Three consequences run through the chapter:

1. **Relief is a claim, never automatic.** Someone must make it, with consent, on time.
2. **The amount is the lower of two figures**: what the surrendering company can give and what the claimant can take (s 138).
3. **The relationship must exist throughout the period measured.** If it stops existing, even on paper (arrangements), the pipe closes.

**The debate (separate entity or single unit?).** One side says the group is the real economic unit, so tax should follow the consolidated result; the CIR (chapter 28) and the Pillar Two rules (chapter 30) already look at the worldwide group. The other side says separate companies are separate legal persons with their own creditors, minorities and regulators, and a claim-based bridge keeps the Exchequer's protections company by company. The UK keeps the second model for losses while borrowing the first for some anti-avoidance rules. The conclusion returns to the tension.

**Misconception: "a group pays tax as one".** It does not. It pays tax as many companies, some of which have agreed to pass relief to others.

Our invented group, **Tarnmoor**, shows the scale. Tarnmoor plc (TPLC), the listed parent, sits above trading, property and finance companies. Its companies, people and numbers are invented for teaching, as throughout this book. In GY2 Tarnmoor Engineering Ltd (TEL) claimed **£17.095m** of group and consortium relief from three sources, each with its own limit. This chapter takes them one at a time.

## Who is in the group.

Two companies are in the same group for group relief if one is the **75% subsidiary** of the other, or both are 75% subsidiaries of a third company (CTA 2010 s 152), holdings being traced directly or indirectly.

**Three tests, not one (s 151).** The parent must:

| Test | Requirement | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary share capital | Beneficially own ≥ 75%, directly or indirectly | s 151(1); CTA 2010 Part 24 Ch 3 (s 1154) |
| Profits | Be beneficially entitled to ≥ 75% of profits available for distribution to **equity holders** | s 151(4)(a) |
| Assets | Be beneficially entitled to ≥ 75% of assets available to equity holders on a winding up | s 151(4)(b) |

**Equity holders** are defined in Part 5 Ch 6 (ss 157–182): in outline, ordinary shareholders and loan creditors whose loans are not normal commercial loans. If the company has no profits in the period, a notional profit of £100 is assumed for the profits test (CTA 2010 explanatory notes). The extra tests stop a parent holding 75% of the shares on paper while the economic interest sits elsewhere through preference shares or special loans; **November 2024 Q2** put preference shares in the facts.

**Indirect holdings multiply.** If P owns 90% of A, and A owns 80% of B, P's indirect interest in B is 90% × 80% = **72%**: P and B are not grouped for group relief, although P–A and A–B are. Adding instead of multiplying, or stopping at the first link, are classic slips.

**Beneficially.** Chapter 14 showed that a company in liquidation loses beneficial ownership of its shares and, in HMRC's view, the group relationship through it ends (gains groups survive: TCGA 1992 s 170(11); chapter 17).

**Tracing through non-residents; relief only between UK related companies.** The chain may run through non-UK companies, but both surrendering and claimant companies must be **UK related** (s 134): UK resident, or non-UK resident and carrying on a trade in the UK through a permanent establishment.

**The case behind it.** In *HMRC v FCE Bank plc* [2012] EWCA Civ 1290, two UK companies had a common US parent; under the law as it then stood, the parent's non-residence stopped them being grouped. The Court of Appeal held that the non-discrimination article of the UK–US treaty (Art 24(5)) required group relief to be allowed. Treaties can reach into group relief; chapter 24 returns to the article.

|---|---|---|---|
| TPLC, TEL, TES, TFL, TWS | Yes | Yes | UK resident, 100% |
| Calder (CVE) from 1 April GY1; Brackenwell (BSL) from 1 July GY2 | Yes from those dates | Yes | Join on completion |

## What can be surrendered.

**CTA 2010 s 99(1)** lists seven surrenderable amounts for the **surrender period** (the surrendering company's accounting period). They fall into two families:

| Family | Amounts | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| A: surrender in full | (a) trading losses; (b) excess capital allowances; (c) non-trading loan relationship deficits | Surrenderable whether or not the company has other profits of its own (s 99) |
| B: surrender only above the **profit-related threshold** | (d) excess qualifying charitable donations; (e) UK property business losses; (f) management expenses; (g) non-trading losses on intangible fixed assets | Only so far as their total exceeds the threshold, and only the excess (s 105) |

Two features of the surrendering side:

- **Choice and splitting.** The surrendering company chooses how much to surrender and to whom; one amount may be split between several claimants, as long as the total stays within what it has.
- **Charitable donations.** A trading company cannot carry excess QCDs forward (a company with investment business can carry those made for the investment business forward as management expenses: chapter 13), so a trading company's unsurrendered excess is lost.

**The profit-related threshold (s 105(3A)).** For surrender periods ending on or after 20 March 2013 (FA 2013), the threshold is:

- the company's **gross profits** of the surrender period (broadly profits before deducting family A and B amounts of the period and any amounts of other periods: s 105(5); CTM80142); **plus**
- any **CFC chargeable profits apportioned** to it under TIOPA 2010 s 371BC step 3 (where it is a chargeable company at step 4), for CFC accounting periods ending in the surrender period.

The logic: a company should use its own family B amounts against its own income before passing the surplus to someone else; and since 2013 the CFC apportionment counts as "income" for this purpose (and the old relief against a CFC charge, TIOPA 2010 s 371UD, was repealed by F(No.2)A 2015).

> **Worked example 15.1: TPLC's surrender to TEL (£; invented)**
>

> |---|---|---|
> | Management expenses of the period | 8,000,000 | 8,500,000 |
> | Gross profits: recharge income from TVS at arm's length (non-trading, CTA 2009 s 979) | 2,100,000 | 2,200,000 |

In our invented case these expenses are **stranded**: cumulative **£825,000** (GY1), **£1.65m** (GY2), **£2.475m** (GY3), **£3.3m** (GY4), with no deferred tax asset recognised (chapter 6). GY3 and GY4 surrenders apply the same cap; in GY5 the TCM apportionment in the threshold rises to **£1,275,000**.

## How much the claimant can take.

The surrendering company's side is half the sum. **Section 137** gives relief by deduction from the claimant's total profits of the **claim period**; **s 138** limits it to the **lower** of:

- the **unused part of the surrenderable amount** for the overlapping period (s 139); and
- the **unused part of the claimant's available total profits** for the overlapping period (s 140).

**Available total profits** (s 140, by reference to s 137(4)(b)) are the claimant's total profits after its own reliefs: its own current-year losses, carried-forward losses (within Part 7ZA), and QCDs; carry-backs from later periods are ignored. Group relief therefore comes **last**, just above TTP (chapter 7's pro forma, line 10).

> **Worked example 15.2: TEL, GY2 (£; invented)**
>
> | £ | |
> |---|---|
> | Trading profits after CAs (chapter 7) = total profits | 26,000,000 |
> | Own losses, b/f losses, QCDs | nil |
> | Available total profits | 26,000,000 |
> | Group relief: TPLC management expenses (s 105 cap) | (5,475,000) |
> | Group relief: TPLC NTLR deficit | (7,670,000) |
> | Group relief: BSL post-acquisition loss (1 July–31 December GY2) | (2,600,000) |
> | Consortium relief: Helmside (link company route) | (1,350,000) |
> | **Total group and consortium relief** | **(17,095,000)** |
> | **TTP** | **8,905,000** |
> | **CT at 25%** | **2,226,250** |
> | Very large company QIPs: 4 × £556,562.50 (14 March, June, September, December GY2), on the full liability | 2,226,250 |
> | RDEC surrendered by BSL (£600,000): discharges part of the liability once claimed; not group relief; does **not** reduce the QIPs (CIRD89870, HMRC's view) | (600,000) |
> | **Net CT for GY2 after the credit** | **1,626,250** |
>
> GY1, for comparison: TTP before reliefs £24,000,000; group relief £11,665,000 (TPLC) + consortium relief £1,800,000 = £13,465,000; **TTP £10,535,000; CT £2,633,750**.

Two practical points:

- **Cash during the year.** A very large claimant pays in months 3, 6, 9 and 12; its instalments must reflect the liability after the group relief it expects to claim. A wrong forecast means QIP interest (chapter 3).
- **Keep the regimes in separate boxes.** The RDEC surrendered by Brackenwell (chapter 10) is not group relief and never nets off an instalment computation.

## When the years do not match.

The **overlapping period** (s 142) is the time common to the surrender period and the claim period. Any part of it during which the group (or consortium) condition is not met is treated as not part of the overlapping period (s 142(2)). Both companies' figures are apportioned to the overlapping period, on HMRC's approach by **time unless that would be unjust or unreasonable** (CTM80260–CTM80265, not opened: label as HMRC's guidance).

**Several claims for the same months (s 142).** Where a claimant takes relief for overlapping periods that cover the same months, prior claims attributable to those months reduce the unused part of its available total profits, so no slice of profit is relieved twice.

> **Worked example 15.3: TEL's capacity for BSL's loss, 1 July–31 December GY2 (£; invented)**
>
> | £ | |
> |---|---|
> | TEL available total profits for the 6 months (26,000,000 × 6/12) | 13,000,000 |
> | Prior claims attributable to those months: TPLC (5,475,000 + 7,670,000) × 6/12 | (6,572,500) |
> | Helmside 1,350,000 × 6/12 | (675,000) |
> | **Unused part for the BSL claim** | **5,752,500** |
> | BSL's surrenderable loss for the overlapping period | 2,600,000 |
> | **Relief (lower)** | **2,600,000** |

On the usual analysis the group relationship began at completion, when TPLC became beneficial owner: a contract whose conditions are outstanding does not by itself pass beneficial ownership (general principle; HMRC's specific guidance on conditional contracts for group relief was not opened: see notes). BSL kept its 31 December year end.

## Arrangements, and the day a group ends early.

If groups ended only at completion, a seller could agree to sell a loss-making subsidiary and keep its losses flowing to the rest of its group until the last day, while the buyer's group lined up to use them too. **CTA 2010 s 154** stops that: two companies are treated as **not in the same group** if one of them has surrenderable amounts and arrangements exist with any of the effects in s 154(3):

| Effect | In outline |
|---|---|
| 1 | One company (or a successor) could cease to be in the same group as the other and could become a member of the same group as a **third company** |
| 2 | A person has or could obtain **control** of one company but not of the other |
| Others | Further effects in s 154(3) (not opened in full) |

**Arrangements** means arrangements of any kind, written or not; no tax purpose is required (CTM80170; CTM80206 gives a simple commercial sale of a subsidiary as the standard example).

**When do they begin? (HMRC's view.)** A question of fact; usually the point of **agreement in principle that the transaction will proceed, even if still subject to contract and not binding** (CTM80190). CTM80195's example: terms agreed subject to contract on 31 January 2012, unconditional contract signed 29 February 2012: arrangements from **31 January**. They can arise earlier (for example an option granted during negotiations). Enabling arrangements (rights in existing documents) count even if not yet operable (CTM80185). The relationship is broken **only while the arrangements exist**, so the overlapping period shrinks to the time before they began.

**The case (TKS recap).** In *Farnborough Airport Properties Co v HMRC* [2019] EWCA Civ 118 (8 February 2019), a receiver was appointed over the whole property of a loss-making sister company (Piccadilly Hotels 2 Ltd) under an existing debenture. The Court of Appeal, agreeing with the FTT and UT ([2017] UKUT 394 (TCC)), held that the appointment was an arrangement under which the shareholders lost control (Effect 2): group relief for losses of about £10.6m (£10,627,709) was denied.

**Safe harbours (ss 155A–155B).** For arrangements made in accounting periods beginning on or after **1 March 2012** (previously ESC C10), certain ordinary JV provisions are not arrangements: share transfer clauses or suspended voting rights triggered by a member's voluntary departure, insolvency, change of control or default, a threatened viability event, or an unresolved disagreement between members (CTM80181). Without them almost every JV agreement would break relief from the start.

**The asymmetry.** Arrangements can **end** a relationship early; they never **start** one early. A company joins only when the ownership tests are actually met. So an acquisition delays relief on the way in and can cut it off on the way out; November 2024 Q2 tested both.

**For the tax function:** diarise the deal timetable, not just completion. When Tarnmoor sells its actuators company in GY6 (chapter 20), the date for group relief is the day the deal is agreed in principle.

## Claims, consents and payments.

Group relief is claimed in the **claimant's company tax return** (FA 1998 Sch 18 Part 8, paras 66–77).

| Rule | Detail | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Consent | Surrendering company gives written notice of consent to HMRC at or before the claim; a copy accompanies the claim | Sch 18 para 70; CTM97020 |
| Consortium claims | Consent of **every member** of the consortium as well, whichever way relief flows (a member = a company owning ≥ 5%); copies accompany the claim | para 70(2), (5); CTM80520 |
| Amending | A notice of consent cannot be amended: withdraw and replace it. Consents are given company by company and period by period; a missing consent sinks the claim | COM53100 |
| Amount | The claim is for a stated amount and may be less than the maximum | Sch 18 Part 8 |
| Time limit | Made or withdrawn up to the **latest** of: first anniversary of the claimant's filing date; 30 days after an enquiry into the claimant's return is completed; 30 days after HMRC amend the return after an enquiry; 30 days after an appeal against that amendment is finally determined (HMRC may allow later) | para 74; CTM97060 |
| Simplified arrangements | One authorised company may make and withdraw claims and consents for the group without sending copies of every notice | SI 1999/2975; CT600C guidance (HMRC's description) |

**Payments for group relief (s 183).** Where surrendering company and claimant agree, and the claimant pays an amount **not exceeding the amount surrendered**, the payment is ignored in computing both companies' profits and losses and is not a distribution. The cap is the **amount of loss**, not the tax saved: a payment of 25p per £ (the tax value) is usual, but up to £1 per £ is within the rule. Minority shareholders and directors' duties are why groups pay at all.

**Contrast the RDEC payment.** TEL's £600,000 payment to BSL for the surrendered expenditure credit (GY2) falls under a different, new rule (FA 2026 s 31, as HMRC's guidance describes it: ignored for CT if it does not exceed the credit surrendered; payments from 26 November 2025): chapter 10.

## Losses brought forward, and the group.

Before April 2017 group relief was for current-year amounts only. **CTA 2010 Part 5A** (ss 188AA–188FD) allows surrender of amounts carried forward from APs beginning on or after 1 April 2017: trading losses, NTLR deficits, management expenses, UK property losses and non-trading intangibles losses.

| Restriction | Content | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Surrendering company's own use first | HMRC: a company may not surrender carried-forward losses under Part 5A if it could deduct them from its own total profits | s 188BE; CTM82030 |
| Claimant's own use first | The claimant must first use its own carried-forward losses | Part 5A Ch 3 |
| Loss restriction | The claimant's Part 5A relief is within Part 7ZA (group's single £5m deductions allowance plus 50%) | chapter 14 |
| Joiners | Pre-acquisition losses of a company joining the group not surrenderable for 5 years | Part 14 Ch 2C; chapter 14 |
| Payments | Disregarded up to the amount of the loss | s 188FA; CTM82170 |
| Consortia | Carried-forward losses can pass between consortium companies and members, within the ownership proportion (link company: its proportion of the claimant's relevant maximum) | CTM82505–CTM82525 |

Think of current-year relief as the main door between the tanks; Part 5A is a second door that opens only after each company has used its own carried-forward losses, and the loss restriction still caps how much old loss shelters new profit wherever it lands. For the exam Part 5A matters most as a contrast: current-year relief is unlimited and immediate; carried-forward relief is conditional, capped and slow.

The first restriction explains TPLC's **stranded** £825,000 a year (book's reading, as above; not tested in any decided case). BSL's pre-acquisition losses were in any case barred until **31 December GY7** (Ch 2C) and restricted under Part 14 Ch 2 (chapter 14).

## Non-residents, branches and dual residents.

**1. Non-resident companies with a UK PE.** A non-UK resident company trading in the UK through a PE is UK related and can surrender and claim, but (in outline: s 107 not re-read) it can surrender only losses of its UK PE activities that are not deductible or otherwise allowable against non-UK profits for foreign tax. The UK will not relieve a loss another country is also relieving.

**2. EEA losses: repealed.** CTA 2010 Part 5 Ch 3 (ss 111–128) let a UK parent claim the losses of an EEA-resident subsidiary where every possibility of relief abroad was exhausted (the *Marks and Spencer* "no possibilities" test). FA 2022 s 24(3) repealed it with effect from **27 October 2021** (CTM81502; the policy paper "Abolition of cross-border group relief" explains that the UK was no longer required to keep the rules after leaving the EU). EEA-resident companies with UK PEs are now treated like other non-residents.

**3. Dual resident investing companies (s 109).** A UK resident company also within a non-UK tax charge (by incorporation, place of management or other residence criteria) cannot surrender if, in outline, it is not a trading company or its trade is mainly acquiring and holding shares or making loans and similar (conditions A–C). The fear is "double dipping": one interest cost relieved in two countries. A DRIC also cannot receive assets at no gain, no loss (TCGA 1992 s 171(2)(d); chapter 17). The examiners reported after N24 that many candidates missed the bar.

**Spotting a DRIC.** The point usually hides in the facts: a company incorporated abroad whose board meets in London, or a UK company whose directors meet abroad. Ask in turn: is it UK resident? Is it also within another country's charge? Does it carry on a real trade other than holding shares or lending? Only yes, yes and no leads to the bar.

**TIL, the near miss (invented).** Before 30 June GY4 TIL was UK resident (CMC in Leeds) with an Irish incorporation, and the UK and Irish competent authorities had agreed it was UK resident for treaty purposes. Even if Ireland had also treated it as resident, s 109 would not have bitten: TIL trades. From 30 June GY4 it is Irish resident with no UK PE (its UK warehouse is let: a UK property business, not a PE), so it is not UK related and neither surrenders nor claims.

## Consortia, and the four tape measures.

Joint ventures between groups are everywhere in infrastructure, energy and property: two or three companies put in capital and none controls the venture. None owns 75%, so group relief fails. **Consortium relief** fills the gap.

**Owned by a consortium (s 153).** A company is owned by a consortium if it is **not a 75% subsidiary of any company** and **at least 75%** of its ordinary share capital is beneficially owned by **companies each owning at least 5%** (the **members**). A trading company that is a **90% subsidiary** of a consortium-owned holding company is also consortium-owned.

**Consortium conditions (ss 132–133).**

| Condition | Who surrenders | Who claims | Requirements |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (s 132) | Consortium company → member, or member → consortium company | | Consortium company is a **trading company or holding company**; both UK related; not if a profit on a sale of the member's shares would be a trading receipt |
| 2 (s 133) | Consortium company | A company in the same group as a **link company** (a member) | Both UK related; same trading-receipt exclusion |
| 3 (s 133) | A company in the same group as a link company | Consortium company | As above |

**Refinements (in outline).** Where the consortium company heads a group of its own, relief its own group could give is taken into account first (s 149; CTM80585). Where a member is in a group, its group companies can claim or surrender through it as link company (below).

**Helmside (invented).** Helmside Energy Ltd was formed on **1 January GY1** to make hydrogen balance-of-plant equipment, and it trades from the start (a consortium company must be a trading or holding company: a pre-trading company fails). Shareholders: TPLC 45%; Greyfell Utilities plc (invented listed utility) 40%; Northlight Infrastructure Fund LP (invented limited partnership) 15%.

| Test | Helmside |
|---|---|
| A 75% subsidiary of any company? | No (largest holding 45%) |
| Ordinary share capital owned by companies each ≥ 5% | TPLC 45% + Greyfell 40% = **85%** ≥ 75% |
| Northlight | A partnership, not a company: not a member; its 15% neither counts towards the 75% nor gives relief |
| Members | **TPLC, Greyfell** |
| Trading company? | Yes |

**Ownership proportion (ss 143–144).** Relief is limited by the member's ownership proportion: the **lowest** of its percentage of (1) ordinary share capital, (2) profits available for distribution, (3) assets on a winding up, and (4) **voting power**; if any changes during the overlapping period, the average is used (s 144). In our invented case Helmside's articles give every share identical rights, so all four measures equal the shareholdings: **TPLC 45%, Greyfell 40%**.

Think of the consortium as a shared allotment: each member takes produce only in proportion to its plot, but the plot is measured with four tape measures, and the smallest reading wins.

**Misconception: "a consortium member takes the JV's losses in proportion to its shares".** It takes them in proportion to the **lowest of four ratios**, one of which is voting power, and only against its own available total profits.

> **Exam lens: consortium relief**
>
> - **Grade:** group relief **1**, consortia included (the "excluding consortia" note is for the Awareness module).
> - **Past appearances:** **N24 Q2** (above): consortium surrender limits, the link company, trading requirement; **M23 Q5** (20: a JV at 75% v 65%: some candidates missed that the second structure was a consortium).
> - **Traps:** counting a partnership or individual as a member; ignoring the 5% floor; using shares rather than the lowest of four ratios; applying the % to the member's loss when the member surrenders down; forgetting consortium consents from every member; ignoring s 147(3) (the consortium company's own potential s 37 claim) and s 155 arrangements.

## Relief up, and relief down.

**Up: consortium company to members.** The member's claim is limited to its ownership proportion of the consortium company's surrenderable amount for the overlapping period (s 143). But first, **s 147(3)**: for a consortium claim, the consortium company's loss is reduced by any s 37 claim it **could** make against its own total profits of the period. HMRC's example (CTM80696): a £10,000 trading loss and £5,000 of gains; a 50% member's claim is limited to 50% × (£10,000 − £5,000) = **£2,500**. Helmside had no other income in GY1–GY3, so nothing is deducted.

**Why Northlight's share is lost.** Consortium relief is relief between companies within the charge to CT. Northlight's investors may be individuals, pension funds or overseas institutions; the relief has no home in their hands. Each corporate member gets its fair slice and the rest stays where it fell.

**The link company (ss 133, 145, 146).** TPLC, the member, had no taxable profits (its own management expenses and deficits covered its income). Under consortium condition 2 a claimant that is not a member can claim if it is in the same group as a member, the **link company**: TPLC is the link company and **TEL claims**. The limit is measured by the **link company's** ownership proportion (s 145), and the total claimed by the link company and all its group companies cannot exceed what the link company itself could have claimed (s 146(1); CTM80525).

> **Worked example 15.5: Helmside's losses, GY1–GY3 (£; invented)**
>
> | £ | GY1 | GY2 | GY3 |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Helmside trading loss | 4,000,000 | 3,000,000 | 1,000,000 |
> | Less potential own s 37 claim (no other profits) (s 147(3)) | nil | nil | nil |
> | Loss available for consortium claims | 4,000,000 | 3,000,000 | 1,000,000 |
> | TEL via TPLC as link company (45%) | (1,800,000) | (1,350,000) | (450,000) |
> | Greyfell (40%) | (1,600,000) | (1,200,000) | (400,000) |
> | Retained by Helmside (Northlight's 15%) | 600,000 | 450,000 | 150,000 |
> | Helmside losses carried forward (cumulative) | 600,000 | 1,050,000 | 1,200,000 |
> | Payment by TEL to Helmside at 25p per £ (s 183) | 450,000 | 337,500 | 112,500 |
> | Payment by Greyfell to Helmside at 25p per £ | 400,000 | 300,000 | 100,000 |
>
> Consents: TPLC and Greyfell (the members) consent to every claim (Sch 18 para 70(2)); Helmside gives notice as surrendering company. The payment terms are in Helmside's shareholders' agreement (invented); each payment is within s 183 (less than the amount surrendered).

**Down: member to consortium company.** When a member surrenders to the consortium company, the limit is the member's ownership proportion of the **consortium company's available total profits** for the overlapping period (s 143). **Not the member's loss.** This is the trap the examiners named after N24 Q2.

**Helmside GY4 (invented).** Helmside's first profit was **£2.5m**. It first used its own carried-forward losses of **£1.2m** (s 45A; within its own £5m deductions allowance, since a consortium company is not in a 75% group: chapter 14), leaving available total profits of **£1.3m**. TPLC had surplus current-year management expenses, and the shareholders' agreement asks members to offer surplus relief to Helmside at full value (25p per £). On a **full year**, the ceiling would be 45% × £1.3m = **£585,000**. Section 155 changes that: see the next section.

**Anti-avoidance (ss 146A–146B).** For claims for APs beginning on or after **12 July 2010**, relief is **halved** where arrangements prevent a member from controlling the consortium company and a main purpose is a tax advantage for the member (CTM80525). The target is a structure that keeps a member just short of control so that relief flows as consortium relief. The Upper Tribunal applied s 146B in *HMRC v South Eastern Power Networks* [2019] UKUT 367 (TCC) (as summarised in HMRC's manual commentary and the professional press; later history not checked).

## When the consortium ends.

On **1 December GY4** TPLC and Greyfell signed **heads of terms**: TPLC would acquire Greyfell's 40% for new TPLC shares. Completion was on **1 March GY5** (chapter 18 tells the share exchange).

**Section 155 (consortium arrangements).** A trading company is **not owned by a consortium** if the trading company or a member has surrenderable amounts for an AP and arrangements exist with an effect in s 155(3), including **Effect 1: the trading company could become a 75% subsidiary of a third company**. A "**third company**" is one that is not, apart from the arrangements, in the same group as the trading company. TPLC held 45% and was not in Helmside's group, so on the statute's words **TPLC is a third company even though it is also a member**. TPLC (a member) had surrenderable amounts in GY4. The consortium relationship is broken only while the arrangements exist (CTM80605).

**When did the arrangements begin? (HMRC's view.)** Direct arrangements usually exist from agreement in principle, even if subject to contract (CTM80625, CTM80630). Tom Hesketh, Tarnmoor's group head of tax, took **1 December GY4**: in our invented case the heads of terms named a price, a structure and a timetable, and both boards had approved them.

> **Worked example 15.6: TPLC's surrender to Helmside, GY4 (£; invented)**
>
> | £ | |
> |---|---|
> | Helmside trading profit | 2,500,000 |
> | Less own carried-forward losses (s 45A) | (1,200,000) |
> | Available total profits for the AP | 1,300,000 |
> | Consortium condition met 1 January–30 November GY4: overlapping period **11 months** | |
> | Available total profits for the overlapping period (1,300,000 × 11/12) | 1,191,667 |
> | **Maximum relief: 45% × 1,191,667** | **536,250** |
> | Full-year ceiling without arrangements (45% × 1,300,000) | 585,000 |
> | Relief lost to the arrangements | 48,750 |
> | **Helmside TTP (1,300,000 − 536,250)** | **763,750** |
> | Helmside CT at 25% | 190,937.50 |
> | Payment by Helmside to TPLC at 25p per £ (s 183) | 134,062.50 |
>
> Exact arithmetic: 1,300,000 × 11/12 × 45% = £536,250 exactly. A day basis (334/365) would give £535,315; the story uses months, as the exam rubric does ("nearest month"). TPLC's surrender is within its s 105(3A) cap (threshold = recharge income + £825,000). Helmside's QIP divisor for GY4 is 1 (no company controlled it at 31 December GY3) and its profits are below £1.5m: it is not large and pays on 1 October GY5.

**Did it cost the group anything?** Very little. The £48,750 of TPLC's management expenses not used by Helmside can go to TEL instead (within TPLC's s 105 cap), worth 25p per £ either way. The arrangements rule bites hardest where relief has nowhere else to go; here it changed which company used it.

**GY5.** From **1 March GY5** TPLC owns 85% and Helmside joins the 75% group: group relief, not consortium relief, is the pipe (overlapping period 1 March–31 December GY5 with each 31 December company). For 1 January–28 February GY5 the arrangements still existed, so Helmside was neither consortium-owned nor in the group: no relief either way. On 1 March GY5 Helmside also joined the group deductions allowance and countersigned TPLC's standing nomination (chapter 14). Whether TPLC's acquisition is a change in ownership of Helmside under CTA 2010 s 719 need not be decided: Helmside had no carried-forward losses left after GY4 (chapter 14).

> **Going further: managing a consortium exit**
>
> - Treat heads of terms as the likely start of arrangements; if relief matters, model the cost of signing on 1 December rather than 31 December, or of completing before the year end.
> - Check whether the JV agreement's existing pre-emption and default clauses fall within the ss 155A–155B safe harbours, so they do not break relief from day one.
> - Record the evidence (board minutes, the date terms were agreed) in the tax file; HMRC may ask for a full account of the negotiations (CTM80190).
> - On joining the group: add the company to the group deductions allowance nomination, the group payment arrangement, SAO and the CIR group (chapters 3, 4, 14, 28).

## Choosing a joint venture structure.

The examiners asked in **M23 Q5** to compare a 75% subsidiary with a 65% one; some candidates missed that the second was a consortium.

| | 75% subsidiary | Consortium company (e.g. 65% / 35%) | Partnership or LLP |
|---|---|---|---|
| Taxpayer | JV company | JV company | Each partner on its share |
| Losses | Group relief to the investor's group, no proportion limit; the minority partner gets none | Each corporate member its ownership proportion; relief can also flow down | Each corporate partner's share is its own loss |
| Assets moving in | No gain, no loss (gains group: chapter 17) | Market value; no gains group | No gains group; each partner taxed on its share of partnership gains |
| Exit | Degrouping charges (chapter 17); arrangements | Arrangements (s 155); s 146A–146B | Partnership disposal rules |
| Dividends received | Exempt (subsidiary) | Exempt: the **40/55 JV class** or ordinary shares class | Not applicable |

> **Worked example 15.7: 75% v 65% (labelled illustration; not a story fact)**
>
> A new venture loses £1,000,000 in its first year. At **75%**, it is in the investor's group: the investor's group can claim the whole £1,000,000; the 25% partner gets nothing. At **65%**, with a company partner at 35%, it is a consortium company: investor £650,000, partner £350,000. Which is better depends on who has profits and what the partners agree about sharing relief.

**Three more JV rules.**

- **Dividends (CTA 2009 s 931E).** A distribution is in the controlled company exempt class where the recipient is one of two persons who together control the payer, the recipient holds **at least 40%** of the relevant interests, and the other holds **at least 40% but no more than 55%** (the 40/55 JV test). TPLC (45%) and Greyfell (40%) fit. Otherwise JV dividends usually fall within the ordinary shares class (s 931F).
- **SSE (TCGA 1992 Sch 7AC paras 23–24).** Special look-through rules for holdings in JV companies so that a venture's trade can count towards the investor's trading status: chapter 18.
- **Employee shares (CTA 2009 s 1008).** Part 12 relief extends to shares in a consortium member that owns the employer or its parent: Helmside's staff could receive TPLC shares, and Helmside could claim the deduction.

> **Exam lens: joint ventures**
>
> - **Grade:** joint ventures **1** (core).
> - **Past appearances:** **M23 Q5** (20 marks: a 75% v 65% JV; gains group v consortium; a property transfer with a rolled-over gain and an earlier no gain, no loss transfer; loss utilisation). Examiners: confusion over the second structure; few saw that a no gain, no loss transfer means a lower base cost later.
> - **Style:** compare structures in a table or short paragraphs; quantify relief; recommend if asked (the examiners deduct for a missing recommendation).

## Planning, and the tax function's year.

For a group like Tarnmoor, group relief is a cycle, not a year-end sum.

> **Going further: the group relief year**
>
> 1. **Forecast** each company's profits and losses at the start of the year and decide where relief will land; for very large claimants the forecast drives QIPs (cash in month 3).
> 2. **Watch the deal pipeline.** Any agreement in principle to sell a subsidiary or change a JV's ownership may start arrangements (s 154, s 155). Signing heads of terms on the 1st of a month rather than the last can cost a month of relief.
> 3. **Order of surrenders.** Current-year relief is unrestricted and certain: use it first. Fill the most valuable claimants first; watch companies whose own carried-forward losses would otherwise sit behind Part 7ZA. In Tarnmoor every profitable company pays 25%; in a smaller group, a pound of loss used to bring a company down from the marginal relief band (marginal rate 26.5%) is worth more than one used where it saves 19% or 25% (M26 Q4: the best answers used group relief to keep profits in the 19% band).
> 4. **Thresholds.** Test s 105(3A) for each family B surrenderer, including CFC apportionments; for consortium claims, check all four ownership measures, not just the register.
> 5. **Returns.** Collect consents (all members for consortium claims); diarise para 74 deadlines; document payments within s 183; consider simplified arrangements.
> 6. **Interaction.** Group relief changes which companies are taxable, and so deferred tax (chapter 6) and the CIR allocation of disallowed interest (chapter 28).
> 7. **Structure with the exit in mind.** The cleanest structure for losses on day one can be the costliest when the partners part.

## What to take away.

- **Separate taxpayers.** Group relief is a claim-based pipe between companies, measured for the overlapping period, limited to the **lower** of the surrenderable amount and the claimant's available total profits (ss 137–142).
- **Group.** 75% of ordinary share capital (indirect holdings multiplied), held beneficially, **plus** 75% of profits and assets available to equity holders (s 151). Traced through non-residents; relief only between **UK related** companies (s 134).
- **What goes.** Trading losses, excess CAs, NTLR deficits in full; QCDs, property losses, management expenses and non-trading intangibles losses only above the **profit-related threshold** (gross profits + apportioned CFC profits: s 105(3A)). TPLC: **£5,075,000** (GY1) and **£5,475,000** (GY2) of management expenses, not £5.9m/£6.3m; **£825,000** a year stranded.
- **Claimant.** Takes group relief last. TEL GY2: relief **£17.095m**; TTP **£8.905m**; CT **£2,226,250**; QIPs 4 × £556,562.50; net after the RDEC **£1,626,250**.
- **Dates.** Groups begin on beneficial ownership (usually completion) and end early under **arrangements** (s 154; HMRC: agreement in principle, even subject to contract). *Farnborough* [2019] EWCA Civ 118: a receiver's appointment broke the group.
- **Claims.** In the claimant's return with notice of consent; consortium claims need every member's consent; deadline the latest of the first anniversary of the claimant's filing date and 30 days after enquiry or appeal events (Sch 18 paras 70, 74). Payments up to the amount surrendered ignored (s 183).
- **Part 5A.** Carried-forward losses surrenderable only after own use, within Part 7ZA, never a joiner's pre-acquisition losses within 5 years.
- **Abroad.** UK PEs of non-residents can surrender losses not relievable abroad; EEA relief repealed from 27 October 2021; DRICs cannot surrender (s 109).
- **Consortium.** Not a 75% subsidiary; ≥ 75% owned by companies each ≥ 5% (s 153). Ownership proportion = lowest of four ratios (ss 143–144). Up: proportion of the consortium company's loss (after its own potential s 37 claim, s 147(3)). Down: proportion of the consortium company's **available total profits**. Link company route (ss 133, 145–146). Helmside: TEL **£1.8m / £1.35m / £0.45m**; Greyfell £1.6m / £1.2m / £0.4m; Helmside c/f **£1.2m**; GY4 surrender down **£536,250** (11 months, after the 1 December heads of terms) instead of £585,000.
- **JVs.** 75% v consortium v partnership; 40/55 dividend class; SSE look-through; s 1008.
- **Corporate partner.** Share computed on CT rules (s 1259) and allocated by profit-sharing arrangements (s 1262); mixed membership rules (s 850C, s 1264A). TES's GY4 share of Moorgate: **£400,000** net (property £550,000 less NTLR debit £150,000).

So the chapter's question has its answer. The law makes losses travel company by company because the group is not a taxpayer. It lets them travel at all because the group is a business. Every limit in this chapter sits on the line between those two truths.

Losses are not the only thing a group moves. Chapter sixteen turns to company gains on property and leases, and chapter seventeen shows how assets, like losses, move inside the group.

Part Two: Measuring Profit.

Chapter five: Tax follows the accounts.

In twenty twenty two, the Supreme Court decided a case about money that never moved. A group of companies had recorded a large expense in their accounts for share options granted to their staff. No cash left the companies. The options came from a trust set up by the parent. Yet the accounting standard said the staff's services had been consumed, and their value had to be charged as an expense.

The tax authority, His Majesty's Revenue and Customs, or H M R C, said the tax computation should ignore that charge. It argued four separate grounds. It lost on every one. The case was H M R C versus N C L Investments Limited, and the judgment was given by Lord Hamblen and Lady Rose, with whom the other three justices agreed.

So here is this chapter's question. When the accountants change their minds, or their rulebook, does the tax bill change too? And if it does, who decides when the law should step in and say no?

This chapter opens Part Two, on measuring profit. It sits underneath almost every computation the Advanced Technical examiner sets. The impact of accounting standards on taxable profits is a core topic in the syllabus, and so are the general calculation rules that Parliament lays over the top. The Living Law, chapter eleven, taught the adjustment of profits and the line between capital and revenue. This chapter asks why the starting point is the accounts at all, and what happens when the accounts themselves change. We work throughout on the law for the financial year twenty twenty six.

The shadow and the walker.

Start with the statute. The Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, which I will call the two thousand and nine Act, sets the rule in section forty six. The profits of a trade must be calculated in accordance with generally accepted accounting practice, subject to any adjustment required or authorised by law.

That one sentence carries two ideas. The first is that tax begins with the accountant's number. The second is that the law may overrule it, but only where a rule says so. Section forty six also says there is no need to follow company law on accounts, except as to the basis of calculation. The tax computation borrows the method, not the paperwork.

Why build tax on the accounts? Three reasons. Accounts already exist, so a second, separate measurement of profit would double the cost for every company. Large companies' accounts are audited and must give a true and fair view, so they carry their own discipline. And accounting standards are written by people whose whole job is to measure profit over a period, which is exactly the question tax needs answered.

The courts reached the same view long before Parliament wrote it down. In Odeon Associated Theatres versus Jones, decided by the Court of Appeal in nineteen seventy one, the issue was the cost of catching up on deferred repairs to cinemas the company had acquired. Lord Justice Salmon's approach, as H M R C's own manual summarises it, has two steps. Follow the ordinary principles of commercial accounting so far as practicable, and then check that no rule of tax law is broken.

Two later cases pushed further. In Gallagher versus Jones, in nineteen ninety three, a taxpayer leased boats with heavy rents payable at the start. The Court of Appeal held that the rents should be spread across the lease, as the accounts spread them, and not deducted when paid. In Johnston versus Britannia Airways, decided in the High Court in nineteen ninety four, an airline provided each year for the future cost of overhauling its jet engines. The provision was accepted for tax because it reflected accepted accounting practice.

Here is an image to hold. Tax follows the accounts the way a shadow follows a walker. Wherever the accountant goes, the taxable profit goes too, at the same pace. But at certain spots Parliament has nailed the shadow to the floor. The walker moves on, and the shadow stays put. Those nails are the adjustments required or authorised by law.

The precise rule matters more than the image. Accounting practice decides when income and expense are recognised, and how they are measured. It does not decide whether an item is capital or revenue for tax, whether an expense is wholly and exclusively for the trade, or whether a specific statute blocks it. Those are questions of law. Accounting evidence is relevant to them, often persuasive, but never conclusive.

What counts as generally accepted.

Generally accepted accounting practice has a statutory meaning. The Corporation Tax Act twenty ten, which I will call the twenty ten Act, defines it in section eleven twenty seven. In outline, United Kingdom accounting practice means practice for accounts intended to give a true and fair view. The tax definition also embraces international accounting standards where a company uses them.

In practice there are three rulebooks a large group will meet. International financial reporting standards, which accountants call I F R S, are written by an international board and used in the consolidated accounts of every listed group. The Financial Reporting Council writes the United Kingdom standards, numbered F R S one hundred to one hundred and five.

Two of them matter most here. F R S one oh one is the reduced disclosure framework. It lets a subsidiary of a listed group use the international recognition and measurement rules, with lighter notes to the accounts. F R S one oh two is the main United Kingdom standard, originally based on the international standard for smaller entities. For tax, the distinction matters because recognition and measurement drive the numbers. A company on F R S one oh one will usually recognise income and expense at the same time as its parent's group accounts.

Our invented group, Tarnmoor, shows the pattern. Tarnmoor plc is a listed engineering group, invented for this book, and it prepares its consolidated accounts under international standards. Its group policy is that every United Kingdom subsidiary reports under F R S one oh one. That way, each subsidiary's numbers feed straight into the group accounts without a second set of adjustments.

A group that mixes rulebooks can create a tax gap between two companies. Suppose one company uses international standards and its sister uses F R S one oh two. They make a deal with each other. One standard makes the seller recognise income later, while the other lets the buyer take the expense earlier. Across the group, the tax deduction runs ahead of the taxable income.

Parliament closed that gap in section nine hundred and ninety six of the twenty ten Act. Where two companies in a group use different frameworks, and the difference gives a tax advantage on a transaction between them, both are treated as using United Kingdom accounting practice. The rule is narrow and mechanical. It does not ban mixed frameworks. It removes the advantage that mixing would otherwise create.

Where Parliament nails the shadow down.

The adjustments required by law come in layers. The first layer is the case law and general statute that every trader meets, which The Living Law, chapter eleven, covered. Capital expenditure is not deductible. Expenses not incurred wholly and exclusively for the trade are not deductible. Depreciation is replaced by capital allowances.

The second layer is a set of specific rules gathered in Part twenty of the two thousand and nine Act, headed general calculation rules. The examiner treats it as core. It runs from section twelve eighty eight to section thirteen oh nine, and it reads like a list of the places where the accounts are not trusted.

Here is the list in outline. Pay that is charged in the accounts but not paid within nine months of the period end is deductible only when paid, under section twelve eighty eight. Contributions to employee benefit trusts face their own timing rules from section twelve ninety onwards. Business entertainment and gifts are blocked by section twelve ninety eight, with narrow exceptions. Charitable donations are kept out of the computation of income by section thirteen oh one B, because they get their own relief later, against total profits. Penalties and late payment interest on tax are blocked by section thirteen oh three. Payments that are themselves crimes, such as bribes, are blocked by section thirteen oh four. Dividends and other distributions are never deductible, under section thirteen oh five. Chapter seven works through each of these in a full computation.

Parliament still adds to the list. The newest entry is section thirteen oh five B, inserted by the Finance Act twenty twenty six. It concerns assets that a company transfers to a securitisation company but which stay on the transferring group's balance sheet, because the accountants say the risks never really left. The company then shows a liability alongside the asset. The new rule denies deductions on that liability where any party has a main purpose of obtaining a tax advantage, on a just and reasonable apportionment. It applies to accounting periods beginning on or after the twenty sixth of November, twenty twenty five, the date of the Budget that announced it.

The third layer is the most interesting, because it shows the law rejecting accounts that were perfectly correct as accounts. The story is G D F Suez Teesside versus H M R C, decided by the Court of Appeal in twenty eighteen. The company, which had operated the Teesside power station, held claims against companies in the collapsed Enron group. It transferred those claims to a non-resident subsidiary in exchange for shares.

The company's accounts complied with accounting practice, and on that basis the transfer produced no taxable credit that reflected the claims' value. The loan relationship rules of the time, in the Finance Act nineteen ninety six, required the accounting to fairly represent the company's profits and losses. The Court of Appeal held that this requirement could override accounts that were compliant with practice. A credit equal to the value of the claims had to be brought into account, and the company's appeal was dismissed.

The lesson is not that accounts can be ignored at will. The fairly represents test was itself a statutory adjustment, required by law. When the loan relationship rules were reformed from twenty sixteen, Parliament moved them to a simpler footing, which the next section but one describes. The general principle survived the case intact. Tax follows the accounts unless a rule of law says otherwise, and the rule must be found, not assumed.

A debit with no cash.

Now back to the share options. In N C L Investments, the companies employed staff and made them available to other group companies for a fee. The parent's employee benefit trust granted options over the parent's shares to those staff. International standards required the companies to charge the fair value of the options as an expense, because the staff's services had been consumed in return for them. The matching entry was a credit to equity, treated as a capital contribution from the parent.

H M R C argued that the debits should not count. It said the law required an adjustment under section forty six. It said the expense was not wholly and exclusively for the trade. It said the debits were capital. And it said the employee benefit trust rules in section twelve ninety blocked them. The tribunals, the Court of Appeal and then the Supreme Court rejected every argument.

The Supreme Court's core reasoning was short. The debits were properly computed under accounting practice. They reflected a real cost of the trade, the consumption of employees' services. No statute required them to be removed. In the words of the Supreme Court, what matters is the character of the Debits, not that of any corresponding credit. A capital credit to equity did not turn a revenue expense into a capital one.

The case is a warning to anyone who thinks a tax deduction needs a cash payment. Under section forty six, it needs an accounting debit that the law does not reject. The burden is on the person who says the accounts should be overridden to find the rule.

There is a twist, and it matters for the exam. N C L concerned accounting periods before the Finance Act twenty thirteen rewrote the rule that now governs most share schemes. For shares and options within the statutory relief for employee share acquisitions, the two thousand and nine Act blocks a deduction for the accounting charge itself, in section ten thirty eight. The company gets a different deduction instead, based on the value of the shares when the employee acquires them. Chapter seven owns that relief. For this chapter, the point is the method. The courts followed the accounts, and when Parliament wanted a different answer, it legislated.

Loan relationships and derivatives follow profit or loss.

The loan relationship rules apply the same philosophy with a sharper edge. Sections three hundred and seven and three hundred and eight of the two thousand and nine Act set the rule. The amounts taxed are those recognised in the company's profit or loss for the period under accounting practice. The derivative contract rules say the same in sections five hundred and ninety five and five hundred and ninety seven.

Two refinements matter. First, amounts that accountants record in other comprehensive income, outside profit or loss, are not taxed when recorded there. They are taxed if and when the accounts recycle them into profit or loss. Second, an entry made directly in equity is not an amount recognised in profit or loss at all. Union Castle Mail Steamship versus H M R C was decided by the Court of Appeal in twenty twenty, under the older rules. It concerned a scheme that tried to turn an equity entry on derivatives into a tax loss of thirty nine point one million pounds. The Court of Appeal held that the amount did not count, and the scheme failed. Today's focus on profit or loss makes that kind of scheme harder still.

The law also overrides the accountant's choice of measurement in places. Any accounting basis that complies with practice can normally be used. But between connected companies, section three hundred and forty nine insists on amortised cost, even if the accounts use fair value. And the Disregard Regulations take certain fair value swings on hedging derivatives out of the computation. Chapter twelve teaches all of this in full, and it matters for our invented group's treasury company, which hedges its listed notes with an interest rate swap.

Provisions, present obligations and the general reserve.

A provision is an accountant's estimate of a cost the business has become committed to but not yet paid. It is where the shadow most often parts company with the cash. So it is where H M R C looks hardest.

H M R C's view, set out in its Business Income Manual, is that a provision is deductible if four things are true. It is made in accordance with accounting practice. It relates to revenue, not capital, expenditure. It does not conflict with a statutory rule about when the expense is allowed. And it is estimated with sufficient accuracy. Each test does real work.

The accounting test now has teeth of its own. Modern standards allow a provision only where the business has a present obligation from a past event, the payment is probable, and the amount can be reliably estimated. That is why the Britannia Airways case is a lesson in history as much as in law. H M R C's manual points out that today's standards would not allow a provision for overhauling engines on aircraft the company owns, because no present obligation exists until the work is done. The tax law has not changed. The accounts have, and the tax has followed them.

The capital test catches provisions for costs that would themselves be capital, such as a write-down of plant or a provision to demolish a building. The timing test catches provisions that a specific statute overrides, such as unpaid pay beyond nine months or pension contributions, which are deductible only when paid. The accuracy test catches round-sum guesses.

That last test explains an old examination favourite, the general provision. A general provision is a reserve set aside against the risk that some unidentified customers will not pay, often a flat percentage of debtors. It is not an estimate of any particular loss, so it is not deductible. A specific impairment of a named customer's debt, properly estimated, is deductible. For companies, trade debts are money debts, and losses on them come in through the loan relationship rules, as H M R C's manual explains. Chapter twelve takes that further.

Watch the direction. When a general provision goes up, the accounts show an expense that tax will not allow, so you add it back. When it goes down, the accounts show a credit that tax never charged, so you deduct it. The examiners reported, after the May twenty twenty three sitting, that the common mistake in a well answered computation was getting the bad debt provision the wrong way round. Modern expected credit loss accounting blurs the old line between general and specific, and that wider debate is beyond the exam. In an exam question, follow the label the examiner gives you.

Now a moment from our invented case. In Group Year Three, on the fifteenth of June, Calder Valve Engineering announces that it will close its process valves division. Calder is the regional manufacturer The Living Law followed, invented for that book, and by now it is a Tarnmoor subsidiary. The announcement creates a present obligation in the accounting sense, because the staff now have a valid expectation that the closure will happen.

In its nine month accounting period to the thirty first of December, Group Year Three, Calder charges a restructuring provision of one point eight million pounds. The full working is in the reading edition. In round terms, one point two million pounds is redundancy and notice pay. Four hundred thousand pounds is a supply contract for castings Calder can no longer use. And two hundred thousand pounds is outplacement and retraining. By the year end, one point three million pounds has been paid. The other five hundred thousand pounds is still a provision.

Graham Pike, Calder's finance director, asks Tom Hesketh, Tarnmoor's head of tax, whether the half million still in the provision is deductible now. Both are invented characters. Tom's analysis follows H M R C's four tests. The provision complies with the standard. The costs are revenue, because Calder's trade continues in its other divisions. The amounts are specific and carefully estimated. The redundancy payments still owed are paid in March of the next year, within nine months, so no timing rule bites. The whole one point eight million pounds is deductible in the nine month period. Dan Hartley's own package, the one The Living Law, chapter nine, followed from his side, is part of it.

When the accountants change their minds.

Accounting policies change. A new standard arrives, a group imposes its own rulebook on a company it buys, or a company decides a different policy gives a truer picture. Each time, income or expense can fall between two stools. It may be taxed twice, once under the old basis and again under the new. Or it may never be taxed at all.

Chapter fourteen of Part three of the two thousand and nine Act, sections one hundred and eighty to one hundred and eighty seven, exists to stop both outcomes. It applies when a company changes the basis on which it calculates its trading profits from one period of account to the next. Both bases must have been valid, meaning each accorded with the law and practice for its own period.

A change of basis covers two things. The first is a change of accounting policy, and the law says expressly that this includes moving from United Kingdom practice to international standards, or back again. H M R C's manual treats any switch of accounting framework as a change of policy. The second is a change in the tax adjustments the company makes, including a change of view about what the law requires.

Two things are not a change of basis. A change made to comply with new legislation that did not apply to the earlier period is excluded. Parliament deals with its own changes through its own transitional rules. And correcting a fundamental error is not a change of basis either, because the old basis was never valid. An error belongs to the period it affected, and is put right there, through the normal routes for correcting returns.

The mechanics come in two steps, in section one hundred and eighty two. Step one totals the understatements, such as income that fell out of the old periods without being taxed, or expenses deducted twice. Step two totals the overstatements, such as income taxed twice or expenses never deducted. The net figure is the adjustment.

Then comes the rule that surprises people. Under section one hundred and eighty one, a positive adjustment is treated as a receipt, and a negative one as an expense. Either way, it arises on the first day of the first period of account on the new basis. There is no general spreading for corporation tax. The whole adjustment lands in a single period.

There are three softer edges. If an expense already deducted under the old basis would be deducted again under the new, section one hundred and eighty three simply denies the second deduction. Where the change comes from a tax adjustment about stock or depreciation, section one hundred and eighty four defers the adjustment until the asset is realised or written off. And where a company moves its trading stock from cost to fair value, the receipt waits until the stock is realised, with an election to spread it over six periods instead. That election must be made within twelve months of the end of the first period on the new basis.

Calder changes its rulebook.

Back to our invented case, a year earlier. Tarnmoor bought Calder on the first of April in Group Year One. Calder kept its thirty first of March year end for now, and its accounts for its first year in the group were still prepared under F R S one oh two. From the first of April in Group Year Two, group policy applies, and Calder reports under F R S one oh one.

The switch is a change of accounting policy within Chapter fourteen, because both frameworks were valid for their periods. In our invented case, applying the group's revenue policies, as the international rules require, brings forward revenue on part completed installation contracts. Revenue that Calder's old policy would have recognised in the new period has already been earned, on the new view, in the old one. So it would never be taxed at all unless the law caught it.

The net adjustment is a positive four hundred thousand pounds. Under section one hundred and eighty one it is a receipt arising on the first of April in Group Year Two, the first day of the new basis. It is taxed in full in Calder's accounting period to the thirty first of March, Group Year Three. It is not spread.

At twenty five per cent, the extra tax is one hundred thousand pounds. Calder is now a very large company in a large group, so it pays its tax in quarterly instalments during the period itself, in months three, six, nine and twelve. Chapter three explains the thresholds. Each of the four instalments rises by twenty five thousand pounds, the first of them due on the fourteenth of June, Group Year Two. Graham Pike's cash forecast had assumed the change cost nothing, because the group's consolidated accounts were unaffected. The tax function's job is to spot the gap before the first instalment, not after it.

Leases come onto the balance sheet.

For decades, a tenant's accounts showed rent as an expense, and the tax computation deducted it. Then the accounting changed. Under the international leasing standard, I F R S sixteen, which took effect for periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty nineteen, a lessee no longer separates finance leases from operating leases. Almost every lease goes onto the balance sheet. The tenant shows a right-of-use asset and a matching lease liability. The rent disappears from the profit and loss account, replaced by depreciation of the asset and interest on the liability.

The United Kingdom standard has now followed. The Financial Reporting Council's amendments to F R S one oh two, from its periodic review, apply to accounting periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six. They bring leases onto the balance sheet in the same way, with exemptions for short leases and leases of low value items. In our invented case, Calder adopted the amended standard in the year The Living Law followed it through, its first year beginning after that date.

Parliament's first instinct, years earlier, had been to freeze the shadow. The Finance Act twenty eleven, in section fifty three, said that changes in lease accounting standards would be ignored for tax. When the international standard finally arrived, the Finance Act twenty nineteen took the opposite view. Schedule fourteen to that Act repealed the freeze and let tax follow the new accounts, with one important cushion.

The cushion is spreading. When a change of accounting policy first brings a right-of-use asset onto the balance sheet, the transition adjustment is not taxed or relieved on day one. It is spread over a spreading period, which is the weighted average remaining term of the leases concerned. Each lease's share of the total adjustment is multiplied by its remaining term, and the results are added up. The amounts are then apportioned by days. The rule is not tied to the international standard by name, so it applies automatically to companies adopting the amended F R S one oh two.

For this example, suppose Calder's transition left a net expense of ninety thousand pounds. Sixty thousand pounds came from a warehouse lease with eight years to run, and thirty thousand pounds from a fleet of forklift trucks with two years left. Two thirds of the adjustment carries a weight of eight years, and one third a weight of two. The weighted average is six years. So Calder deducts fifteen thousand pounds a year for six years, instead of ninety thousand pounds at once.

Beyond the transition, the underlying tax law is unchanged. H M R C's guidance says the twenty nineteen changes were designed to give no tax advantage or disadvantage to lessees. H M R C's view is that, in practice, the tenant deducts the depreciation and the interest instead of the rent. Capital elements inside the right-of-use asset, such as a premium paid for the lease or stamp duty land tax, are not deductible as part of that depreciation and must be stripped out. And every lease must still be tested against the long funding lease rules, which chapter nine explains. A lease of plant that counts as a long funding lease gives the tenant capital allowances instead.

The corporate interest restriction also notices. The Institute of Chartered Accountants in England and Wales reads the rules this way. Interest on a lease liability counts as interest for that regime only where the lease would have been a finance lease under the old accounting test. So the old classification survives in the tax records, even though the accounts no longer show it. Chapter twenty eight picks this up.

The spreading myth.

Here is a belief that costs real money. People assume that when an accounting standard changes, the tax effect can be spread over several years. Usually it cannot. They believe it because the most famous recent change, the leasing one, was spread. And the loan relationship rules also spread some transition amounts. So the exception is mistaken for the rule.

The rule is Chapter fourteen's. A change of basis in a trade is taxed or relieved in full on the first day of the new basis. That includes the big revenue change that came with the amended F R S one oh two, which moved United Kingdom revenue recognition onto a five step model aligned with the international standard. The same Institute warned, in March twenty twenty six, that the transition profit could push a company into quarterly instalments in a single year. It could also push profits over the five million pound allowance for using carried forward losses.

There are only three established routes to spreading. First, the lease transition, under Schedule fourteen to the Finance Act twenty nineteen. Second, the election for a move of trading stock to fair value, over six periods. Third, prescribed loan relationship and derivative amounts under the change of accounting practice regulations. As H M R C's manual describes them, those regulations bring prescribed amounts into account at one tenth a year over ten years. A special five year pattern applies to certain changes in the accounting for a company's own credit risk, at forty, twenty five, fifteen, ten and ten per cent. The amended regulations are not shown in their current form on the official legislation website, so the manual is the working source.

Outside those routes, the loan relationship rules have their own transition mechanism in sections three hundred and fifteen and three hundred and sixteen. Suppose a change of policy means the tax carrying value of a loan at the end of one period differs from its value at the start of the next. The difference is brought in as a credit or debit in the later period. Again, a change made only to comply with new legislation is excluded.

So the honest answer to a finance director who asks whether a change of standard can be spread is a question back. Which change, and under which rule?

Accounts in another currency.

The accounts follow the business, and some businesses do not think in pounds. A United Kingdom company that buys, sells and borrows mainly in euros may find that its accounts measure everything in euros. Its functional currency, in the accountant's language, is the currency of the main economic environment in which it operates.

The tax law starts from sterling. Section five of the twenty ten Act sets the basic rule that profits are calculated in sterling. Sections six to eight then follow the accounts. If a company's accounts are in a foreign currency but its functional currency is sterling, the profits are worked out in sterling, as if the accounts had been kept in sterling. If its functional currency is not sterling, the profits are worked out in that functional currency. And a company whose accounts are kept in a foreign currency outside those two cases works in the accounts currency.

Either way, the final figure is turned into sterling under section eleven. H M R C's manual says the translation uses an average rate for the period or an appropriate spot rate, and that an average rate will usually be right. Losses carried back or forward between periods have their own translation rules.

For this example, suppose a United Kingdom subsidiary with a euro functional currency makes taxable profits of four point six million euros. Using the book's assumed story rate of one point one five euros to the pound, invented for illustration, that is four million pounds. Tax at twenty five per cent is one million pounds. The point is not the arithmetic. It is that exchange movements on the company's own euro assets and debts largely drop out, because tax is measured in euros too. Its sterling items now produce the exchange differences instead.

Investment companies have an extra choice. An investment company's functional currency is often its parent's currency, which may not match its assets. Section nine A of the twenty ten Act lets a United Kingdom resident investment company elect a designated currency, if conditions are met. One condition, as H M R C reads it, is that a significant proportion of the company's assets and liabilities are in that currency. The election has applied to periods of account beginning on or after the first of April, twenty eleven.

Exchange gains and losses on loans are part of the loan relationship rules, with one carve out worth knowing. Translation differences that the accounts record in other comprehensive income, when a business's results are translated into another currency, are left out. The Advanced Technical syllabus names profits accounted for in a foreign currency and the functional currency election, so expect them as add-on marks rather than a whole question.

The tax function and accounting change.

For a group tax function, accounting change is a project, not a footnote. Four tasks recur.

First, get a seat at the table early. When a group moves a subsidiary onto its policies, as Tarnmoor did with Calder, the tax team should model the Chapter fourteen adjustment before the first day of the new basis. A positive adjustment is taxed at once, through instalments that may start in month three.

Second, quantify the cash effect separately from the accounting effect. A change that leaves the consolidated accounts untouched can still move a subsidiary's tax, its instalments, its use of carried forward losses, and the group's corporate interest restriction. Chapter six adds the deferred tax side.

Third, document provisions as they are made. The four tests are factual. A board paper showing the announcement date, the people affected and the basis of each estimate is the evidence that wins an enquiry years later.

Fourth, connect accounting judgements to governance. A provision against an uncertain tax position can itself trigger notification under the uncertain tax treatment rules, which chapter four explains. And the senior accounting officer's certificate depends on accounting systems that get these adjustments right.

There is a judgement call too. A company may face a choice of accounting policy that happens to defer tax. Choosing a policy because it is the truer picture is ordinary. Choosing one only for the tax, especially across group companies, invites the mixed framework rule, the loan relationship purpose tests, and the question every adviser should ask about professional conduct.

How the examiner tests it.

The impact of accounting standards on taxable profits is core, and so are the general calculation rules in Part twenty. Neither usually appears as a whole question. Instead, accounting items run through every computation the examiner sets, and they cost candidates marks in predictable places.

The examiners have reported the same errors across sittings. A general bad debt provision taken in the wrong direction, in May twenty twenty three. Capitalised revenue expenditure handled badly, in November twenty twenty three. Errors of direction when adjusting the accounting profit, in May twenty twenty six. Impairments that need sorting between a trade debt and a loan to a fellow subsidiary, in November twenty twenty four.

Two of those deserve a sentence each. Impairing land, as in May twenty twenty five, is a capital write-down, so it is added back. And a loan to a fellow subsidiary is a connected loan relationship, so writing it off gives no debit, while a trade debt owed by an outside customer does.

The Chartered Institute of Taxation's prospectus for the paper says candidates are expected to understand current accounting issues relevant to tax, naming deferred tax, profit recognition and share schemes. Profit recognition is this chapter. The functional currency material sits inside the core row on calculating the liability, which, reading the syllabus grid, is not limited to sterling for this paper.

The style is technical prose and computation, never a letter. Marks come at half a mark or a mark per valid point, so a short reason beside each adjustment is worth more than a paragraph at the end. In a computation, start from the profit before tax, and label every adjustment with its reason. When you meet a change of accounting standard, say whether Chapter fourteen applies, when the adjustment arises, and whether any spreading rule applies. Usually none does. The reading edition sets out the past appearances and the traps in full.

What to take away.

The chapter asked whether the tax bill changes when the accountants change their minds. Mostly, yes. Tax follows the accounts, and that is a choice Parliament made deliberately, because accounts are already prepared, audited and built to measure profit over time. But the law nails the shadow down wherever it chooses to.

Here are the rules to keep.

Section forty six of the two thousand and nine Act. Trading profits are calculated in accordance with generally accepted accounting practice, subject to any adjustment required or authorised by law. The accounts decide timing and measurement. The law decides capital or revenue, wholly and exclusively, and anything a statute blocks.

The cases. Odeon, Gallagher and Britannia Airways built the accounting principle. N C L Investments, in the Supreme Court in twenty twenty two, held that a properly computed accounting debit counts unless a statute removes it, cash or no cash. G D F Suez Teesside, in the Court of Appeal in twenty eighteen, showed a statutory test overriding accounts that were correct as accounts.

Part twenty of the two thousand and nine Act. Unpaid pay beyond nine months, employee benefit contributions, entertaining and gifts, charitable donations, penalties, crime-related payments, distributions, and from the Finance Act twenty twenty six, losses on non-derecognition liabilities in securitisations.

Loan relationships and derivatives. Amounts in profit or loss are taxed. Other comprehensive income is taxed only when recycled. Connected companies use amortised cost.

Provisions. In H M R C's view, a provision is deductible if it follows accounting practice, is revenue, meets any statutory timing rule and is accurately estimated. A general provision is not deductible. Watch the direction of any movement. Calder's one point eight million pound restructuring provision is deductible in its nine month period.

Change of basis. Chapter fourteen of Part three. A change of accounting policy, or of view of the law, but not a change forced by new legislation, and not the correction of an error.

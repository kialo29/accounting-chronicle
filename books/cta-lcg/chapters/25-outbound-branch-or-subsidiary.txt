Chapter twenty five: Outbound, branch or subsidiary.

A choice that may not last.

On the thirteenth of July, twenty twenty six, H M R C published draft legislation that would take away the choice this chapter is about. For fifteen years, a United Kingdom company with a branch abroad has been able to decide whether that branch's profits and losses stay inside United Kingdom corporation tax, or fall outside it. The draft would make the exemption compulsory, for accounting periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty seven. A policy paper published in May had explained the aim. It was directed at companies using the losses and capital allowances of foreign branches to reduce their United Kingdom profits.

That draft is proposed, not law. Your examination in twenty twenty seven tests the Finance Act twenty twenty six, and under that Act the exemption is still an election. So the old question is still the live one, and it is one of the oldest in international tax. When a company goes abroad, should it go as itself, through a branch, or through a new company, a subsidiary? And if it goes as itself, should it ask to have the branch taken out of the United Kingdom tax base?

This is chapter twenty five, the fourth chapter of Part Four, on crossing borders. Overseas branches and subsidiaries sit inside the core syllabus row on residence and permanent establishments. Foreign dividends and unremittable income are core. Cross-border transfers between companies of different member states, and international movements of capital, are non-core. The Living Law, chapter twenty six, introduced the branch and the subsidiary, the exemption election and its opening negative amount, the dividend exemption, and the relief for incorporating a branch. Here we take each to Advanced Technical depth.

Most of the law sits in the Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, which I will call the two thousand and nine Act. Double tax relief is in the Taxation, International and Other Provisions, Act twenty ten, which I will call the International Act. Incorporation reliefs are in the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act nineteen ninety two, the Gains Act. Throughout, we apply the law for the financial year twenty twenty six.

Two ways to go abroad.

The question exists because a United Kingdom resident company is taxed on its profits wherever they arise. That is the starting rule in the two thousand and nine Act. A branch is not a separate person. It is the company itself, doing business in another country. So the branch's profits are the company's profits, and they sit in its United Kingdom computation, whatever tax the other country also charges.

A subsidiary is different. It is a new company, with its own legal personality. If it is incorporated abroad and managed abroad, it is not resident in the United Kingdom, and the United Kingdom does not tax its profits as they arise. Chapter twenty two explained how a company's residence follows its central management and control. A foreign subsidiary whose real decisions are taken in Leeds can find itself resident in Leeds, and that would defeat the purpose of creating it.

The Living Law used a homely picture. A branch is a spare room in your own house. A subsidiary is a separate flat that you own. What happens in the spare room is part of your household's accounts. The flat has its own meter, its own bills and its own tenancy, and money comes back to you only when the flat pays you something.

For most of the last century the United Kingdom taxed both routes on a credit basis. Branch profits were taxed as they arose, and dividends from foreign subsidiaries were taxed when they arrived, with credit for the foreign tax already paid. Then came the territorial turn. Dividends from foreign companies became broadly exempt from the first of July, two thousand and nine. The branch exemption election arrived on the nineteenth of July, twenty eleven, inserted by the Finance Act twenty eleven. A year later the reformed controlled foreign company rules completed the set. The United Kingdom moved from taxing foreign profits with credit to leaving most of them alone, while building tools to stop profits being diverted from home.

So a company going abroad has three possible positions, not two. A branch taxed in the United Kingdom with credit. A branch exempted by election. Or a subsidiary, whose profits come home as exempt dividends. The rest of this chapter takes each in turn, and then shows how a board chooses.

The branch taxed as part of the company.

Start with the default. Without an election, the branch is simply part of the company's business. If the company trades in the United Kingdom and also carries out a contract abroad through a permanent establishment there, the foreign profits and losses are part of one trading computation. The other country taxes the profits attributable to the permanent establishment, as its treaty with the United Kingdom allows. The United Kingdom taxes them again, and gives credit for the foreign tax.

That has two consequences, and both matter to a board. First, a branch loss reduces United Kingdom tax at once. A new venture that loses money in its early years is relieved against the company's other profits in the same period. Second, a profitable branch never pays less than the United Kingdom rate overall. If the foreign rate is lower, the United Kingdom collects the difference. Credit relief only ever tops foreign tax up to United Kingdom tax. It never refunds foreign tax that is higher.

Chapter twenty four set out how credit works. The credit for each source is capped at the United Kingdom tax on that income, after deductions are allocated to it, and the company may allocate its deductions to maximise relief. Two refinements matter for branches. Unrelieved foreign tax on a permanent establishment's profits can be carried forward, or back for up to three years, against United Kingdom tax on the same permanent establishment's profits. And sometimes a branch loss is used abroad against someone else's profits, for example in a foreign group consolidation. Then the foreign tax available for credit on the branch's later profits is reduced by the tax that loss saved.

Now meet our invented group, Tarnmoor. Tarnmoor P L C is a listed engineering group based in Leeds, and everything about it, its companies, people and numbers, is invented for teaching. In Group Year One it bought Calder Valve Engineering, a valve maker with a thirty first of March year end. In August of Group Year Two, Calder began an installation contract at a refinery in Vallaria, an invented country with a corporation tax rate of twenty per cent. The project ran for fourteen months, until September of Group Year Three. The treaty between the United Kingdom and Vallaria, also invented, treats a construction or installation project lasting more than twelve months as a permanent establishment. So Calder had a Vallarian branch.

The branch lost three hundred thousand pounds in Calder's accounting period to the thirty first of March, Group Year Three. That loss simply reduced Calder's taxable trading profits, saving seventy five thousand pounds of United Kingdom tax at twenty five per cent. In Calder's next period, the nine months to the thirty first of December, Group Year Three, the branch made nine hundred thousand pounds. Vallaria taxed it at twenty per cent, one hundred and eighty thousand pounds. The United Kingdom charged twenty five per cent, two hundred and twenty five thousand pounds, and gave credit for the one hundred and eighty thousand. Calder paid a top-up of forty five thousand pounds. To keep the story simple, we ignore Vallaria's own loss relief, which in real life would probably have reduced the Vallarian tax on the second year's profit.

How does the credit limit work on a branch inside a trade? The branch's profit is part of Calder's single trading computation. So the International Act limits the credit to the United Kingdom tax attributable to the income from that particular contract, after a just and reasonable share of the trade's deductions. In Calder's case the limit was twenty five per cent of nine hundred thousand pounds, well above the Vallarian tax, so every pound of Vallarian tax was credited.

Look at the whole life of the project. Vallaria collected one hundred and eighty thousand pounds. The United Kingdom gave seventy five thousand pounds of relief and then collected forty five thousand. The group's total tax was one hundred and fifty thousand pounds on a net profit of six hundred thousand pounds. That is exactly twenty five per cent. Without an election, a branch is taxed at the United Kingdom rate on its net result, with Vallaria taking its share first.

The election.

The alternative is the election in section eighteen A of the two thousand and nine Act. When a company has made it, exemption adjustments are made in calculating its taxable total profits. Profits of its foreign permanent establishments are left out. So are their losses. That symmetry is the price. The exempt branch's profits escape United Kingdom tax, and its losses no longer reduce it.

The mechanics work territory by territory. For each foreign territory where the company has a permanent establishment, there is a relevant profits amount or a relevant losses amount. The statute adds up the profits amounts, subtracts the losses amounts, and calls the result the foreign permanent establishments amount. It can be positive or negative, and the adjustments remove it from the computation, whichever it is.

How much profit belongs to the branch? Suppose the United Kingdom has a full treaty with the territory, meaning one with a non-discrimination article that covers permanent establishments. Then the profit is what that treaty's business profits article would attribute to the branch. Where there is no full treaty, the profit is what would be attributed under a treaty in the form of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development Model. The Finance Act twenty twenty six updated the statute's definition of that O E C D Model to the version approved on the eighteenth of November, twenty twenty five. Capital allowances are handled by notional allowances. H M R C's manual explains that they are given automatically, on the most favourable assumptions, so that allowances on an exempt branch's assets cannot reduce the profits that stay taxable.

The exemption has edges. Profits of any part of the business that consists of making investments are left out of the relevant amounts. United Kingdom land and United Kingdom property business profits are never exempted. A small company gets the exemption only for branches in full treaty territories. Chargeable gains are generally inside the exempt amounts, but a close company's gains are excluded, unless they arise on assets used solely for the branch's trade. Shipping and air transport in international traffic is outside the regime.

Two consequences follow from the International Act. Foreign tax on exempt branch profits is not creditable at all, because there is no United Kingdom tax for it to offset. And the new exemption from transfer pricing between United Kingdom companies, which chapter twenty seven explains, does not apply where branch exemption adjustments are made in relation to the activities.

The election is all or nothing. It covers every foreign permanent establishment the company has, now and in the future. It cannot be made branch by branch. A group that wants different answers for different branches has to put them in different companies.

Timing is everything. Under section eighteen F, the election applies to every accounting period beginning on or after the relevant day. For a United Kingdom resident company, H M R C's manual explains, the relevant day is the day the company's next accounting period is expected to begin when the election is made. So an election never reaches back into the period in which it is made. It starts with the next one.

Until that day arrives, the company that made the election can revoke it. After that, it is irrevocable. It comes to an end only if the company stops being resident in the United Kingdom. The manual adds that if the company later changes its accounting date, the election still takes effect from the original day.

That design makes the election a forecast. The board has to decide, before a period begins, what its branches will do in all the periods to come. A company that elects while a branch is profitable gives up relief for every future branch loss, in every country, forever. That is the misconception this chapter has to correct. People say that electing is obvious once a branch is profitable. It is not. It is an irrevocable bet on the whole future pattern of the company's overseas business, and it is made in advance.

The opening negative amount.

Suppose a company has had branch losses relieved in the United Kingdom, and then elects just as the branch turns profitable. Without a further rule, it would have the best of both worlds. Losses relieved here when they arose, and profits exempt when they came. Parliament closed that door with the transitional matching rules in sections eighteen J to eighteen N.

H M R C's manual describes the test. Look back over the six years before the first period the election covers. Add up the net losses of all the company's foreign permanent establishments, net of their profits. If there are net losses that later profits have not already absorbed, they form the total opening negative amount. Chargeable gains and losses are left out of this look-back.

A simple labelled example shows the arithmetic. Suppose a company has had two branches in the six years, in two countries we will call A and B. Country A's branch lost five hundred thousand pounds in total, and country B's branch made two hundred thousand. The net loss is three hundred thousand pounds, and that is the opening negative amount.

Then the matching begins. Until the opening negative amount has been fully matched by later branch profits, those profits are not exempt. They stay inside corporation tax, with credit for foreign tax in the usual way. Only once the negative amount is used up does the exemption start to work.

The Living Law gave an image for this. It is like paying back the rent rebate you enjoyed while the spare room stood empty. You had relief while the room cost you money. Before its income becomes yours tax free, you give that relief back.

There is one refinement. By default the negative amount is a single pool across all the company's branches, so a profitable branch in one country can be held in charge by old losses in another. A streaming election lets the company attach the negative amount to the territory that produced it. The statute says the company must specify, in its company tax return for the first period the election covers, how much of the amount it streams to each territory. Streamed amounts are then matched only against that territory's profits.

Calder's decision.

Return to Calder. When the refinery contract was signed in Group Year Two, the board compared a branch with a Vallarian subsidiary, and chose the branch. We will see why shortly. It did not make the exemption election then. The forecast showed early losses, the project was a single contract, and Calder's engineers expected more installation work abroad as their new valve technology sold. An election would have bound every one of those future branches.

In the summer of Group Year Three, with the profit now clear, Calder's finance director, Graham Pike, asked Tom Hesketh, the group head of tax, whether it was too late. It was. Calder was by then in its nine-month period to the thirty first of December. An election made in that period would take effect only from the first of January, Group Year Four. The project would be finished by then. The election would exempt nothing, and it would still bind Calder's future branches. Calder did not elect.

Was the board wrong not to elect a year earlier? The full working is in the reading edition, but the answer is short. Had Calder elected during its period to the thirty first of March, Group Year Three, the relevant day would have been the first of April. The six-year look-back would have found the three hundred thousand pound loss. So the first three hundred thousand pounds of the nine hundred thousand pound profit would have stayed taxable, with credit. Only six hundred thousand pounds would have been exempt. The top-up tax would have fallen from forty five thousand pounds to fifteen thousand. A saving of thirty thousand pounds.

Against that, Calder would have given up relief for every future branch loss, at twenty five per cent, in every country. For a company about to export a new technology, often through installation projects that start with losses, thirty thousand pounds was a small prize for a large and permanent commitment. The examiners like this kind of judgement, and they want it stated as a recommendation.

Guard rails.

An exemption invites diversion. A company could move mobile income, such as interest or royalties, into a branch in a low-tax country and exempt it. The answer is an anti-diversion rule in sections eighteen G to eighteen I. Since the controlled foreign company reforms, H M R C's manual explains, it is aligned with those rules. Imagine the branch were a controlled foreign company. Profits that would then be caught as diverted are not exempt, unless the branch meets one of the entity exemptions adapted from the controlled foreign company rules. Chapter twenty six teaches those exemptions in full. The manual adds that the test looks at each accounting period separately, so a branch can fall foul of it in one year and pass in the next. Periods before the controlled foreign company reforms used an older motive test, which you will not need for the examination.

Other links run the same way. An asset used in an exempt branch cannot be passed on tax neutrally to another group company under the intangibles group rules, as chapter eleven explained. The Finance Act twenty twenty six also allows regulations to create reporting rules for international controlled transactions. Those regulations, not yet made, can reach transactions relevant to branch exemption adjustments.

And there is the proposal we began with. The draft legislation of July twenty twenty six would make the exemption compulsory for accounting periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty seven. There would be exceptions for United Kingdom property businesses and investment business. It would repeal the opening negative amount rules and replace them with transitional rules restricting the carry forward of losses attributable to foreign branches. Commentators report that the anti-avoidance rules would apply to arrangements made on or after the thirteenth of July, twenty twenty six. All of this is proposed, not law. It does not apply to the twenty twenty seven examinations, which test the Finance Act twenty twenty six. But if you meet a real branch in practice next year, check where that Bill has got to.

The subsidiary route.

Now the separate flat. A foreign subsidiary pays foreign tax on its own profits, and the United Kingdom leaves those profits alone. Its losses stay with it, for the foreign country's rules to relieve. Its dealings with the United Kingdom parent must be priced at arm's length, under the transfer pricing rules of chapter twenty seven. And the controlled foreign company rules of chapter twenty six stand guard over profits diverted into it.

Money comes home mainly as dividends, and dividends are where the territorial system shows its hand. Part nine A of the two thousand and nine Act charges distributions received by a company, and then exempts most of them. Take a large or medium company, like those in Tarnmoor. Its dividend is exempt if it falls into an exempt class, is not one of the distributions that really stand in for interest, and is not deductible for the payer abroad.

There are five exempt classes, and one is enough. Distributions from controlled companies. Distributions on non-redeemable ordinary shares. Distributions from portfolio holdings of less than ten per cent. Distributions from transactions not designed to reduce tax. And distributions on shares accounted for as liabilities. The controlled company class has a joint venture limb. A company that controls the payer together with one other person is inside it if it holds at least forty per cent. The other person must hold at least forty but not more than fifty five per cent.

Anti-avoidance rules stop the classes being manufactured. For example, the controlled company class can be switched off where a scheme is designed to bring profits that arose before control within it. And one rule deserves a box of its own. If the paying company gets a tax deduction abroad for the dividend, it is not exempt, and the International Act also denies credit for the underlying tax. Only any withholding tax can be credited.

A small company has a different and simpler test. Its dividend is exempt if the payer is resident only in the United Kingdom or in a qualifying territory, broadly one with a suitable double tax treaty. The distribution must also not be interest-like, not be deductible abroad, and not be part of a tax advantage scheme. Large groups rarely meet the small company definition, so the exempt classes are the test that matters for them.

Tarnmoor Vallaria, the group's Vallarian subsidiary, is wholly owned by Tarnmoor P L C. Its dividends are exempt under the controlled company class. But Vallaria withholds fifteen per cent from dividends under the invented treaty. Suppose, for this example, that Tarnmoor Vallaria pays a dividend of ten million pounds. Vallaria keeps one point five million pounds. Because the dividend is exempt, there is no United Kingdom tax for that withholding to be credited against. It is a pure cost.

A company can choose to have a dividend taxed instead. The election must be made within two years after the end of the period in which the dividend is received. Why would anyone volunteer for tax? Typically because a foreign country gives its lower treaty withholding rate only if the dividend is taxable in the recipient's hands, or because credit for foreign taxes would cover the United Kingdom tax anyway. On Vallarian numbers it achieves nothing. The underlying tax at twenty per cent and withholding at fifteen per cent together exceed the United Kingdom's twenty five per cent. So the credit wipes out the United Kingdom tax, and part of the withholding is still lost.

Branch or subsidiary.

Back to Calder's board in Group Year Two, choosing how to carry out the refinery contract. Tom Hesketh's paper ran the two routes side by side, on the same simplification that Vallaria taxes each period's profit at twenty per cent.

As a branch, the result was the one we have seen. One hundred and fifty thousand pounds of tax over the project's life, because the United Kingdom relieved the first year's loss at once and topped up the second year's profit to twenty five per cent.

As a Vallarian subsidiary, the three hundred thousand pound loss would have stayed in Vallaria, with no United Kingdom relief. The subsidiary would pay one hundred and eighty thousand pounds of Vallarian tax on its nine hundred thousand pound profit. Paying the remaining seven hundred and twenty thousand pounds home as a dividend would cost a further one hundred and eight thousand pounds of withholding, a total of two hundred and eighty eight thousand pounds. The dividend itself would be exempt in Calder's hands. The controlled foreign company rules would not bite, because Vallarian tax at twenty per cent is eighty per cent of the United Kingdom equivalent, comfortably above the seventy five per cent the tax exemption requires.

For a short project that starts with a loss, the branch won. The general lesson is subtler. Over a long life, a profitable subsidiary in a lower-tax country keeps its lower rate, provided its profits are reinvested locally or come home without much withholding. A branch without an election is always topped up to the United Kingdom rate. The branch wins early, while losses need relief. The subsidiary, or an elected branch, wins later, when profits are steady.

Tax is not the whole table. A subsidiary ring-fences legal liability, which matters on a refinery contract. It may be needed for local licences, or because customers want a local counterparty. It needs its own board, managed abroad, so that its residence stays abroad. Both routes need arm's length pricing, in one case as transfer pricing and in the other as the attribution of profit to a permanent establishment. And both are measured in Vallaria's jurisdiction under the Pillar Two rules of chapter thirty.

The examiners set exactly this choice in May twenty twenty six, a twenty mark question about a construction company's first overseas contract. They reported that many candidates wrongly discussed the residence or migration of the United Kingdom company. The best answers covered the permanent establishment thresholds, the exemption election and its timing for losses, and the overseas rate. They also covered the subsidiary, through central management and control and the controlled foreign company rules.

Incorporating a branch.

A branch that succeeds often grows into a subsidiary. Transferring the branch's business to a new foreign company is a disposal of its assets, and the gains could be large. Section one four oh of the Gains Act offers postponement.

The conditions are precise. A United Kingdom resident company carries on a trade outside the United Kingdom through a permanent establishment. It transfers that trade, with all its assets or all except cash, to a company not resident in the United Kingdom. The consideration is wholly or partly shares, or shares and loan stock, in the transferee. And after the transfer the transferor holds at least twenty five per cent of the transferee's ordinary share capital. On a claim, the net gains on the transferred assets are postponed, in the proportion that the securities bear to the whole consideration.

Suppose, for this example, that the gains on a branch's assets are one million pounds, and the new company pays three million pounds in shares and one million pounds in cash. Three quarters of the consideration is shares. So seven hundred and fifty thousand pounds of gain is postponed, and two hundred and fifty thousand pounds is chargeable now, sixty two thousand five hundred pounds of tax at twenty five per cent.

The postponed gain comes back into charge in two ways. When the transferor disposes of the shares, at any time, the postponed gain is added to its proceeds. And if the transferee disposes of the transferred assets within six years, the corresponding part of the gain comes back at once. The examiners noted, after the November twenty twenty five sitting, that few candidates saw that an incorporation gain could be postponed at all.

Two points of judgement go with it. First, if the company has already elected for the branch exemption, gains on the branch's assets generally fall within the exempt amounts. So the relief matters most for branches that were never exempt, or for close companies. Second, the new subsidiary is now a foreign company controlled from the United Kingdom, so the controlled foreign company rules come with it, along with capital allowance balancing adjustments and any realisation of intangible assets.

The Gains Act also keeps a family of reliefs born in European law, in sections one four oh A to one four oh L. They are non-core. Since the end of twenty twenty, regulations made for leaving the European Union have reworded some of them around a relevant state, defined as the United Kingdom or a member state. One relief gives no gain, no loss treatment where a United Kingdom business passes between companies resident in different relevant states wholly for shares. Another, section one four oh C, covers a United Kingdom company transferring a business carried on through a permanent establishment in a member state to a company resident in a member state. Gains and losses are netted, and notional double tax relief may be available for foreign tax that the transfer did not actually bear. Each has a commercial purpose test. Vallaria is not a member state, so none of them helps Calder.

Unremittable income.

Sometimes the money is earned but cannot come home. A government imposes exchange controls, or its central bank runs out of hard currency. Taxing a company on income it cannot touch would be harsh, so the law gives relief. There are two separate reliefs, and choosing the right one is the first mark.

For trading receipts, the relief is in the trading income rules of the two thousand and nine Act, in sections one hundred and seventy three to one hundred and seventy five. H M R C's manual explains that a debt blocked by exchange controls is not a bad or doubtful debt. Instead, an unremittable amount brought into account as a receipt is deducted from trading profits, but not so as to create a loss. Any excess is carried forward. The relief is withdrawn, and the amount treated as a trading receipt, in several cases. They include the amount ceasing to be unremittable, being exchanged for a remittable amount, or being used to finance expenditure or investment abroad.

For other income arising outside the United Kingdom, such as foreign interest, royalties or a taxable dividend, the relief is in Part eighteen of the same Act, from section twelve seventy four. Two conditions must be met. The income cannot be transferred to the United Kingdom because of the local law, executive action by the foreign government, or the impossibility of obtaining transferable currency. And the company has not realised it abroad for an amount it could transfer. The company must claim within two years after the end of the accounting period in which the income arises. The income then stays out of account until it ceases to be unremittable, and comes back into charge then, under the valuation rules that follow. The controlled foreign company rules carry their own version of this relief.

Here is a labelled example. Suppose, in Group Year Six, a Marrovian customer of Tarnmoor Engineering pays four hundred thousand pounds for pumps into a local bank account, and Marrovia's new exchange controls stop it leaving. Marrovia is invented. The sale is a receipt of Tarnmoor Engineering's United Kingdom trade, so the relief is the trading one, not a Part eighteen claim. The four hundred thousand pounds is deducted from its Group Year Six profits. When the controls lift in Group Year Seven and the money is converted, the relief is withdrawn and the amount is taxed then. The deferral is worth the time value of one hundred thousand pounds of tax for a year.

International movements of capital.

The last topic is a piece of history that became a reporting duty. Until two thousand and nine, section seven six five of the Income and Corporation Taxes Act nineteen eighty eight required Treasury consent for certain capital movements. For example, a United Kingdom company needed consent before letting a foreign subsidiary issue shares or debentures. The rule dated from an age of exchange control. The Finance Act two thousand and nine repealed it, and its section thirty seven and Schedule seventeen replaced consent with reporting, for events on or after the first of July, two thousand and nine.

The duty falls on a reporting body. That is a United Kingdom corporate parent, a United Kingdom resident company that controls one or more foreign companies and is not itself controlled by a United Kingdom resident company. Where a foreign-controlled group has several United Kingdom parents, rules decide which one reports. A reportable event or transaction must have a value of more than one hundred million pounds. Examples are an issue of shares or debentures by a foreign subsidiary, or a transfer of shares or debentures in one. Ordinary trading transactions are excluded, and so are transactions where all the parties are resident in the same territory. The report goes to H M R C within six months, so that it can consider whether a tax advantage results. Failure is penalised under the special returns penalty in section ninety eight of the Taxes Management Act nineteen seventy. The regime is narrow, and that is the point. It catches the large, unusual capital movement that H M R C would want to see, and leaves the daily business of a multinational alone.

In a labelled example, suppose Tarnmoor Vallaria issues one hundred and twenty million pounds of new shares to Tarnmoor P L C to fund a new plant. Tarnmoor P L C is a reporting body. The issue exceeds one hundred million pounds, and the parties are in different territories. So Tarnmoor P L C must report it within six months.

Going further.

A good tax function treats outbound structure as a running decision, not a one-off. It keeps a register of every foreign permanent establishment, including construction sites and dependent agents that may create one, because the exemption election, if made, would capture all of them. It models the election before each accounting period begins, since it can only ever take effect from the next one. If an election is made, it tracks the opening negative amount and makes any streaming election in the first return.

It plans repatriation. Dividends from a subsidiary are usually exempt, but withholding on them is a cost. Interest, royalties and management charges are deductible abroad and taxable at home, with credit for any withholding, and they must be at arm's length. In our invented case, Tarnmoor Vallaria pays interest to the group's finance company, a royalty to Calder and a management charge to Tarnmoor P L C, and it can pay dividends too.

It also remembers that the choice belongs to a company, not to a group. A group that wants its loss-making ventures inside the United Kingdom computation and its mature branches exempt can hold them in different United Kingdom companies, one elected and one not. Some years later in our invented story, Tarnmoor buys Jess Hartley's cycle business, which has a showroom branch in Dublin, and the election question comes back in a new form. Chapter thirty one tells that story.

It watches deadlines. The election to tax a dividend, and a Part eighteen claim for unremittable income, each within two years after the period ends. A capital movement report within six months. And it keeps an eye on the compulsory exemption in the draft Bill, which would turn every branch decision in this chapter into a question of transitional loss rules.

Finally, it records the reasoning. A decision not to elect, like Calder's, should be minuted with the forecast behind it, so that a later finance director knows why the company is where it is. A decision to elect should come with a schedule of the opening negative amount and of every territory in which the company might later have a permanent establishment. Neither decision is an avoidance question. Both elections are offered by Parliament, and using them as intended is ordinary tax management.

How the examiner tests it.

This material appears often, usually inside a larger international question. In May twenty twenty three, a fifteen mark question asked whether a company should keep its overseas branches taxable, elect for exemption, or incorporate them, and asked for a recommendation. The examiners reported that some candidates failed to give one, and lost marks. In November twenty twenty five, a fifteen mark question tested a permanent establishment analysis and then credit relief, the election and incorporation. In May twenty twenty six came the twenty mark branch or subsidiary question.

The traps repeat. Treating the election as branch by branch. Forgetting that it starts only with the next accounting period, and cannot be undone after that. Missing the opening negative amount. Crediting foreign tax on exempt profits. Discussing residence when the question is about a permanent establishment. And failing to recommend.

The grades are worth knowing. Overseas branches and subsidiaries, foreign dividends and unremittable income are core, and the examiner can build a whole question around them. The cross-border transfer reliefs in the Gains Act and international movements of capital are non-core, so expect a paragraph's worth of marks rather than a computation.

The style is technical prose with short computations. A good answer to a branch question sets out the three options in turn. Taxable branch, with credit relief and the timing of loss relief. Elected branch, with its irrevocability, its coverage of every permanent establishment, and the opening negative amount. Subsidiary, with residence kept abroad, the dividend exemption, withholding as a cost and the controlled foreign company test. Then it compares the tax cost of each over the period the question gives, and ends with a recommendation that names the option and the main reason. Allow about two minutes a mark, so roughly forty minutes for a twenty mark question of this kind.

What to take away.

A United Kingdom company is taxed on its profits wherever they arise, so a foreign branch is taxed here unless the company elects otherwise. Without an election, branch losses are relieved at once, and branch profits are taxed at twenty five per cent with credit for foreign tax, capped at the United Kingdom tax on that income. The branch's net result is taxed at the United Kingdom rate.

The branch exemption election in section eighteen A removes the profits and the losses of every foreign permanent establishment, territory by territory, measured under the treaty or the O E C D Model. It covers all branches, present and future. It applies from the relevant day, the start of the next accounting period, can be revoked only before then, and is irrevocable afterwards. Foreign tax on exempt profits cannot be credited. Small companies get it only in full treaty territories, close companies' gains are mostly outside it, and an anti-diversion rule shaped like the controlled foreign company rules protects it.

The opening negative amount looks back six years. Net branch losses must be matched by later branch profits before the exemption starts, and a streaming election ties the matching to each territory.

In our invented case, Calder's Vallarian installation branch lost three hundred thousand pounds, then made nine hundred thousand. Vallarian tax one hundred and eighty thousand pounds, United Kingdom tax two hundred and twenty five thousand, top-up forty five thousand. Calder did not elect, because by the time the profit was clear the election could only start after the project ended. With hindsight, an earlier election would have saved thirty thousand pounds, at the price of binding every future branch.

A subsidiary keeps its own profits, losses and rate. Its dividends are exempt if they fall into one of five classes, including controlled companies and the forty fifty five joint venture limb, unless deductible abroad. Withholding on an exempt dividend is a pure cost. The election to tax a dividend must be made within two years.

Incorporating a branch can postpone gains under section one four oh, if the transferor ends with at least twenty five per cent, in proportion to the shares received. The gain returns when the shares are sold, or if the assets are sold within six years.

Unremittable trading receipts are deducted under the trading rules. Other unremittable foreign income needs a Part eighteen claim within two years. And a United Kingdom parent must report foreign subsidiaries' share or debenture transactions over one hundred million pounds within six months.

So, branch or subsidiary? A branch while losses need relief, a subsidiary or an elected branch when profits are steady, and a recommendation either way. The answer may become compulsory one day, but under the Finance Act twenty twenty six it is still a choice.

Once a subsidiary exists, the United Kingdom still watches what it earns. Chapter twenty six, on controlled foreign companies, explains how.

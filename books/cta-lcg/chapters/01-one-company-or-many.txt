Part One: The Larger Company.

Chapter one: One company or many?

On the first of April, at the start of what our story will call Group Year One, a regional engineering business owned by one family stopped being a family firm. Calder Valve Engineering Limited woke up that morning as it had gone to bed. Same works, same three hundred staff, same order book. By lunchtime its shares belonged to Tarnmoor, an invented listed engineering group, which had paid thirty two million pounds for them.

Listeners to The Living Law have met Calder before. It is invented too, like every company and person in Tarnmoor's story. In The Living Law, chapter five, Calder was a company on its own, with no associated companies. It had taxable total profits of two million pounds and a corporation tax bill of five hundred thousand pounds, paid in four quarterly instalments of one hundred and twenty five thousand pounds. One company, one return, one bill.

Nothing in the purchase changed Calder's legal identity. It still computes its own profits, files its own return and owes its own tax. And yet, from that first of April, almost every number around it moves. The point at which it must pay tax early moves. The rules on how it prices its dealings with sister companies switch on. A board in Leeds now answers for its tax governance. Its interest costs are tested against a group it has never met.

So here is the question this chapter answers. When a company joins a large group, is it still one taxpayer among many, or has it become a small part of one large taxpayer? The short answer is both, and the skill of this paper lies in knowing which rule takes which view.

This is the first chapter of Part One, on the larger company. It sets up the whole book. It builds on The Living Law, chapter twenty, on the foundations of corporation tax, and chapter twenty four, on groups. No single syllabus row belongs to it. Instead it gives you two tools you will use in every later chapter, a ladder of size thresholds and a family of group definitions, and it introduces the invented group whose story runs through the book.

Meet Tarnmoor.

Tarnmoor plc is our invented parent company. It is incorporated in England, its head office is in Leeds, and its shares are listed on the main market of the London Stock Exchange. Nobody controls it. Its shareholders are pension funds, insurers and thousands of private investors. That shape is deliberate. The Chartered Institute of Taxation describes this paper in these words. Scenarios will typically be based around multinational groups and larger companies that may be listed, with no individual controlling shareholders. Tarnmoor is exactly that kind of group.

Under the parent sit the companies you will come to know. Tarnmoor Engineering Limited is the main trading company, making industrial valves, actuators and pumps. In Group Year One it sells six hundred and thirty million pounds of them. Tarnmoor Estates holds the group's property. Tarnmoor Finance is the treasury company, with five hundred and fifty million pounds of listed notes paying five and a half per cent. Tarnmoor Water Systems runs a water infrastructure business. There is a dormant company, Tarnmoor Pumps, that does nothing at all, and it will matter precisely because it does nothing.

Abroad, three companies carry the group across borders. Tarnmoor Vallaria makes and distributes valves in Vallaria. Tarnmoor Capital is a finance company in Marrovia. Both countries are invented, carried over from The Living Law. Tarnmoor Ireland is incorporated in Ireland, a real country, but its directors meet in Leeds, so for now it is resident in the United Kingdom. Finally there is Helmside Energy, a hydrogen joint venture formed on the first of January of Group Year One. Tarnmoor holds forty five per cent, an invented utility called Greyfell holds forty, and an invented infrastructure fund called Northlight holds fifteen.

Every company in the group draws up accounts to the thirty first of December, except Calder, which keeps its thirty first of March year end for now. Group Years are Tarnmoor's accounting years. We never attach a calendar year to them, and we run every computation on the law for the financial year twenty twenty six, whatever Group Year the story has reached.

In Group Year One, consolidated revenue is one thousand one hundred and eighty million pounds, and the group employs about six thousand people. Three invented people will guide us. Nadia Kerr is Tarnmoor's chief financial officer. Tom Hesketh is the group head of tax, and his team is where the book's questions land. Graham Pike is Calder's finance director, before and after the deal.

And one more person. Dan Hartley, a chartered engineer from The Living Law, works in Calder's process valves division. Listeners to chapters eight to ten of that book followed his commute, his benefits and his pay, and by now his long secondment to a client's plant on Teesside is over. They also know how his time at Calder ends. This book will show the company's side of that story. For now, Dan is about to be asked to give most of his time to a new research project.

Why one company stays one taxpayer.

The Living Law, chapter twenty, told how a company became a person in law, separate from those who own it. The leading case is Salomon versus A Salomon and Company Limited, decided by the House of Lords and reported in eighteen ninety seven. The Lords held that a properly formed company is a legal person distinct from its members, however its shares are held. Corporation tax, created by the Finance Act nineteen sixty five, was built on that foundation. The taxpayer is the company.

This is the separate entity principle. Each company in a group computes its own taxable total profits, for its own accounting periods, on its own return. Each pays its own tax. Each has its own losses, its own capital allowance pools, its own base costs in its own assets. If Tarnmoor Estates sells a building to Tarnmoor Engineering, two taxpayers have dealt with each other.

Why does the law insist on this, when a group plainly runs as one business? There are good reasons. Company law gives each company its own assets and debts, and its creditors look only to it. Groups change shape constantly, buying and selling companies, and a tax system based on the separate company follows those changes without reopening a consolidated position. Minority shareholders, like Greyfell and Northlight in Helmside, need each company's profit to be its own. And when a company is sold, the buyer inherits its tax history, which only makes sense if that history belongs to the company.

But the principle has costs. A loss in one company and a profit in another would both be taxed as if strangers held them. An asset moved between sisters would trigger a gain on paper, though nothing has left the group. So Parliament has built a toolkit of exceptions. Group relief lets current losses move between companies. Assets move between members of a gains group at no gain, no loss. Roll-over relief looks across the group. Stamp duty and stamp duty land tax spare transfers between companies under common ownership.

Notice the pattern. The United Kingdom never abandons the separate entity. It bridges it, rule by rule, and each bridge has its own conditions. Only in its newest rules, the corporate interest restriction and the global minimum tax, does the law begin to look at the group as a single economic unit. That tension, between many companies and one business, is the first of this book's great debates, and it returns in chapters fifteen, twenty eight and thirty.

The group's tax charge is not its tax bill.

Here is a misconception worth killing early. In The Living Law, chapter twenty four, the myth was that a group pays tax as one. A trainee at a large firm rarely believes that in its simple form. The grown-up version is subtler. It says that the tax charge in the consolidated accounts is the group's tax bill.

People believe it because it is the number they see. Tarnmoor's annual report shows one income tax expense in one income statement, and analysts quote the effective tax rate that comes from it. Boards are measured on it.

But that consolidated charge is an accounting figure. It adds up current tax across every company in the group, United Kingdom and overseas, so it includes Vallarian and Marrovian tax that H M R C never sees. It includes deferred tax, which is not a payment at all but a recognition of tax that will fall due, or be saved, in later years. Chapter six explains it. It reflects consolidation adjustments that no tax return contains.

The United Kingdom tax bill is something else entirely. It is a set of separate liabilities, company by company. Each is computed on that company's own taxable total profits and paid on that company's own timetable. In Group Year One, Tarnmoor Engineering's liability, after group and consortium relief, comes to about two point four three million pounds. Calder, in its first accounting period in the group, computes its own liability of seven hundred and fifty thousand pounds before setting off its research credit. Nobody adds these together to file one return, because there is no group return for corporation tax.

There is a second reason the two numbers differ. The accounts measure profit under accounting standards, and tax law then adjusts that profit, adding back some costs, deducting others, and replacing depreciation with capital allowances. Some of those differences reverse in later years and some never do. The consolidated charge captures the reversing ones as deferred tax. The return simply taxes this year's adjusted profit, and has no place for tax that belongs to other years.

The practical lesson is that a tax function reconciles. It explains the consolidated charge to the board, the auditors and the market, and it builds up the company returns underneath it, and it must make the two stories agree. When an examiner asks for the tax charge in the accounts, that is a different question from what tax is payable, and a different answer. The examiners have been asking it more often, as a tail to a computation question, and candidates who confuse the two lose easy marks.

House rules with their own guest lists.

The Living Law, chapter twenty four, pictured a group as a family of separate wallets living under one roof, with house rules about when money can pass between them. Here is the extension this book needs. Each house rule has its own guest list. Who counts as family depends on which rule you are applying. A cousin may be welcome at one table and turned away from the next.

Take the lists one at a time. Start with associated companies. Under the Corporation Tax Act twenty ten, which I will call the twenty ten Act, two companies are associated if one controls the other or both are under common control. Control here is a broad test of power over the company's affairs. Overseas companies count. A company that carries on no trade or business, such as a dormant one, is ignored, and so is a passive holding company that does nothing but hold shares and pass dividends up. The number of associated companies divides the marginal relief limits and, under H M R C's guidance and the instalment regulations, the thresholds for paying tax by instalments.

Next, the fifty one per cent group, built on owning more than half of a company's ordinary share capital, directly or indirectly. It defines who may join a group payment arrangement, the group for the substantial shareholding exemption, the group from which H M R C can recover unpaid tax on a gain, and the groups whose size is measured for uncertain tax treatment and for publishing a tax strategy.

Then the seventy five per cent group for group relief. The parent must own at least three quarters of the ordinary share capital, and also be entitled to at least three quarters of the profits available for distribution and of the assets on a winding up. Losses can pass only between companies within the charge to United Kingdom tax. Alongside it sits the consortium, where companies each holding at least five per cent together own at least three quarters of a trading or holding company.

The gains group, under the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act nineteen ninety two, which I will call the Gains Act, uses seventy five per cent subsidiaries again, but adds a twist. Every member must also be an effective fifty one per cent subsidiary of the principal company, judged on profits and assets all the way down the chain. Overseas companies can be members, though the reliefs mostly bite only on assets within the United Kingdom charge.

Stamp duty and stamp duty land tax use their own seventy five per cent tests of shares, profits and assets, with extra conditions aimed at arrangements for the buyer to leave the group.

Now the wide lists. The corporate interest restriction, in the Taxation, International and Other Provisions, Act twenty ten, which I will call the International Act, works on the worldwide group. That is the ultimate parent and every subsidiary it consolidates in its accounts under international accounting standards. Pillar Two, the global minimum tax, uses a similar consolidated group. The country-by-country reporting regulations count the whole consolidated group too.

Finally, the control tests for cross-border rules. Transfer pricing applies where one party participates in the management, control or capital of the other. The controlled foreign company rules ask whether a United Kingdom company controls an overseas one. Chapters twenty six and twenty seven take these apart.

The reading edition sets all of this out in one matrix. Learn it as a matrix, because the exam rarely names the test. It describes a shareholding and expects you to see which lists it puts a company on.

Applying the guest lists to Tarnmoor.

Run Tarnmoor through the lists in Group Year One and the family changes shape before your eyes.

Count the associated companies first. Tarnmoor plc controls everything it owns outright, so it, Tarnmoor Engineering, Estates, Finance, Water Systems, Vallaria, Capital and Ireland are all associated, and Calder joins them on the first of April. That makes nine. The two overseas companies count, because association looks at control, not residence. Tarnmoor Pumps does not count, because it is dormant. Helmside does not count, because forty five per cent is not control, and no single shareholder controls it. So each company's thresholds are divided by nine, the company itself plus its eight associates.

Now group relief. Every company that Tarnmoor owns outright passes the seventy five per cent tests. But group relief moves losses only between companies within the charge to United Kingdom corporation tax. Tarnmoor Vallaria and Tarnmoor Capital are outside it, so their losses stay abroad. Tarnmoor Ireland, though incorporated in Ireland, is resident here while its board meets in Leeds, so it can surrender and claim like any other United Kingdom company. Helmside is not in the group relief group at all. It is a consortium company, and its losses flow in proportion to the members' holdings. Chapter fifteen works through the numbers.

The gains group is almost the same list, overseas companies included, with the principal company at the top. The worldwide group for the corporate interest restriction is larger still. It is Tarnmoor plc and every subsidiary in its consolidated accounts, including Vallaria, Marrovia and the dormant Pumps company. Helmside sits outside it, as a joint venture that Tarnmoor does not consolidate.

So which is the real Tarnmoor group? The question has no single answer, and that is the lesson. There is a nine-company association, a United Kingdom group relief group, a gains group, a stamp duty group, a fifty one per cent group and a worldwide group. A good adviser carries all of them in mind, and asks of every transaction which list matters. Move a building from Estates to Engineering, and the gains group and the stamp duty land tax group matter. Lend money from Finance to Vallaria, and the control tests for transfer pricing matter. Borrow from a bank, and the worldwide group matters.

Calder's purchase shows why. On the first of April it joins every list at once. It becomes associated, a member of the group relief and gains groups, part of the worldwide group, and a company controlled by a listed parent. Each membership switches on a different consequence, and the rest of this chapter is about the ones that come from size.

What makes a company larger, the threshold ladder.

The paper's title speaks of larger companies, and this book uses larger company as a working term, not a legal one. No statute defines it. Instead, dozens of rules each draw their own line, and a company or group above the line meets extra obligations.

Why should size matter at all? Three reasons recur. The first is revenue. A small number of large businesses pay a large share of corporation tax, so the state wants their money sooner and their risks visible. The second is capability. A group with a tax function, auditors and systems can carry burdens that would crush a small company, so Parliament places reporting and governance duties where they can be borne. The third is international. Large groups can move profit across borders. Working through the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, governments have agreed common lines, most famously seven hundred and fifty million euros of revenue, above which shared reporting and the global minimum tax apply.

There is a cost to every line. A threshold is a cliff edge. One pound or one euro either side of it changes a company's obligations completely, and groups cross thresholds by accident, through acquisitions, mergers, currency movements or a single good year. That is why a tax function watches them, and why this chapter fixes them in one place.

Picture a ladder. A company starts at the bottom with the ordinary obligations every company has. Each rung it climbs switches on something more. The rungs come from different statutes and use different measures, profits on some, interest on others, turnover or revenue on the rest, so climb carefully. The reading edition sets them out in a single table.

The first rung is about paying early. A company is large for the instalment rules if its profits exceed one and a half million pounds, and very large if they exceed twenty million pounds. H M R C's guidance says both figures are divided by the number of associated companies, counting the company itself. That has been the rule since the associated company rules returned in twenty twenty three. Both figures are also reduced for short periods. A large company pays in months seven, ten, thirteen and sixteen, counted from the start of the period. A very large company pays in months three, six, nine and twelve, all inside the period. Chapter three owns the detail, including the first-year exception for large companies, which very large companies do not get.

The second rung is interest. The corporate interest restriction can bite only where a worldwide group's net interest expense exceeds two million pounds a year. Below that, there is no restriction.

The third rung is losses. Carried forward losses can be set without limit against the first five million pounds of profit each year, the deductions allowance, with only half of the profit above it available. A group shares one allowance.

The fourth rung is two hundred million pounds of turnover, or two billion pounds of balance sheet total. Above it, a United Kingdom incorporated company must have a senior accounting officer who certifies its tax accounting arrangements. The group must publish a tax strategy. And the group must tell H M R C about uncertain tax treatments worth more than five million pounds. Chapter four covers all three. H M R C's Large Business directorate works with most businesses whose turnover is above that same line.

The fifth rung is seven hundred and fifty million euros of consolidated revenue. Above it, the group files a country-by-country report. Its United Kingdom companies must keep a master file and a local file of transfer pricing records. It must publish a tax strategy even if the fourth rung did not catch it. And if revenue exceeds that figure in at least two of the previous four years, Pillar Two's minimum tax applies.

One more line runs the other way. Small and medium-sized enterprises are exempt from most transfer pricing, and the test looks at the whole group of linked enterprises. Fewer than two hundred and fifty staff, and turnover no more than fifty million euros or a balance sheet no more than forty three million euros, keeps a company medium-sized. Join a big group, and the exemption is gone.

Tarnmoor on the ladder.

Now put Tarnmoor on the ladder, using our invented figures for Group Year One.

Consolidated revenue is one thousand one hundred and eighty million pounds. Our story assumes an exchange rate of one point one five euros to the pound, invented for illustration, which gives about one thousand three hundred and fifty seven million euros. That is far above seven hundred and fifty million euros, and it would be even at parity. So Tarnmoor files a country-by-country report, keeps transfer pricing master and local files, and, assuming as our story does that it has been this size for years, falls within Pillar Two.

United Kingdom turnover is far above two hundred million pounds. Tarnmoor Engineering alone sells six hundred and thirty million pounds. So the senior accounting officer rules, the tax strategy rules and uncertain tax treatment reporting all apply. Nadia Kerr, the chief financial officer, is the senior accounting officer for every United Kingdom incorporated company in the group. There is a subtlety even here. Tarnmoor Ireland is resident in the United Kingdom but incorporated in Ireland, and the senior accounting officer rules apply only to companies incorporated in the United Kingdom.

Interest is far above the two million pound de minimis. Tarnmoor Finance alone pays thirty point two five million pounds a year on its notes. Chapter twenty eight shows that in Group Year One the group loses relief for about two point two one million pounds of interest.

Now the instalment rung, company by company. With nine companies in the association, a divisor of nine, the very large threshold for each company is twenty million pounds divided by nine, about two point two two million pounds. The large threshold is about one hundred and sixty seven thousand pounds.

Watch what that does to Calder. On its own, with profits of two million pounds, Calder was large, because it was over one and a half million pounds, but nowhere near very large. It paid in months seven, ten, thirteen and sixteen. In its first group period, its taxable total profits are three million pounds. That is above two point two two million pounds, so Calder is now very large, and pays in months three, six, nine and twelve. Its profits rose, but on its own three million pounds is nowhere near twenty million. Its cash timetable moved four months earlier because it acquired eight associates.

And transfer pricing arrives. Calder, with three hundred staff, was too large to be a small or medium-sized enterprise even on its own. Many targets are not. The moment they join a group like Tarnmoor the exemption vanishes, because the size test looks through to the whole group. In the story, Calder's dealings with Tarnmoor Vallaria will be priced at arm's length, and tested. Chapter twenty seven tells that story. The full working for all of this is in the reading edition.

Who does the work.

A larger company's tax is not done by one person with a spreadsheet. It is done by a system, and the paper expects you to know the parts.

At the centre is the group tax function. In our invented case it is Tom Hesketh's team. It prepares or reviews every company's return, manages instalments, runs the transfer pricing documentation, prepares the interest restriction return and the tax numbers in the accounts, and keeps a map of the thresholds and group lists we have just built.

Above it sits the board, which approves the published tax strategy and owns the group's appetite for tax risk. The chief financial officer, Nadia Kerr in our story, is the senior accounting officer and personally certifies, each year, whether the tax accounting arrangements are appropriate. The audit committee and the external auditors test the tax numbers in the accounts.

Across the table sits H M R C. Its Large Business directorate works with around two thousand of the country's largest businesses. Each has a Customer Compliance Manager, a senior H M R C official who learns the business and coordinates H M R C's own specialists. H M R C's guidance rates each business's tax risk on a scale running from low to high. A good relationship means issues raised early and resolved in real time.

The work runs to a calendar. Instalments fall due through the year. The tax numbers for the annual report are prepared at the year end and audited. Returns follow within twelve months, and the interest restriction return with them. The senior accounting officer's certificate is due when the accounts are due to be filed, six months after the year end for a public company. Each deadline belongs to a particular company, and missing one costs penalties.

Finally, the specialists. Transfer pricing economists, valuers, research and development claim teams, stamp taxes lawyers, accountants who know international standards. Nobody holds all of it.

That is why the Chartered Institute frames this paper the way it does. Candidates, it says, are not expected to have deep knowledge of all tax areas but should be able to identify what the potential tax issues are. They should know specialist areas well enough to coordinate the work of the firm's specialists. Read that as a job description. The examiner rewards the adviser who sees every issue a transaction raises, says what it means, and knows who to call.

Mapping the thresholds every year.

The planning lesson of this chapter is unglamorous and vital. A tax function maps every threshold and every group list, every year, and again whenever the group changes shape.

Acquisitions are the usual trigger. A group just under seven hundred and fifty million euros can cross it by buying one company, and Pillar Two, country-by-country reporting and transfer pricing records follow. Each new subsidiary also changes the associated company count, which lowers every company's instalment thresholds. When Tarnmoor buys an invented sensor developer called Brackenwell in Group Year Two, the count rises to ten, and every company's very large threshold falls to two million pounds. Disposals and demergers work the other way. Currency can do it too. The highest line is drawn in euros, so a weaker pound can carry a sterling group across it without a single extra sale.

A joiner brings its own checklist. Its year end may differ from the group's, as Calder's does. Its returns and instalments need taking over. Its losses may be restricted after the change of ownership. It may lose a relief it enjoyed as a small company. It may have been paying corporation tax on one timetable and now, as Calder does, must pay on another, earlier one. If it was a small or medium-sized enterprise claiming the enhanced relief for research intensive companies, it loses that relief and falls back on the expenditure credit. Its directors may have been the only people answerable for its tax, and now a senior accounting officer in Leeds must certify its arrangements too. Chapters three, four, ten, fourteen and twenty pick up each of these.

There is a professional point too. Thresholds are measured on different numbers, from different accounts, over different periods. One rule looks at the previous financial year, another at the current accounting period, another at two of the last four. A careless adviser applies one test to all of them. The examiners have noticed. In May twenty twenty five, candidates asked about the tax strategy thresholds apportioned subsidiaries' figures instead of using the right accounts. A good tax function writes the tests down, with the source of each figure, and the senior accounting officer can rely on them. It also reviews the map whenever the deal team is busy, because the tax function is often the last to hear about a purchase and the first to be blamed for a missed deadline.

How the paper is built.

A brief word on the exam this book serves, because it shapes every chapter. Chapter thirty two owns the craft.

The paper is three and a half hours long and normally has six questions, each worth ten, fifteen or twenty marks, for a hundred marks in total. The pass mark is fifty. It was renamed in twenty twenty three, from Taxation of Major Corporates, a change that signals its focus on groups. Its syllabus covers corporation tax, including tax on chargeable gains, then capital allowances, and stamp duty and stamp duty land tax at awareness level. At least seventy per cent of the corporation tax element comes from core material.

Each syllabus row carries a grade. Core items are examined in depth, non-core items at working level, and awareness items for recognition. In the grid the book is built on, most rows are core, including transfer pricing, controlled foreign companies, the interest restriction and hybrids. When the twenty twenty seven grid appears, check the grades against it.

The scenarios follow a pattern. A United Kingdom listed group with overseas subsidiaries. A foreign parent buying a United Kingdom company. A joint venture. Expansion abroad by branch or subsidiary. A property reorganisation, a disposal or a company leaving the country. Every one of them is a story about crossing a threshold or joining a list.

The questions want technical prose and computations, not letters. They ask you to calculate with explanations, to explain, to discuss and sometimes to recommend. Marking guides award half a mark or a mark for each relevant point, so breadth of spotting matters as much as depth.

This chapter's material has been examined head on. In November twenty twenty four, a twenty mark question had an American multinational buy a United Kingdom company that had been a small or medium-sized enterprise. The marking guide gave marks for instalments now that the company sat in a large group, for country-by-country reporting, for transfer pricing switching on, for the interest restriction, and for stamp duty on the share purchase. That is the threshold ladder and the guest lists, applied to one acquisition. The examiners reported that most candidates found transfer pricing, but some wrote at length about it and missed easier points. Spot everything first, then go deep.

The pass rates are reassuring. Between May twenty twenty three and May twenty twenty six they ran from fifty nine to seventy four per cent, and the chief examiner reported after the May twenty twenty four sitting that Joint Programme candidates had the highest pass rates. That is no reason for complacency. Particular questions, on controlled foreign companies and on group relief, have been answered badly. With three and a half hours for a hundred marks, you have a little over two minutes a mark, and spotting the issues quickly is what buys the time.

What to take away.

A company that joins a large group is still a separate taxpayer. Salomon's case, decided by the House of Lords and reported in eighteen ninety seven, confirmed that a company is a person distinct from its members, and corporation tax taxes that person. Each company computes, files and pays its own tax. There is no group return for corporation tax.

The law bridges the gap between the many companies and the one business with a toolkit of group regimes. Each regime has its own guest list. Associated companies turn on control and include overseas companies, but ignore dormant and passive holding companies. The fifty one per cent group governs group payment arrangements, the shareholding exemption, recovery of tax on gains, and the tax strategy and uncertain tax treatment groups. The seventy five per cent group, with its profits and assets tests, governs group relief, and a consortium needs members of at least five per cent owning at least three quarters. The gains group adds the effective fifty one per cent test. Stamp duty and stamp duty land tax use their own seventy five per cent tests. The worldwide group of consolidated companies governs the interest restriction, and Pillar Two and country-by-country reporting use consolidated groups too.

The consolidated tax charge is not the tax bill. It includes overseas and deferred tax. The United Kingdom bill is a set of company liabilities.

Then the ladder, for the financial year twenty twenty six. Profits over one and a half million pounds make a company large for instalments, and over twenty million pounds very large, both divided by the company plus its associated companies. Net interest over two million pounds brings the interest restriction into play. Five million pounds is the deductions allowance for losses. Two hundred million pounds of turnover or two billion pounds of balance sheet switches on the senior accounting officer, the tax strategy and uncertain tax treatment reporting. Seven hundred and fifty million euros switches on country-by-country reporting, transfer pricing records and Pillar Two. Leaving the small and medium-sized category switches on transfer pricing.

For Tarnmoor in Group Year One, that means revenue of one thousand one hundred and eighty million pounds, about one thousand three hundred and fifty seven million euros at our invented rate, a divisor of nine for the association, a very large threshold of about two point two two million pounds, and Calder turned from large to very large overnight.

And the job is to identify the issues, explain them precisely, and coordinate the specialists.

To answer this chapter's question, then. Calder is one company among many for computing and paying its tax, and part of one large group for almost every threshold that decides how. Next, chapter two asks where all of these rules actually live, and how an adviser finds them.

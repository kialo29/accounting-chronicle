Chapter ten: Research and development.

In our invented case, it is a grey morning in early April of Group Year Two, and Graham Pike, the finance director of Calder Valve Engineering, is reading a checklist from the group tax team. Calder is the invented valve maker from The Living Law, now owned by our invented group, Tarnmoor. Its first year inside the group has just ended, on the thirty first of March. Calder has spent two million pounds developing a valve that can sense its own wear. Item four on the checklist asks a simple question. Has Calder made a research and development claim in the last three years? Graham checks the files. Calder has never claimed at all.

That one fact sets a clock running. If Calder does not tell H M R C, His Majesty's Revenue and Customs, that it intends to claim, within six months of the end of that year, the claim is invalid. Not reduced. Invalid. A credit worth four hundred thousand pounds would be lost for want of a short online form.

This chapter is about the relief that pays companies to invent, and about the machinery around it, which is now as much about proof and procedure as about rates. It sits in Part Two of this book, on measuring profit, because the relief works inside the trading computation. All three syllabus rows it covers are core for the paper. They are the research and development expenditure credit, research and development intensive companies, and research and development allowances. The Living Law, chapter twenty one, introduced the merged scheme and the intensive relief at Taxation Knowledge level. Here we go deeper, into the payment steps, group surrender, the size tests that catch a start-up the day a large group buys it, the overseas restriction, and the claim notification trap that Graham has just found.

The law is the law for the financial year twenty twenty six, under the Finance Act twenty twenty six. The main rules live in Part thirteen of the Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, which I will call the two thousand and nine Act, and the allowances in Part six of the Capital Allowances Act two thousand and one, which I will call the Capital Allowances Act.

Why the state pays for ideas.

Start with the problem the relief solves. When a company discovers something, it cannot keep all of the value. Competitors learn from its products, its engineers move on, and other firms build on what it has shown is possible. Economists call this a positive externality. The company bears the whole cost but captures only part of the benefit, so left alone it invests less in research than would be best for the economy as a whole.

The government says exactly this. Its guidance on the merged scheme gives the policy aim as supporting and incentivising research and development, helping overcome a market failure, a positive externality, which causes underinvestment. The relief is a subsidy delivered through the tax system, and it is a large one. H M R C's statistics published in September twenty twenty six give a provisional figure of eight point two billion pounds of relief claimed for twenty twenty four to twenty five, on about forty thousand three hundred claims. The number of claims fell by about seventeen per cent in a year, driven by fewer claims from smaller companies.

Does the subsidy work? The evidence says it buys extra research, though by how much depends on the scheme. An H M R C working paper in twenty fifteen estimated that each pound of tax forgone stimulated between one pound fifty three and two pounds thirty five of extra research spending. H M R C's evaluation of the expenditure credit, published in November twenty twenty, put the figure for that scheme at between two pounds forty and two pounds seventy for each pound of relief. Those estimates count only extra spending by companies already claiming, not wider spillovers.

The other side of the story is abuse. During the years of easy claims, a market grew up of advisers who promised refunds for routine work and took a share of the cash. H M R C later revised its estimate of error and fraud for twenty twenty to twenty one to sixteen point seven per cent of the cost of the reliefs, about one point one three billion pounds. In the old small company scheme the figure was twenty four point four per cent, almost one pound in every four. Those numbers explain almost every procedural rule in this chapter, from the advance notification the additional information form to the restriction on overseas work and the cap on payable credits by reference to the company's payroll taxes.

So the policy debate has two sides, and both are serious. One side says research relief is among the most effective things the tax system does, and that complexity and compliance burdens deter the honest small firms who need it most. The other says a relief paid in cash to loss-making companies will attract fraud unless it is fenced, and that the fences are the price of keeping the relief at all. The law today reflects the second view tempered by the first. As a larger company adviser, you will meet both. There is the group that under-claims because nobody joined up the engineers and the tax team, and there is the boutique adviser who offers to find a refund that is not there.

What counts as research and development.

Before any rate applies, the activity must be research and development. The tax definition starts from generally accepted accounting practice and is then shaped by guidelines issued by the government department responsible for science, which tax law adopts. The heart of the test is that a project seeks an advance in science or technology, by resolving scientific or technological uncertainty. The guidelines also ask whether a competent professional working in the field would regard the advance as one, rather than as routine development. Work that merely applies known techniques, however clever or commercially valuable, is not research and development for tax.

The courts have tested this line twice in recent years, both times in the First-tier Tribunal, so neither decision binds other tribunals. In Get Onbord Limited versus H M R C, decided in twenty twenty four, a company had built an artificial intelligence tool for know your client checks. H M R C refused the claim, saying the project did not advance overall knowledge or capability. The tribunal allowed the company's appeal. It accepted the company's evidence and said that, once a taxpayer has shown enough to suggest an overall advance, H M R C needs some material of its own to show the advance was only routine.

In Tills Plus Limited versus H M R C, decided in the same year, the result went the other way. The company's software project was found to combine existing technology and products rather than to develop something new. The tribunal found no evidence of an advance achieved by resolving scientific or technological uncertainty, and the claim failed. One commentary noted that an expert report put forward for the company lacked evidence and reasoning.

Read together, the two cases make one lesson. The test is the same. Evidence decides it. A claim stands or falls on contemporaneous records that show what was uncertain, why a competent engineer could not simply look the answer up, and what the team tried.

Now to our invented case. Calder's Project Ashlar is a smart process valve. Sensors inside the valve body measure vibration and seal wear, so that a refinery can replace a valve before it fails rather than on a fixed timetable. The uncertainty is real. Nobody knew whether sensors and their wiring could survive the pressure and heat inside the valve body, or whether a signal could pass reliably through thick steel. Dan Hartley, the invented chartered engineer whose life we followed in The Living Law, spends sixty per cent of his time on Ashlar in Calder's first group year. His design notes and failed prototypes are exactly the evidence that a tribunal looks for.

Here is a misconception worth clearing early. People assume research must happen in a laboratory, with white coats. It need not. The workshop where Dan's team cracks open a failed prototype qualifies, if the project seeks a genuine advance. Equally, the shiniest laboratory in the group does not qualify for work that is routine testing of a known product.

The merged expenditure credit.

For many years the United Kingdom ran two schemes side by side. Small and medium-sized companies received an enhanced deduction, and loss makers could swap losses for cash. Larger companies received an expenditure credit, shown in the accounts above the line, like a grant. In the Autumn Statement of the twenty second of November, twenty twenty three, the government announced that the two would be merged. Schedule one to the Finance Act twenty twenty four enacted the merged scheme, alongside a separate enhanced relief for loss-making small companies that are research intensive. The start date was fixed by regulations. The merged scheme applies for accounting periods beginning on or after the first of April, twenty twenty four.

The merged scheme's credit is called the research and development expenditure credit, which practitioners say as R D E C. The rate is twenty per cent of qualifying expenditure. A higher rate of forty nine per cent applies to ring fence oil and gas trades, which this paper does not examine. Any trading company can claim, whatever its size, including a small company that is not research intensive enough for the enhanced relief.

Three features set the credit apart from the old enhanced deduction.

First, the credit is taxable. It is treated as a trading receipt of the period. The company deducts its research spending in full, as normal trading expenses, and then adds the credit back as income. That is why the credit appears above the line.

Second, the net value is lower than the headline. For a company paying corporation tax at twenty five per cent, a credit of twenty per cent is worth twenty per cent less a quarter of itself, which is fifteen per cent of the spending. For a company taxed at nineteen per cent, the net value is sixteen point two per cent.

Third, the credit is used through a fixed set of payment steps, which first pay the company's own corporation tax, and only at the end produce cash. Those steps are where the examiners have caught candidates, and we take them slowly in a moment.

Calder shows how the credit runs through a computation. In its year to the thirty first of March of Group Year Two, Calder's qualifying expenditure is two million pounds. Its credit is twenty per cent of that, four hundred thousand pounds. Before the credit, Calder's adjusted trading profit is two point six million pounds, after deducting the two million pounds of research costs as ordinary expenses and after capital allowances. Add the credit as a trading receipt and the taxable total profits become three million pounds. Corporation tax at twenty five per cent is seven hundred and fifty thousand pounds.

The credit then pays four hundred thousand pounds of that tax, leaving three hundred and fifty thousand pounds to pay. The group's real benefit is four hundred thousand pounds of credit less one hundred thousand pounds of tax on it, three hundred thousand pounds, which is the fifteen per cent we expected.

Qualifying expenditure.

The credit is twenty per cent of qualifying expenditure, so the next question is which costs qualify. The law lists categories, and a cost outside them does not count, however closely it relates to the project.

Staffing costs come first and are usually the largest. They are the salaries, employer's national insurance contributions and employer pension contributions of staff directly and actively engaged in the research. Where a person works only partly on research, the cost is apportioned on a just and reasonable basis. Dan Hartley's employment costs are about one hundred and twenty thousand pounds a year. Sixty per cent of his time goes on Ashlar, so seventy two thousand pounds of his cost qualifies.

Externally provided workers are people supplied by an agency or another company who work under the claimant's direction. Contractor payments are payments for research that another business carries out under contract. For both, the qualifying amount from an unconnected provider is restricted to sixty five per cent of the payment. Connected providers, or providers who jointly elect to be treated as connected, are measured differently, by reference to the provider's own relevant costs. 
Calder subcontracted part of the sensor design to an invented start-up called Brackenwell Sensors, paying four hundred thousand pounds. Brackenwell was unconnected throughout Calder's year, so sixty five per cent qualifies, two hundred and sixty thousand pounds.

Then come consumable items, such as materials used up or transformed in prototypes. Calder burns through castings, seals and sensors worth one hundred and eighty thousand pounds. Software, data licences and cloud computing services qualify too, so far as they are used directly in the research. Data and cloud costs have qualified since the first of April, twenty twenty three. Calder's are sixty thousand pounds. Its remaining staffing costs for the engineers and technicians on the project are one point four two eight million pounds. Add them up and Calder's qualifying expenditure is two million pounds exactly. The full working is in the reading edition.

Every category also has a payment condition. No amount can be claimed until it has actually been paid.

Notice what is missing. Repairs to the test bay are not research, even if the bay is used for research, and the November twenty twenty five examiners criticised candidates who put repairs into a claim. Rent, rates and general overheads do not qualify. Nor does capital expenditure. When Calder spends half a million pounds on a test rig, that cost does not enter the credit at all. It gets a capital allowance of its own, which we come to later in the chapter.

The categories are also a test of judgement. A cost is either in a category or out. The adviser's job is to resist the temptation to stretch a category to cover something that feels like research but is not, and to make sure that genuine costs, such as cloud processing of test data, are not left out because nobody told the tax team they existed.

Who claims, and research done abroad.

When research is done under a contract, two companies might want to claim the same work. The merged scheme answers with a simple principle. The company that decides to undertake or initiate the research claims it. The company that merely does the work to order cannot claim for it, where the customer intended or contemplated the research when the contract was made.

So Calder claims for the Ashlar sensor design, sixty five per cent of what it paid Brackenwell. Brackenwell, which did the work, cannot also claim for that work, even though it was the company whose engineers solved the problem. Its own claims rest on research it decided to undertake for itself. This is the cure for an old weakness, where both parties sometimes claimed the same project under different schemes.

Then there is the overseas restriction, in section eleven thirty eight A of the two thousand and nine Act. Since accounting periods beginning on or after the first of April, twenty twenty four, contractor payments for research carried out abroad, and the costs of externally provided workers who are not subject to United Kingdom payroll taxes, are excluded from both the merged scheme and the intensive relief. There is one exception. The research qualifies if conditions that are necessary for it are not present in the United Kingdom, are present where the work is done, and it would be wholly unreasonable to replicate them in the United Kingdom. The conditions may be geographical, environmental, social, legal or regulatory. The law says expressly that the cost of workers and the availability of workers are to be disregarded.

Suppose, for this example only, that Calder had paid a laboratory in Vallaria, an invented country, to run the pressure tests because it was cheaper. That spending would be excluded, because cheaper labour is exactly the reason the law disregards. Suppose instead that the tests needed a natural geological condition found only in Vallaria. Then the exception might apply. The May twenty twenty five paper tested this rule, with a company that subcontracted work overseas.

The Finance Act twenty twenty six made one small change here, in section thirty four. A separate relaxation lets Northern Ireland companies claiming the intensive relief escape the overseas restriction, because of the Windsor Framework. The Act confirms that this relaxation applies only to the intensive relief, and the change has effect for claims made on or after the thirtieth of October, twenty twenty four. In other words, it is retrospective. It will rarely matter in a paper about large groups, but it is a good illustration of how a technical correction can reach back in time.

Seven steps to cash.

Now the hard part, slowly. The credit is not simply paid out. It flows through seven steps set out from section ten forty two I of the two thousand and nine Act onwards. Picture a waterfall. The credit first fills the company's own tax pool. What spills over loses a splash to notional tax. Then it meets a weir set by the company's payroll taxes. Only what passes all of that flows on to other periods, other group companies, other taxes, and finally to the company's bank account.

Before the steps begin, any step two amount brought forward from an earlier period is used to pay the current period's corporation tax.

Step one. The credit discharges the company's corporation tax liability for the period of the claim. Calder stops here. Its credit of four hundred thousand pounds is less than its tax of seven hundred and fifty thousand pounds, so all of it is used at step one.

Step two is the notional tax deduction. If any credit is left after step one, it is reduced by notional tax. The rate is the main rate of twenty five per cent if the company's profits are chargeable at the main rate, and that includes a company in the marginal relief band. Otherwise the rate is the small profits rate of nineteen per cent, which covers small profit companies and loss-making companies. The lower rate for loss makers is the main design concession in the merged scheme. The amount deducted is called the step two amount, and it is not lost. It can be surrendered to another company in the group, or carried forward to pay the company's corporation tax in later periods.

Why notional tax at all? Because the credit is taxable. A loss-making company pays no tax on its credit this year, but the credit reduces its loss, and so its future relief. The notional tax deduction stops the company taking cash today for the tax element of the credit.

Step three is the pay as you earn cap. Cash from the credit is limited to twenty thousand pounds plus three hundred per cent of the company's relevant pay as you earn and national insurance liabilities for the period. Anything above the cap is carried forward as credit of the next period. The cap does not apply if the company is exempt. Broadly, H M R C's guidance says the company is exempt if its staff are creating or managing intellectual property it can exploit, and no more than fifteen per cent of its qualifying expenditure goes on connected subcontractors and externally provided workers. The cap targets claims by shell companies with no real payroll, which is where much of the fraud sat.

Step four. What remains discharges the company's corporation tax for any other accounting period.

Step five. A company in a group may surrender the whole or part of what remains to another group member, which uses it to pay its own corporation tax.

Step six. What remains can discharge the company's other liabilities to H M R C, such as value added tax or pay as you earn.

Step seven. Only then is any balance paid to the company in cash, and H M R C need not pay while the company is not a going concern or while its return is under enquiry.

The May twenty twenty four examiners reported that candidates confused step one, the set off against the period's own tax, with step two, the notional tax restriction. Learn the order as a sentence. Own tax, notional tax, payroll cap, other periods, group, other taxes, cash.

Surrender inside the group.

Step five is where a large group finds value, and Brackenwell shows how.

In our invented case, Tarnmoor buys Brackenwell Sensors on the first of July of Group Year Two. Brackenwell is the loss-making sensor developer founded by Doctor Asha Varma, who stays on as its chief technology officer. In Group Year Two, which for Brackenwell is the year to the thirty first of December, its qualifying expenditure is three million pounds. As we shall see in a moment, Brackenwell is no longer a small company that year. So it claims the merged credit, twenty per cent of three million pounds, six hundred thousand pounds.

Run the steps. At step one, Brackenwell has no corporation tax to pay, because it is making losses. Nothing is used. At step two, it is a loss maker, so notional tax is at nineteen per cent. Nineteen per cent of six hundred thousand pounds is one hundred and fourteen thousand pounds, and four hundred and eighty six thousand pounds remains. At step three, Brackenwell's relevant payroll taxes are about nine hundred thousand pounds, so its cap is twenty thousand pounds plus two point seven million pounds, two point seven two million pounds. The cap does not bite. At step four, there is no other period's tax to pay.

At step five, Tom Hesketh, the group's head of tax, has a choice. Brackenwell could go on to take the four hundred and eighty six thousand pounds in cash. Or it could surrender it to a group company that pays tax. Tarnmoor Engineering, the main trading company, has a corporation tax bill of two million and twenty thousand pounds for Group Year Two. Brackenwell surrenders the four hundred and eighty six thousand pounds to it. It also surrenders the one hundred and fourteen thousand pound step two amount, which would otherwise sit idle in a company that will make losses for years. Together the two surrenders pay six hundred thousand pounds of Tarnmoor Engineering's tax.

Does the group lose anything? The credit is taxable income for Brackenwell, so its loss is six hundred thousand pounds smaller, and so is the loss it can surrender to Tarnmoor Engineering as group relief. That costs the group one hundred and fifty thousand pounds of tax at twenty five per cent. The net benefit is four hundred and fifty thousand pounds, fifteen per cent of three million pounds. The steps move cash around. The rate decides value.

Next, the money between the companies. Tarnmoor Engineering pays Brackenwell for the credit it received, so that each company's accounts show what it gave up and what it gained, which matters to its directors and its creditors. Here the Finance Act twenty twenty six helps. Section thirty one inserts new subsections into section ten forty two N. A payment for surrendered credit, made under an agreement between the companies and not exceeding the credit surrendered, is ignored in computing both companies' profits, and is not a distribution. It applies to payments made on or after the twenty sixth of November, twenty twenty five. So Tarnmoor Engineering's payment of six hundred thousand pounds is tax neutral. Pay more than the credit, and the excess falls outside the protection.

Research and development intensive companies.

Now the second relief. Alongside the merged credit, the law keeps an enhanced relief for one kind of company only, a small or medium-sized enterprise that is loss-making and research intensive. Practitioners call it enhanced R and D intensive support, or E R I S.

Start with size. For research relief, a small or medium-sized enterprise is defined by the European Commission's recommendation of two thousand and three, with the ceilings doubled. The company must have fewer than five hundred staff, and either turnover of no more than one hundred million euros or a balance sheet total of no more than eighty six million euros. The recommendation's own ceilings are two hundred and fifty staff, fifty million euros and forty three million euros. The twenty twenty six exam tax tables set out the definition, so you need to know how to apply it rather than memorise every figure.

The recommendation does not look at the company alone. A linked enterprise, broadly one that controls or is controlled by the company, usually through more than half of the voting rights, is added in full. A partner enterprise, broadly a holding of between twenty five and fifty per cent, is added in proportion to the holding.

Next, intensity. The company's relevant research expenditure must be at least thirty per cent of its total relevant expenditure, with the figures of connected companies brought in. A company that met the test in its previous twelve month period, with a valid claim, gets one year's grace if it dips below.

Then the relief. The company deducts its qualifying expenditure in full and takes an extra deduction of eighty six per cent, one hundred and eighty six per cent in all. If that leaves a loss, it may surrender part of the loss for a payable credit at fourteen point five per cent. The surrenderable loss is the lower of the unrelieved trading loss and one hundred and eighty six per cent of the related qualifying expenditure. The unrelieved loss is the loss left after any relief against the company's other profits of the period. The credit is not taxable, unlike the merged credit, and the loss surrendered is gone. At best, a company receives one hundred and eighty six per cent of fourteen point five per cent, almost twenty seven pence in cash for each pound of qualifying spending. The pay as you earn cap applies here too, as does the exemption. Companies in Northern Ireland meet one further limit, under section eleven twelve J, which caps their extra relief at the level of de minimis State aid.

Brackenwell, before Tarnmoor bought it, is the model case. In its year to the thirty first of December of Group Year One, it had forty five staff and was independent, owned by its founders and venture investors. Its relevant research spending was three point five million pounds out of total relevant expenditure of five million pounds, an intensity of seventy per cent. Its qualifying expenditure was three point five million pounds, so the extra deduction was three point zero one million pounds. Its trading loss of four point two million pounds grew to seven point two one million pounds.

The cap on the surrenderable loss is one hundred and eighty six per cent of three point five million pounds, six point five one million pounds. That is less than the loss, so Brackenwell surrenders six point five one million pounds, and its credit is fourteen point five per cent of that, nine hundred and forty three thousand nine hundred and fifty pounds. Its payroll tax cap, at two point seven two million pounds, does not bite. The remaining loss of seven hundred thousand pounds is carried forward.

That credit was due to Brackenwell for a year when it was independent. It matters in the deal. A buyer who pays for a company with a large research credit pending must decide whether the price includes it, and the sale agreement should say who benefits if H M R C reduces it. Chapter twenty, on buying and selling companies, picks that up.

Linked enterprises and the cliff edge of acquisition.

Here is this chapter's main misconception. Many people think a small subsidiary of a large group can claim the intensive relief because the subsidiary itself is small. It cannot. Size is tested across linked enterprises. The moment Tarnmoor owns Brackenwell, the group's thousands of staff and its revenue of more than a billion pounds are added to Brackenwell's own. Brackenwell is no longer a small or medium-sized enterprise. It can claim only the merged credit.

The May twenty twenty four paper turned on exactly this. A parent and its research subsidiary were linked enterprises, so the size tests had to be applied to both together, and the examiners reported that most candidates missed it.

The next question is timing. The recommendation normally lets a company keep its status until it crosses the thresholds in two consecutive years, a cushion against a single good or bad year. H M R C's view is that the cushion does not apply when a company is taken over by a large group. In H M R C's view, the company is treated as large for research purposes for the whole accounting period in which it is acquired, including the months before the takeover.

That is why Brackenwell's Group Year Two claim is a merged credit claim on all three million pounds of spending, including the half year before the first of July. The point is H M R C's guidance rather than settled law, but it is how claims are processed and a well advised buyer plans around it. Advisers sometimes suggest that the target's accounting period should end at completion, so that the pre-acquisition months form a separate period that can still be tested as a small company. In our invented case, nobody thought of that until too late. The lesson is to put research status on the due diligence list before the sale agreement is signed.

The size test also works in reverse. A small company that is part owned by an investor holding between twenty five and fifty per cent must include a share of the investor's own figures. Before acquisition, the question was whether Brackenwell's venture investors made it a partner of anything larger. The recommendation has special rules for some kinds of investor, and in our invented case they were satisfied. In real work you would check them against the text in H M R C's manual.

Telling H M R C first.

Back to Graham Pike and his clock. Two procedural requirements now guard every claim.

The first is claim notification. Section eleven forty two A of the two thousand and nine Act, inserted by the Finance Number Two Act twenty twenty three, requires a company to notify H M R C in advance if it is claiming for the first time, or if it has not made a claim in the three years ending with the last day of the notification period. The notification period begins on the first day of the period of account and ends six months after the period of account ends. Without a notification, a claim made after that period ends is invalid. Regulations made in twenty twenty three set out what the notification must contain, and H M R C's directions require it online, naming the company officer responsible.

For Calder, the period of account ends on the thirty first of March of Group Year Two, so the notification must be in by the thirtieth of September of Group Year Two. Graham files it in April. Brackenwell does not need to notify for Group Year Two, because it claimed the intensive relief for Group Year One, within the three year window.

Note the trap in the wording. The window runs from the period of account, not the accounting period. Where a long period of account is split into two accounting periods, the six months run from the end of the period of account. And a company whose last claim was more than three years ago is treated like a first time claimant.

The second requirement is the additional information form. For claims made on or after the eighth of August, twenty twenty three, every claim must be supported by a form giving the projects, the costs by category and the people involved, submitted before the claim in the return. H M R C has power to remove a claim from a return where the company failed to meet a requirement for making it, such as the form.

The ordinary time limit then applies. A research claim can be made, amended or withdrawn within two years from the end of the period of account, in the company return rules in Schedule eighteen to the Finance Act nineteen ninety eight.

For a large group, this becomes a calendar. Tom Hesketh's team keeps a list of every company that does research, its period of account, whether it has claimed in the last three years, and the notification deadline. A company that joins the group, a newly formed subsidiary, or a company that pauses its research for a few years, is exactly the kind that falls through the gaps. Losing a claim through a missed form is not a technical failure that H M R C can overlook. The law simply says the claim cannot be made.

Research and development allowances.

Not all research spending is revenue. Calder's half million pound test rig is plant used for research. Capital expenditure never enters the merged credit or the intensive relief. Instead, Part six of the Capital Allowances Act gives a research and development allowance of one hundred per cent.

The allowance applies to capital expenditure on research and development related to the trade, undertaken by the trader or on its behalf. The meaning of research and development follows the same accounting based definition and guidelines as the credit. Land does not qualify. The allowance is given for the chargeable period in which the expenditure is incurred, or for the period in which the trade starts if the spending comes first, and the company may claim less than the full amount. When the asset is later sold, its disposal value brings a balancing charge.

Calder's rig gets a five hundred thousand pound allowance in its year to the thirty first of March of Group Year Two. In our invented case, that allowance sits inside Calder's capital allowances of one point eight million pounds for the year, alongside one point three million pounds of ordinary plant and machinery allowances.

Why choose the research allowance over full expensing? For a company, new plant may already get full expensing at one hundred per cent, and chapter eight covered it. The research allowance has one big advantage. It covers capital spending on buildings used for research, which full expensing never does. Against that, a disposal triggers a balancing charge. The November twenty twenty five paper had a new subsidiary with research equipment, and the examiners reported that few candidates mentioned the research allowance at all.

Where the regimes meet.

Research relief does not live alone, and the examiner likes the joins.

First, the corporate interest restriction, which chapter twenty eight owns. The restriction allows interest deductions by reference to tax E B I T D A, short for earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, computed on tax figures. The law excludes from tax E B I T D A both the merged credit and the intensive relief deductions. So the credit does not increase a company's capacity for interest, and capital allowances, including research allowances, do not reduce it. That is why Calder's tax E B I T D A for its first group year is four point four million pounds, before both its allowances and its credit.

Second, the accounts. The merged credit is designed to sit above the line, in operating profit, like a grant, and it is taxable. The intensive relief payable credit is not taxable, and in practice it is often shown in the tax charge. Chapter six, on deferred tax, explains why the difference affects the effective tax rate the group reports.

Third, intellectual property. Ashlar's patents, filed in Group Year Two, will later earn Calder the Patent Box's ten per cent rate. The Patent Box rewards research done by the company itself more than acquired research or work bought from connected parties. Chapter eleven explains how.

Fourth, transfer pricing. When research is done in one group company for the benefit of another, the price must be at arm's length. The November twenty twenty three paper asked candidates to price a research centre, and the cost plus method was the answer expected. Chapter twenty seven takes this further, and shows how the location of research functions affects where the group's intangibles profits are taxed.

Fifth, groups. Surrender of the credit is a group matter, governed by the payment steps and the Finance Act twenty twenty six payment rule. It is separate from group relief for losses, which chapter fifteen owns, though the two interact, as Brackenwell's reduced loss showed.

Going further, the tax function's work.

In a large group, the tax function's job is to make sure that every genuine claim is made, and that no claim is made that should not be.

The first task is finding the research. Engineers do not think in tax categories. A group needs a process that reaches the people who run projects, asks them what was uncertain, and records the answers when the work happens, not two years later. Dan Hartley's project notes are worth more than any adviser's narrative written after the event.

The second task is the calendar, which we have seen, with its claim notification deadlines, additional information forms, and return deadlines, company by company.

The third task is location. The overseas restriction means research done abroad through contractors will usually not qualify. A group that is choosing where to place a laboratory needs that fact on the table, alongside the transfer pricing consequences of putting valuable functions in one country rather than another.

The fourth task is acquisitions. Due diligence on a research company must ask whether it has claimed correctly, whether its size status is right, whether credits are pending, and whether the acquisition will end its intensive relief.

The fifth task is ethics. In our invented case, a firm offers to review the group's claims for a share of any extra refund it finds. Tom Hesketh asks it to explain each additional project in technical terms. It cannot. He declines. The professional conduct rules, which chapter four discusses, expect an adviser to stand behind every claim filed. What to take away.

Research relief exists because ideas leak. The company that pays for research cannot keep all of its value, so the state shares the cost through the tax system. The price of that generosity was fraud and error on a large scale, and almost every procedural rule in the chapter is a fence built in response.

For accounting periods beginning on or after the first of April, twenty twenty four, the merged scheme gives a research and development expenditure credit of twenty per cent of qualifying expenditure. The credit is a taxable trading receipt. Its net value is fifteen per cent for a company paying twenty five per cent, and sixteen point two per cent at nineteen per cent.

Qualifying expenditure is staffing costs, externally provided workers, contractor payments, consumables, software, data and cloud costs. Unconnected contractors and externally provided workers count at sixty five per cent. Every cost must have been paid. Capital spending is out of the credit, and gets a one hundred per cent research and development allowance under Part six of the Capital Allowances Act instead. Repairs and overheads are out. Contractor work done abroad is out, unless the conditions needed are not present in the United Kingdom and it would be wholly unreasonable to replicate them, with cost and availability of workers ignored. The company that decides to undertake the research claims it.

The credit runs through seven steps. Own tax. Notional tax at twenty five per cent, or nineteen per cent for small profit and loss-making companies, with the step two amount surrendered or carried forward. The payroll cap of twenty thousand pounds plus three hundred per cent of pay as you earn and national insurance, unless exempt. Other periods. Group surrender. Other taxes. Cash. A payment for surrendered credit, up to the credit surrendered, is ignored for tax and is not a distribution, under the Finance Act twenty twenty six.

Enhanced R and D intensive support is for loss-making small or medium-sized enterprises only. That means fewer than five hundred staff, and turnover up to one hundred million euros or a balance sheet up to eighty six million euros, with linked enterprises added in full and partners in proportion. Intensity must be at least thirty per cent. The relief is an eighty six per cent extra deduction, one hundred and eighty six per cent in all, and a fourteen point five per cent payable credit on the lower of the unrelieved loss and one hundred and eighty six per cent of the qualifying expenditure. A subsidiary of a large group cannot claim it, and H M R C's view is that a company acquired by a large group is large for the whole period of the acquisition.

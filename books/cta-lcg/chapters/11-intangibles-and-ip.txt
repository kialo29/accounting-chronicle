Chapter eleven: Intangible assets and intellectual property.

On the thirtieth of September, in Group Year Three of our invented group, Tarnmoor, Dan Hartley clears his desk at Calder Valve Engineering. His division is closing. The next morning, the first of October, Calder sells that division's customer contracts and its manufacturing know-how to the group's Vallarian subsidiary, Tarnmoor Vallaria, for six million pounds. Vallaria, like everything else in this story except Ireland, is invented.

Think about what was sold. Not a building. Not a machine. The machines went separately. What crossed the border was a set of promises from customers and a body of knowledge, much of it written down in the weeks before, from interviews with engineers like Dan. You cannot drop it on your foot. Yet it was worth six million pounds, and the tax system has to decide three things about it. Is the money income or a capital gain? What was it worth, when the buyer and the seller answer to the same board? And when the buyer is abroad, what stops the know-how leaving the United Kingdom's tax net at a bargain price?

This chapter answers those questions. It sits in Part Two, on measuring profit, because the intangibles code is the clearest case of tax following the accounts. Intangible fixed assets are core for the paper, grade one. The Patent Box is awareness, grade three. The Living Law, chapter twenty one, introduced the regime at the level of a company buying and writing off an asset. Here we go deeper, into goodwill, reinvestment, groups, degrouping, related parties, the twenty twenty six move to arm's length pricing, and the Patent Box. Everything is taught on the law for the financial year twenty twenty six.

Why intangibles got their own code.

Before April two thousand and two, a company's intangible assets lived in an awkward place. Goodwill and most other intangibles were capital assets. A company that bought goodwill got no deduction as it wore away. When it sold, it had a chargeable gain. Patents and know-how had their own small capital allowance codes. Trade marks and brands, in general, got nothing. The result was a patchwork that bore little relation to how the accounts treated the same assets, and it fitted badly with an economy whose value was moving from factories to ideas.

The answer was a single code, introduced by Schedule twenty nine to the Finance Act two thousand and two, and now rewritten as Part eight of the Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, which I will call the two thousand and nine Act. Its central idea is simple. For a company, an intangible fixed asset is taxed as income, not as capital. The amortisation and impairment in the accounts are deductions. Receipts and profits on sale are taxable credits. Losses on sale are deductions. Tax follows the accounts, with a few deliberate departures that are where most of the exam marks sit.

The code is symmetrical, and that is its purpose. A company that gets a deduction as an asset is written down must give the deduction back if it later sells for more. Think of it as a meter running in both directions. Every pound of relief is metered out, and if the asset turns out to have kept its value, the meter runs back.

There is a boundary. Part eight applies to assets created on or after the first of April, two thousand and two, and to assets acquired from an unrelated party after that date. Older assets, held before the regime began, stayed in the capital gains world. From the first of July, twenty twenty, any asset acquired from that date comes into Part eight whoever the seller is. The detailed rules for the older assets, in Chapter sixteen of Part eight, are outside the syllabus. You need only spot that an asset is old and say that it sits outside the code.

For a large group the code matters in four ways. It gives relief for bought intangibles. It taxes the sale of home-grown ones, often at far more than their book value. It lets assets move around a group without tax, but polices them when a company leaves. And it decides the price when an intangible crosses a border to a related company.

What counts as an intangible fixed asset.

The definition starts with the accounts. An intangible asset has its meaning under generally accepted accounting practice. The statute then widens it. It includes intellectual property, which covers patents, trade marks, registered designs, copyright and design right. It also covers any idea, information or technique not protected by such a right. That is how know-how gets in. Goodwill is expressly included.

The word fixed matters. The asset must be acquired or created for use on a continuing basis in the company's activities. Know-how bought to be resold quickly is not a fixed asset.

Next come the exclusions. Rights over land and over tangible movable property are out. Financial assets are out. So are shares and rights in companies and trusts. Shares are the big one. When a group buys a company, it buys shares, and the goodwill inside that company never becomes the group's intangible asset for tax. A company may also elect to keep capital spending on computer software within the capital allowance rules instead.

An asset inside the code is called a chargeable intangible asset. Each one is either used in a trade, held for a property business, or held for some other purpose. Credits and debits on trading assets go straight into the trading profit. Everything else is non-trading, and pools into a non-trading gain or loss on intangibles. We come back to that pool shortly.

In our invented case, the customer contracts and know-how Calder sold were trading assets. They were also home-grown. Calder had built them up over decades, and the cost of building them had always been charged in the accounts as it was spent. So they had no cost on the balance sheet and no cost for tax. Hold on to that, because it decides how big the tax bill on the sale will be.

The same is true of most intellectual property a group creates for itself. When Calder's engineers worked on Project Ashlar, the cost of their research was charged in the accounts as it was spent, and relieved through the research and development expenditure credit that chapter ten explains. The patents that came out of that work carry only a small capitalised cost, such as filing fees. That is a fair result. The research has already been relieved once. But it means that when home-grown intellectual property is sold, almost all of the price is taxable. Groups that think of their patents as capital assets, protected by the substantial shareholding exemption, are often surprised. The exemption protects a sale of shares, never a sale of the assets themselves.

Writing down by the accounts, or at four per cent.

The default rule is that the tax deduction follows the accounts. If a company amortises an intangible, or writes it down for impairment, the amount in the profit and loss account is the deduction. Where the tax cost differs from the accounting value, the statute scales the accounting write-off by the ratio of tax written down value to accounting value. A later reversal of an impairment is a taxable credit.

Following the accounts creates a problem. Some intangibles are never amortised. Under international accounting standards, goodwill and intangibles with an indefinite useful life are not amortised at all. They are only tested for impairment. Under F R S one oh two, by contrast, every intangible, goodwill included, is treated as having a finite life and is amortised. So two identical acquisitions can produce very different tax deductions, depending on the accounting framework.

The fixed-rate election is Parliament's fix. A company may elect to write an asset down at four per cent of its cost each year, whatever the accounts do. The debit is the lower of four per cent of cost and the remaining tax written down value. At four per cent, the cost is relieved over twenty five years.

The conditions are strict. The election is made in writing to H M R C, which is His Majesty's Revenue and Customs, within two years after the end of the accounting period in which the asset was created or acquired. It covers all the capitalised spending on that asset. And it is irrevocable.

When does it help? Take a labelled example, not part of the story. Suppose Tarnmoor Engineering buys a registered trade mark for five million pounds and its accounts give it an indefinite life, so there is no amortisation. Without an election, there is no deduction unless the brand is impaired. With the election, there is a deduction of two hundred thousand pounds a year, worth fifty thousand pounds of tax a year at twenty five per cent. For a long-lived asset that the accounts will never amortise, the election is usually worth making. For an asset the accounts write off over five years, it would slow relief down. The skill is choosing asset by asset, inside the two-year window.

There is one twist for goodwill. As we will see, goodwill and customer-related assets that qualify for relief at all are written down at a fixed six point five per cent, not four per cent, and not by the accounts.

Realisation, when an intangible leaves.

A realisation is the event that ends, or reduces, the company's ownership. In general it is a transaction that causes the asset to cease to be recognised on the balance sheet, or would do so if it had a balance sheet value. A sale is the obvious case. A part disposal is a part realisation.

The arithmetic depends on the asset's history. If the asset has been written down for tax, the credit is the proceeds less the tax written down value. If it has not been written down, the credit is the proceeds less the cost recognised for tax. If the proceeds are lower, there is a debit instead. Proceeds are the amount recognised in the accounts, less incidental costs of the realisation. Where the consideration is not in money, it is treated as money equal to what it would reasonably have fetched in the open market. On a part realisation, only a matching proportion of the cost is set against the proceeds.

An asset that was never on the balance sheet is treated as if it had a balance sheet value. That is the rule that catches home-grown assets. Their cost was expensed long ago, so their tax cost is nil, and the whole of the proceeds is a credit.

Now return to the first of October, in Group Year Three. Calder sells its process-valve customer contracts and know-how to Tarnmoor Vallaria for six million pounds. The cost for tax is nil. So the realisation credit is the full six million pounds. Because the assets were used in Calder's trade, the credit is a trading credit. It lands in Calder's nine-month accounting period to the thirty first of December, Group Year Three. At twenty five per cent, it carries one point five million pounds of corporation tax.

Notice what has not happened. There is no capital gain, no indexation, no capital loss to set against it, and no substantial shareholding exemption. It is income, taxed as trading profit. The related plant, sold for one point four million pounds, follows the capital allowance rules of chapter eight, not these.

Also notice the price. Six million pounds is what Calder and Tarnmoor Vallaria agreed. Both are owned by Tarnmoor. Later in this chapter we will see that the law does not simply accept that figure. It asks what independent parties would have paid. In our invented case, H M R C will later argue for nine and a half million pounds, and the dispute will settle years afterwards at eight million. Chapter twenty seven tells that story. Here the point is only that a realisation credit is only as good as the price behind it.

Non-trading debits, credits and losses.

Not every intangible is used in a trade. A holding company might own a brand and license it to its subsidiaries. An investment company might hold a patent portfolio. For those assets, the credits and debits do not go into a trading profit. They are pooled. Royalty income, amortisation, and realisation credits and debits on non-trading assets net off to give a single non-trading gain or loss on intangible fixed assets for the period.

A net gain is simply taxed as part of total profits. A net loss gives three choices. The company may claim to set it against its total profits of the same period. It may surrender it to another group company as group relief, under the rules chapter fifteen covers. Or it may carry it forward, where it is treated as a non-trading debit of the next period and joins that period's pool.

Two classic exam points sit here. First, a debit that the goodwill rules deny as an ordinary write-down may still arise on realisation, and then it is a non-trading debit even if the asset was used in the trade. That rule stops a company turning a blocked deduction into a trading loss. Second, change of ownership rules in Part fourteen of the Corporation Tax Act twenty ten, which I will call the twenty ten Act, can block the carry forward of a company's non-trading intangibles losses, just as they block other carried-forward reliefs. Chapter fourteen has the detail.

For a trading group like Tarnmoor, most intangibles are trading assets, and the non-trading pool is small. But in the exam, a holding company with a licensing arm is a favourite way of moving an intangible out of the trade and into this pool. Read the facts for who uses the asset, not who owns it.

A labelled example shows the pool at work. Suppose a holding company owns brands that it licenses to the group's trading companies. In a year it receives royalties of one million pounds, writes its brands down by three hundred thousand pounds, and sells one brand at a loss of two hundred thousand pounds. None of these is a trading item. The pool nets them to a non-trading gain of five hundred thousand pounds, taxed as part of the holding company's total profits. Had the loss on sale been larger than the royalties, the net loss could have been set against other profits of the year, surrendered to a group company, or carried forward.

Goodwill, a door that opened, slammed and reopened.

Goodwill is the most valuable intangible most businesses own, and its tax history is a saga. Picture a door. In two thousand and two, it opened. Companies buying a business could write off purchased goodwill against profits, following their accounts. Then it slammed shut. For acquisitions from the eighth of July, twenty fifteen, relief for goodwill and customer-related intangibles was withdrawn. And in April twenty nineteen it reopened, but only for visitors carrying intellectual property.

Why slam it? The Treasury's policy paper of twenty fifteen gave the reason. Relief was available only when a company bought a business directly, not when it bought the shares of a company owning the business. That distorted deals in favour of asset purchases. The paper said that removing the relief would bring the regime into line with other major economies, reduce distortion and level the playing field for mergers and acquisitions. Why reopen it? Because the government then wanted to support investment in intangibles and the United Kingdom's attractiveness for business. The compromise of twenty nineteen is the law today, in Chapter fifteen A of Part eight.

Here is how it works. The rules apply to relevant assets. Those are goodwill, information about customers or potential customers, customer relationships, unregistered trade marks and other signs used in a business, and licences of any of these. For a relevant asset acquired or created on or after the first of April, twenty nineteen, the asset is written down at a fixed six point five per cent a year.

Then come three restrictions. First, an asset caught by the old twenty fifteen rules stays caught. Second, if the relevant asset was not acquired as part of acquiring a business, or the business acquired included no qualifying intellectual property assets, there are no write-down debits at all. Qualifying intellectual property means patents, registered designs, copyright, design right and plant breeders' rights, and their foreign equivalents and licences. Third, if the asset was acquired from a related individual or a firm, typically when a sole trader incorporates, relief is denied unless a third-party acquisition condition is met.

Finally, the cap. Where the business does include qualifying intellectual property, relief on the relevant assets is limited to six times the spending on that intellectual property. If the relevant assets cost more than that, the debits are scaled down in proportion.

Take a labelled example. Suppose a group company buys a business for twenty million pounds, with patents of two million pounds and goodwill and customer relationships of fifteen million pounds. Six times two million is twelve million. So only twelve fifteenths, which is four fifths, of the six point five per cent write-down is allowed. The annual debit is seven hundred and eighty thousand pounds, worth one hundred and ninety five thousand pounds of tax a year.

Now the story. In Group Year Two, Tarnmoor Ireland, then still resident in the United Kingdom because its board met in Leeds, bought an Irish distributor's business, including its customer list, for three million pounds. The distributor had no patents, designs or copyright to speak of. So the customer list is a relevant asset acquired with a business that had no qualifying intellectual property. There are no debits at all. Its tax value stays at its cost of three million pounds. That matters later, when Tarnmoor Ireland moves its management to Dublin and the list is deemed sold at market value, which is chapter twenty two's story.

The myth of relief for bought goodwill.

Here is the misconception of this chapter. Goodwill bought with a business always gets tax relief. Many people believe it because it was true from two thousand and two to twenty fifteen, because accountants amortise goodwill under F R S one oh two, and because the word acquisition sounds like a purchase of assets.

It is wrong in at least four ways. First, buying shares gives no relief at all. The goodwill is the subsidiary's, or appears only in the consolidated accounts, and Part eight never sees it. Most large acquisitions are share deals, so most purchased goodwill gets nothing. Nor can a group fix that afterwards by hiving the business up from its new subsidiary into another group company. The intra-group transfer is tax-neutral, as we will see, so the transferee takes over the subsidiary's own tax cost, which for home-grown goodwill is usually nil, not the price the group paid for the shares. Second, an asset deal with no qualifying intellectual property gives no debits, as Tarnmoor Ireland found. Third, even with intellectual property, the six times cap limits relief. Fourth, a purchase from a related individual, typically an incorporation, is blocked.

And when relief is due, it is at six point five per cent, not the accounts rate. That means just over fifteen years to relieve the whole cost.

The myth has a planning consequence. A buyer will often prefer an asset deal, to get relief on the intellectual property and, within the cap, on goodwill. A seller usually prefers a share sale, because a company selling a trading subsidiary can often use the substantial shareholding exemption, which I will call the shareholding exemption, and pay no tax on the gain. That tension is at the heart of every negotiation in chapter twenty, on buying and selling companies. Price usually settles it.

The examiner tested this ground in November twenty twenty three, question four. A loss-making retailer had acquired goodwill and registered designs in twenty nineteen, inside a full computation. The registered designs were qualifying intellectual property, which opened the door for the goodwill. A candidate who said no relief for goodwill, without checking for qualifying intellectual property and the date, lost the marks. A candidate who gave relief on the full cost of the goodwill without testing the cap risked losing them too.

Reinvestment relief.

Suppose a company sells an intangible at a profit and uses the money to buy another. Should it be taxed on the first sale? The capital gains code has long said no, through roll-over relief. The intangibles code has its own version, called reinvestment relief, in Chapter seven of Part eight.

The conditions mirror roll-over. The old asset must have been a chargeable intangible asset throughout the company's ownership. The proceeds of realisation must exceed its cost recognised for tax. The company must spend on other chargeable intangible assets in a window running from twelve months before the realisation to three years after it, a window H M R C may extend. The new spending must be capitalised in the accounts. And the company must claim.

The arithmetic has a familiar shape. If the company reinvests at least the full proceeds, the relief is the proceeds less the old asset's cost. If it reinvests less than the proceeds, the relief is the amount reinvested less the old asset's cost, and nothing if that is negative. Either way, the realisation credit is reduced by the relief, and so is the tax cost of the new asset.

Notice what is never relieved. Any write-downs already given are clawed back in full. Reinvestment relief defers only the profit above original cost.

Take a labelled example. Suppose a company bought a brand for one million pounds, wrote it down for tax to six hundred thousand, and sells it for three million pounds. The realisation credit is two point four million. It spends two and a half million on a new patent within the window. That is less than the proceeds, so the relief is two and a half million less the original cost of one million, giving one and a half million. The taxable credit falls to nine hundred thousand pounds. That is the four hundred thousand of clawed-back write-downs plus the half a million not reinvested. The new patent's tax cost is one million pounds. The full working is in the reading edition.

Groups can share this relief. Spending by another company in the same intangibles group counts, on a joint claim. And if a group company buys control of a company that owns chargeable intangibles, the underlying assets can count as reinvestment, even though the group bought shares.

In our invented case, Tom Hesketh, the group head of tax, looks at whether Calder's six million pound credit could be deferred. Calder's know-how had no tax cost, so the potential relief is large. But it needs capitalised spending on new intangibles by Calder or a group company inside the window, and a claim would cut the new asset's tax cost by the same amount. Tom finds nothing that would make a claim worth having. In our invented case, no claim is made, and Calder pays the tax.

Tax-neutral transfers inside a group.

A group often wants to move an intangible from one company to another. A brand migrates to a new brand-owning company. Patents follow a business into a new subsidiary before a sale. If each move were a taxable realisation at market value, groups would be stuck. So the code makes intra-group transfers tax-neutral.

First, the group. Part eight uses a group definition built on the same lines as the capital gains group. A principal company and its seventy five per cent subsidiaries, and their seventy five per cent subsidiaries, so long as each is an effective fifty one per cent subsidiary of the principal company. Non-resident companies can be members.

The rule in section seven hundred and seventy five of the two thousand and nine Act has three conditions. Both companies are members of the same group at the time of transfer. The asset is a chargeable intangible asset of the transferor immediately before the transfer. And it is a chargeable intangible asset of the transferee immediately after. If so, the transfer is tax-neutral. The transferor has no credit or debit. The transferee steps into its shoes, as if it had held the asset all along and done everything the transferor did. And the transfer pricing rules do not apply to it.

The third condition is the one that matters across borders. Tarnmoor Vallaria is a member of the Tarnmoor intangibles group. But after the sale, the know-how is not a chargeable intangible asset of Tarnmoor Vallaria, because Tarnmoor Vallaria is not within the charge to United Kingdom corporation tax on it. So the October sale fails the test. It is a realisation at the transfer price, taxed in Calder. Tax neutrality is only for assets that stay inside the United Kingdom net.

There are exclusions too. For example, a transferor that has elected for the foreign branch exemption cannot pass on neutrally an asset used in its exempt foreign branch.

Now a transfer that passes. In Group Year Six, Tarnmoor Engineering hives down its actuators business into a new subsidiary, Tarnmoor Actuators. Among the assets are Tarnmoor Engineering's actuator patents, with a tax written down value of one point two million pounds and a market value of four million pounds. Both companies are in the group and in the charge to corporation tax. The patents pass at no tax cost. Tarnmoor Actuators simply takes over the tax written down value of one point two million pounds. Chapter nineteen covers the hive-down of the trade itself.

The degrouping charge and its switch-off.

Tax neutrality invites a game. Move an asset into a subsidiary at no tax cost, then sell the subsidiary's shares. The group has sold the asset, but no realisation has happened. The degrouping charge stops that game.

The law says that where a company leaves a group within six years of acquiring an asset in a tax-neutral transfer, and still holds the asset when it leaves, it is treated as having realised and immediately reacquired the asset at its market value at the time of the original transfer. The resulting credit, or debit, is brought into account immediately before the company leaves the group. There is a related rule where an associated company that leaves at the same time holds the asset.

Two features differ from the capital gains degrouping charge in section one seven nine of the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act nineteen ninety two, which I will call the Gains Act. First, the intangibles charge falls on the company leaving the group, not on the seller of its shares. Second, until twenty nineteen there was no equivalent of the twenty eleven gains reform, which adds the degrouping gain to the share sale proceeds so that the shareholding exemption can cover it. That left a gap. A share sale fully exempt for gains could still carry an intangibles charge inside the company being sold, which its buyer would then inherit.

The Finance Act twenty nineteen closed the gap with section seven hundred and eighty two A of the two thousand and nine Act, for degroupings on or after the seventh of November, twenty eighteen. Where a company leaves a group because of a disposal of shares by another group company, and that disposal would qualify for the shareholding exemption, the degrouping charge simply does not apply. H M R C's manual describes the purpose as stopping degrouping adjustments from impeding genuine commercial disposals of trading businesses. Think of it as the twin of the gains rule, reached by a different road. The gains rule moves the charge into an exempt sale. The intangibles rule switches it off.

Back to the story. On the thirty first of December, Group Year Six, Tarnmoor Engineering sells Tarnmoor Actuators to an invented American buyer, Brennock Industries, eleven months after the hive-down. Chapter twenty explains why the sale qualifies for the shareholding exemption, even though Tarnmoor Engineering has held the new company's shares for less than twelve months. Because it qualifies, the patents' degrouping charge is switched off.

What would it have cost? Ignoring any write-downs since the transfer, Tarnmoor Actuators would have been treated as selling and buying back the patents at four million pounds. Less the one point two million of tax written down value, that is a credit of two point eight million pounds, and seven hundred thousand pounds of tax at twenty five per cent. Switched off, the charge is nil. But note the price of the switch-off. With no deemed reacquisition, the patents keep their low tax value of one point two million pounds in Tarnmoor Actuators' hands. The buyer gets no uplift, which a well-advised buyer will reflect in the price.

Where the switch-off does not apply, the group has tools. The degrouping credit can be reallocated to another group company by election, it can be matched against reinvestment, and unpaid tax can be recovered from other group companies or a controlling director. In the exam, the first question is always whether the share sale qualifies for the shareholding exemption.

Related parties and the arm's length turn of twenty twenty six.

The group rules deal with transfers inside the United Kingdom net. The related party rules deal with everything else between people who are not at arm's length. Without them, a company could sell an intangible to a friendly party cheaply, or buy one dearly, and shift profit with a stroke of a pen.

A related party, for this code, includes a company that controls or is controlled by another, two companies under common control, and a company in which another has a major interest. Participators and their connected individuals are also related to a close company. Between related parties, a transfer of a chargeable intangible asset is treated as made at market value. The rule does not apply to a tax-neutral group transfer, which has its own rules.

Market value has a cousin, the arm's length price under the transfer pricing rules in Part four of the Taxation, International and Other Provisions, Act twenty ten, which I will call the International Act. The two can differ. Market value asks what the asset would fetch in an open market. Transfer pricing asks what independent enterprises would have agreed in the same circumstances, looking at each party's functions, assets, risks and realistic options. For a cross-border transfer, both tests could apply, with different answers.

The Finance Act twenty twenty six chose one. Paragraphs twenty five and twenty eight of its Schedule six say that the market value rule does not apply to a cross-border transfer that is subject to transfer pricing. Such a transfer is treated as made at the arm's length price. For other related party transfers, market value still applies. The new rule covers transfers and grants made on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six, unless they were made under a contract that became unconditional before that date.

Take a labelled real-calendar example. Suppose a United Kingdom company sells a brand to its French sister company on the fifteenth of February, twenty twenty six. That is a cross-border transfer subject to transfer pricing, so it is priced at arm's length, not at market value. If the same sale had been under a contract that became unconditional in December twenty twenty five, the old market value rule would still govern.

In our invented case, every story period falls under this law. So Calder's sale to Tarnmoor Vallaria is priced at arm's length. The six million pounds came from a valuation report. Whether it was the arm's length price is a transfer pricing question about who really controlled the development and use of the know-how. That is where Dan's interviews come back, in chapter twenty seven.

The Patent Box, a ten per cent rate for inventions.

The last tool in this chapter is a reward, not a restriction. The Patent Box, in Part eight A of the twenty ten Act, lets a company elect to have profits from patented inventions taxed at an effective rate of ten per cent. It is awareness for the paper, grade three. You need the principles, not the full computation.

Why does it exist? It is an incentive. By cutting the tax on profits from patented inventions, it makes the United Kingdom a more attractive place to own and exploit them, not just to do the research. The regime began on the first of April, twenty thirteen, phased in so that the full benefit applied from the first of April, twenty seventeen. It was then reshaped by an international agreement through the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, the O E C D, that preferential regimes for intellectual property must reward real research done by the company itself. That is the nexus approach, which applies to elections made after the thirtieth of June, twenty sixteen.

The conditions in outline. The company must be within the charge to corporation tax and must elect. It must hold qualifying patents, granted by the United Kingdom Intellectual Property Office, the European Patent Office or certain other European states, or an exclusive licence of them. It must meet the development condition, which means it, or a group company, did qualifying development on the invention. If the development was done by another group company, the holder must actively manage its portfolio.

The mechanism is a deduction, not a different rate. The deduction is the relevant intellectual property profits, multiplied by the main rate less ten per cent, divided by the main rate. At a twenty five per cent main rate, that is fifteen twenty fifths, or three fifths, of the relevant profits.

Finding relevant profits is the hard part. In outline, income is streamed between income from the patented inventions and other income. A routine return of ten per cent of certain costs is taken out, because ordinary manufacturing profit is not a reward for invention. A return for marketing assets, such as brands, comes out too. A simplified route exists for small claims. Then the nexus fraction applies. It is the company's own research spend plus research subcontracted to unconnected parties, times one point three, divided by the total research spend including connected-party subcontracting and the cost of acquiring the intellectual property. It is capped at one.

Now the story. Calder filed patents on Project Ashlar's valve design in Group Year Two. They were granted in Group Year Four. Calder elects into the Patent Box from Group Year Four and licenses the patents to Tarnmoor Vallaria for a royalty of six per cent of its Ashlar valve sales. That royalty is one point two million pounds a year. Calder did the development itself, with some work subcontracted to Brackenwell Sensors before Tarnmoor bought it, when it was unconnected. So the nexus fraction is one.

In a simplified illustration, the relevant intellectual property profits are nine hundred thousand pounds. The deduction is three fifths of that, five hundred and forty thousand pounds. The remaining three hundred and sixty thousand pounds is taxed at twenty five per cent, which is ninety thousand pounds. That is ten per cent of the nine hundred thousand. Without the box, the tax would have been two hundred and twenty five thousand. The real computation streams the profits and tests every step, and the reading edition shows the shape.

The election must be made within twelve months after the filing date of the return for the first period it is to cover. A company that revokes cannot come back in for five years.

The tax function's work, and the other regimes.

An intangibles question rarely stands alone. For a large group, the tax function's work runs across several regimes at once.

Start with acquisitions. On an asset deal, the purchase price must be allocated between goodwill, customer relationships, intellectual property, plant and stock. The allocation drives everything. It decides how much is qualifying intellectual property, how much goodwill can be relieved within the six times cap, and what capital allowances and stamp duty land tax follow. The tax team works with the valuers before the accounts are finalised, and diarises every four per cent election inside its two-year window.

Next, internal moves. Before any hive-down, check that the tax-neutral rule applies, and record the market value at the date of transfer, because that is the value the degrouping charge would use within the next six years. Before any share sale, check whether the shareholding exemption applies, because that decides whether the switch-off applies.

The calendar matters as much as the analysis. A four per cent election must be made within two years after the end of the period of acquisition. A Patent Box election must be made within twelve months after the filing date of the return for the first period it covers. A reinvestment claim must be made within the ordinary time limit for claims, with a provisional declaration possible where the reinvestment is still planned. Each of these is easy to miss in a busy group, and each is lost if missed.

Then cross-border transfers. Since the first of January, twenty twenty six, a transfer to an overseas group company is priced at arm's length. That means a functional analysis, a valuation, and transfer pricing documentation prepared at the time, as Tarnmoor's chapter twenty seven shows. A restructuring that moves know-how abroad is exactly the kind of transaction H M R C reviews.

Royalties raise their own questions. A United Kingdom company paying a royalty for intellectual property to a non-resident must normally deduct income tax at the basic rate, unless it reasonably believes a treaty lower rate applies, as chapter twenty three explains. Calder's royalty from Vallaria bears Vallarian withholding of five per cent, sixty thousand pounds a year, and the credit for it, and its limit when the Patent Box has already cut the United Kingdom tax, is chapter twenty four's business.

Finally, the corporate interest restriction in chapter twenty eight. Its measure of taxable earnings, tax E B I T D A, which stands for earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, ignores Patent Box deductions. It also ignores intangibles amortisation, including four per cent write-downs, and losses on disposals of intangibles. Realisation credits are treated more narrowly. A credit is left out only so far as it claws back earlier tax debits, which means up to the asset's cost less its tax written-down value. Think of it as amortisation in, amortisation out. H M R C's own summary sounds wider, but the statute is the test. Calder's know-how and customer contracts had no tax cost and no past debits, so its six million pound credit is a gain over cost. It raises both taxable profit and interest capacity. Chapter twenty eight works through the effect on Tarnmoor's figures.

How the examiner tests it.

Intangible fixed assets are core in the twenty twenty six grid, grade one, after moving up from non-core. They have not appeared as a standalone question in the sittings from May twenty twenty three to May twenty twenty six. They appear inside computations, as in November twenty twenty three, question four. In the twenty twenty eight grid they fall back to non-core, but your sitting uses the current grid. Patent Box is grade three and has not appeared recently.

The style is technical prose and computation, with marks of half a mark to one mark per point. A typical requirement says calculate, with explanations, the tax-adjusted trading profit, and an acquisition of a business with goodwill and intellectual property is dropped into the facts. The marks come from explaining each adjustment. Say why the goodwill qualifies, or why it does not. State the six point five per cent rate and its source. Test the cap with a short calculation. Show whether the amortisation in the accounts is added back, and what is deducted instead. The examiners reported that question four of November twenty twenty three was very well answered overall, so the marks in this area are there to be banked by those who know the rules exactly.

For Patent Box, awareness means you should be able to spot that an election would help a company with profits from patents, state the ten per cent effective rate and how the deduction works, and name the main conditions. You will not be asked to stream profits.

The traps are predictable. First, the three goodwill restrictions and the six times cap, and the six point five per cent rate, not four per cent and not the accounts. Second, the four per cent election deadline, two years after the end of the period of acquisition, and that it is irrevocable. Third, tax-neutral transfers need the asset to be chargeable in both companies. Fourth, the degrouping charge falls on the leaving company, uses market value at the time of the transfer, and is switched off by a share sale qualifying for the shareholding exemption, the twin of the gains rule. Fifth, a realisation of a home-grown asset taxes the whole proceeds. And since the Finance Act twenty twenty six, a cross-border related party transfer uses the arm's length price.

What to take away.

Intangible fixed assets are taxed as income, following the accounts. That is Part eight of the Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, for assets created or acquired from April two thousand and two, and for all acquisitions from the first of July, twenty twenty.

Write-downs follow the accounts, unless the company elects for four per cent a year. The election must be made within two years after the end of the period of acquisition, and it is irrevocable. It helps most for assets the accounts never amortise.

A realisation gives a credit of the proceeds less the tax written down value, or less cost if the asset was never written down. A home-grown asset has no tax cost, so the whole of the proceeds is taxed. In our invented case, Calder's sale of know-how and customer contracts to Tarnmoor Vallaria gave a credit of six million pounds and one point five million pounds of tax.

Goodwill and customer-related assets acquired from April twenty nineteen are relieved at six point five per cent a year, but only if acquired with a business that includes qualifying intellectual property, and only up to six times that intellectual property spend. There is no relief where the business had no qualifying intellectual property, as for Tarnmoor Ireland's three million pound customer list. Buying shares never gives relief for the goodwill inside.

Reinvestment relief defers the profit above original cost where proceeds are reinvested in capitalised intangibles from twelve months before to three years after. Write-downs are always clawed back.

Inside a seventy five per cent group, a transfer is tax-neutral only if the asset is chargeable in both companies' hands. A transfer to an overseas company outside the United Kingdom net is a realisation.

If a company leaves the group within six years holding such an asset, it bears a degrouping charge at the market value at the time of the transfer. The charge is switched off where it leaves through a share sale that qualifies for the shareholding exemption. That saved Tarnmoor Actuators a two point eight million pound credit on its patents.

Between related parties, transfers are at market value. Since the first of January, twenty twenty six, cross-border transfers subject to transfer pricing are at the arm's length price instead.

The Patent Box taxes relevant intellectual property profits at an effective ten per cent, through a deduction of three fifths of those profits at the twenty five per cent main rate. For Calder, nine hundred thousand pounds of relevant profits gave a deduction of five hundred and forty thousand pounds and tax of ninety thousand pounds.

All of this is the law for the financial year twenty twenty six.

The intangibles code showed tax following the accounts for things you cannot touch. Chapter twelve, on loan relationships and derivatives, follows the accounts for the most intangible thing of all, money owed and money promised.

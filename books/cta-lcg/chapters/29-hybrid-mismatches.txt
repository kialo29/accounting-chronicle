Chapter twenty nine: Hybrid mismatches.

The slide deck arrived in Group Year Four. In our invented story, an outside adviser had a proposal for the group's treasury company, Tarnmoor Finance. It would issue eighty million pounds of perpetual notes to Tarnmoor Capital, the group's finance company in the invented country of Marrovia. The coupon would be seven per cent, five point six million pounds a year.

The adviser's pitch rested on one fact. The United Kingdom would see the notes as debt, so the coupon would be deductible here. Marrovia, under its invented law, would see the same notes as shares, so the coupon would arrive as an exempt dividend. One payment, two readings. A deduction in one country, and nothing taxed in the other. The project even had a name. Project Undertow.

Tom Hesketh, the group's head of tax, read the deck and recognised it at once. This was exactly the kind of arrangement that the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, the O E C D, had set out to stop. On the fifth of October, twenty fifteen, it published its final report on neutralising the effects of hybrid mismatch arrangements. The United Kingdom turned that report into law from the first of January, twenty seventeen.

So this chapter asks one question. When two countries describe the same payment differently, which of them gets to tax it, and what happens if neither does?

This is the eighth chapter of Part Four, Across Borders. Hybrid mismatches are core material on the Advanced Technical paper under the current syllabus grid, although they fall to non-core from twenty twenty eight. The Living Law only signposted them, so we build from first principles. The law throughout is that for the financial year twenty twenty six.

One coin, two readings.

Start with the problem. Every tax system has to decide what a payment is. Interest or dividend. A person or a pass-through. Here or there. Each country answers by its own law, and the answers need not match.

Picture one coin tossed between two countries. If both call it heads, both give a deduction for the same expense. If both call it tails, neither taxes the income. A hybrid mismatch is that gap. The rules exist to make one country call the coin fairly.

The O E C D's work came out of its base erosion and profit shifting project, which everyone calls B E P S. Action two of that project dealt with hybrids. Its key choice was to target outcomes, not motives. The rules ask what happened to the payment. Was it deducted here and not included there? Was one expense deducted twice? They do not start by asking why.

The O E C D named two outcomes. The first is the deduction non-inclusion mismatch, which practitioners shorten to D N I. A payment is deductible for the payer, but the payee does not bring it in as ordinary income. The second is the double deduction. The same amount is deductible in two territories.

Its second key choice was a pair of responses. A primary response tells one country to act first, usually the payer's. A secondary response tells the other country to act if the first does not. Whichever country moves, the mismatch is neutralised once, and only once.

The United Kingdom already had rules aimed at this territory. Anti-arbitrage rules arrived in the Finance Number Two Act two thousand and five and were later rewritten into the Taxation, International and Other Provisions, Act twenty ten, which I will call the International Act. Those rules did nothing until H M R C served a notice. They were repealed for accounting periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty seventeen.

Their replacement is Part six A of the International Act, inserted by the Finance Act twenty sixteen. It applies to payments made on or after the first of January, twenty seventeen. And unlike its predecessor, it needs no notice. A company must apply it in its own return.

That is the shift to remember. From rules that H M R C switched on, to rules that switch themselves on.

The vocabulary of Part six A.

Part six A has its own dictionary, and the exam rewards using it precisely.

Tax, for these rules, means income tax and corporation tax on income in the United Kingdom, and taxes on income abroad. It also includes the controlled foreign company charge, which I will call the C F C charge, and foreign C F C charges. Withholding tax is ignored. So a payment that suffers withholding but is never taxed as income is still not included.

Ordinary income is income brought into account in calculating the profits on which tax is charged. Income that is exempt or excluded is not ordinary income. Income taxed at a reduced rate because of an instrument's terms is included, but under-taxed, and the law scales it.

A hybrid entity is an entity that one territory regards as a person, while another territory treats its income as someone else's. A partnership seen as a company in one country is the obvious case. So is a company that its foreign parent treats as transparent.

Most chapters apply only between related parties, or under a structured arrangement. Two parties are related if they are in the same control group, or if one has a twenty five per cent investment in the other, or a third person has a twenty five per cent investment in both. A control group broadly means companies consolidated together, or linked by the transfer pricing participation test, or by a fifty per cent investment.

A structured arrangement catches unrelated parties. The law asks whether it is reasonable to suppose the arrangement was designed to secure the mismatch, or that its terms share the mismatch's economic benefit between the parties. A commercial objective does not save it. Notice what this means. For related parties there is no purpose test at all.

Dual inclusion income is income taxed in both of the relevant territories. Many responses let a deduction survive only against dual inclusion income, because a deduction set against income taxed twice gives no one a windfall.

Timing gets its own concept. A permitted taxable period is broadly one that begins within twelve months after the end of the payer's period. Income included that late still counts.

Last, the law fixes an order, because one payment can trigger several chapters. Chapter four, on hybrid transfers, goes first, then chapter three, on financial instruments, then chapters five to ten in turn.

A misconception belongs here. People assume the hybrid rules are anti-avoidance rules with a motive test. Between related parties they are mechanical. If the mismatch exists and arises from the hybrid feature, the counteraction follows.

Financial instruments.

Chapter three of Part six A is the one the examiner meets most often, and it is Project Undertow's chapter. It deals with a deduction non-inclusion mismatch arising from the terms or other features of a financial instrument.

The conditions are these. There is a payment under a financial instrument. The payer is within the charge to corporation tax, or the payee is. The mismatch arises because of the instrument's terms or features. And the payer and payee are related, or the arrangement is structured.

There are two cases. In the first, the deduction exceeds the ordinary income that arises to the payee in a permitted taxable period. The classic example is a coupon the payee's country treats as an exempt dividend. In the second, the income is included, but under-taxed, because of the instrument's features.

The primary response falls on the payer. Where the payer is a United Kingdom company, its deduction is reduced by the amount of the mismatch.

The secondary response applies where the United Kingdom is the payee's country and the payer's country has not counteracted. Then the United Kingdom payee is taxed on the relevant amount as income.

Here is the first case in miniature. Suppose, for this example only, a United Kingdom company pays a one million pound coupon to its parent abroad. The parent's country treats the instrument as equity and exempts the receipt. The whole one million pounds is a mismatch. The deduction is denied, and that costs two hundred and fifty thousand pounds of corporation tax.

Now the second case, which is harder. Suppose instead the parent's country taxes the coupon, but at a special rate of five per cent, where its full marginal rate is twenty per cent. The law measures the mismatch by scaling the under-taxed amount. Multiply it by the full rate less the actual rate, and divide by the full rate.

So one million pounds, times fifteen, divided by twenty, gives a mismatch of seven hundred and fifty thousand pounds. The United Kingdom payer keeps a deduction of only two hundred and fifty thousand pounds. The tax cost of the counteraction is one hundred and eighty seven thousand, five hundred pounds. The full working is in the reading edition.

Why scale it that way? Because the income was taxed at a quarter of the full rate. So a quarter of the payment is treated as properly included, and three quarters as not.

Timing can also create a first case mismatch. Suppose the payer accrues interest and deducts it each year, but the payee's country taxes it only when it is paid, many years later. If the income is included in a permitted taxable period, beginning within twelve months after the end of the payer's period, there is no mismatch. If it comes later, the deduction is denied for now. When the income finally arrives, the law lets the position be adjusted, so the relief is delayed, not lost.

Chapter four applies the same thinking to hybrid transfers, such as repos and stock lending. There, two countries disagree about who owns the transferred instrument, so both may give relief, or neither may tax a return. The payer again responds first, and the payee second.

One boundary matters. The mismatch must come from the instrument. If the payee is simply exempt because of who it is, a pension fund or a sovereign body perhaps, the shortfall is not a hybrid mismatch.

When the United Kingdom is the payee.

So far the United Kingdom has mostly been the payer. Turn the coin over. Suppose a United Kingdom company receives a payment that its foreign payer deducts, but which the United Kingdom would treat as an exempt dividend.

The first line of defence is not in Part six A at all. It sits in the dividend exemption in Part nine A of the Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, which I will call the two thousand and nine Act. A distribution received by a large company is exempt only if, among other conditions, no deduction is allowed for it abroad. The test is whether a resident of any territory outside the United Kingdom gets a deduction under that territory's law. Small companies face the same test.

That is a linking rule. If the payer abroad gets a deduction, the United Kingdom recipient loses the exemption, and the receipt is taxed. The mismatch closes from the payee's side, without the hybrid rules lifting a finger.

H M R C's guidance explains how this sits with Part six A. Where a payment is deductible for the payer but treated as a distribution in the payee's country, the two sets of rules overlap. There is no statutory order, but the distribution exemption may be considered first. And where another country has an equivalent linking rule and taxes the receipt, the United Kingdom will not usually deny the payer's deduction. H M R C refers such cases to a specialist team.

The Undertow facts show the reverse. Marrovia, in our invented case, has no linking rule. It exempts the coupon whatever happens in the United Kingdom. That is precisely why the United Kingdom, as payer's country, must make the primary response.

Where the United Kingdom payee is caught by the hybrid rules themselves, the secondary response in chapter three applies. If the payer's country has not counteracted, the United Kingdom payee is charged on the relevant amount as income, in addition to whatever it already includes.

The lesson for an answer is to check both ends of the payment. Ask how the payer's country treats it and how the payee's country treats it. Then ask which country's rules respond first. A good answer names both countries and both rules, even where only one of them in the end bites.

Hybrid entities, payees and establishments.

The next group of chapters deals with mismatches caused not by the instrument but by the entity in the middle.

Chapter five covers the hybrid payer. A company pays, and its own country gives a deduction. But the investor's country treats the payer as transparent, so it disregards the payment altogether. The payee, often the investor itself, includes nothing. The response is to let the hybrid payer deduct the payment only against dual inclusion income. Any surplus waits. If the payee is in the United Kingdom and the payer's country does nothing, the payee is taxed instead.

Chapter six deals with transparent establishments, meaning permanent establishments. A United Kingdom permanent establishment of a foreign company may claim deductions for deemed payments to its own head office. If the head office's country ignores those internal payments, there is a mismatch. The establishment's deduction is limited to dual inclusion income, with the excess carried forward.

Chapter seven covers the hybrid payee. Here the payee is the hybrid. A reverse hybrid is the textbook case. Its own country treats it as transparent, while its investors' country treats it as a company, so nobody taxes what it receives. The payer's deduction is the primary target. A United Kingdom investor may be taxed as the secondary response. The Finance Act twenty twenty two widened this chapter, with retrospective effect, to cover other transparent entities as well as partnerships.

H M R C's guidance adds a useful point here. Where a payment to a reverse hybrid is caught by a C F C charge, that charge can count as inclusion, so no mismatch may remain. Hold on to that point. It comes back in Project Undertow.

Chapter eight covers the multinational payee. A company receives a payment through a permanent establishment that no country taxes, because the head office country exempts branch profits and the branch country does not recognise the branch. The payer's deduction is reduced.

Notice the pattern across all four chapters. Identify the hybrid element. Measure the mismatch. Then apply the response the chapter gives, in the right country.

Double deductions.

The second family of mismatches is the double deduction. One expense, two deductions.

Chapter nine deals with hybrid entities. A company, say a United Kingdom subsidiary, is a taxable person here but transparent to its foreign parent. Its expenses are deducted in the United Kingdom, and they also flow up and are deducted against the parent's income abroad.

The primary response falls on the investor. Where the investor is in the United Kingdom, its deduction is allowed only against dual inclusion income. The secondary response falls on the hybrid entity. If the investor's country does not counteract, and the two are in the same control group or the arrangement is structured, the United Kingdom restricts the hybrid entity's own deduction.

Here is the arithmetic. Suppose, for this example only, a United Kingdom company is owned by a parent in a country with no hybrid rules. That country treats the United Kingdom company as transparent. The United Kingdom company has two million pounds of expenses, deductible in both countries. It also has one point four million pounds of income taxed in both, which is dual inclusion income.

The United Kingdom applies the secondary response. The company deducts one point four million pounds now. The other six hundred thousand pounds is carried forward, to be set against future dual inclusion income. This year's tax is one hundred and fifty thousand pounds higher, but the relief is deferred, not destroyed. The law also allows stranded deductions to be released in some cases, where no more dual inclusion income can arise.

Chapter ten applies the same idea to dual resident companies and to companies with permanent establishments abroad. If an amount is deductible both in the United Kingdom and elsewhere, the United Kingdom restricts it to dual inclusion income.

Dual inclusion income can be lumpy. One company in a group may have more than it needs while another has too little. The Finance Act twenty twenty one added chapter twelve A, which lets a group allocate surplus dual inclusion income from one company to another. It applies for claimant accounting periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty one.

The tax function's job here is tracking. Dual inclusion income is not a figure in the accounts. Someone must compute it every year, by company and by entity, and keep the carried-forward amounts alive.

Imported mismatches.

Chapter eleven reaches further. It asks whether a United Kingdom payment funds a mismatch that happens somewhere else.

Picture a chain. A United Kingdom company pays interest to a group company abroad. That company, in turn, makes a hybrid payment to a third territory, where a mismatch arises. The United Kingdom payment is ordinary, but it feeds the mismatch. Chapter eleven denies the United Kingdom deduction to the extent of the mismatch it funds, where the parties are in the same control group or the arrangement is structured.

An example helps, with every country invented. A United Kingdom company borrows from a group finance company in country A, which has hybrid rules matching the O E C D's. The finance company funds itself with a hybrid instrument from a group company in country B, which has no such rules. Country A can counteract that hybrid itself, so the United Kingdom need do nothing. But if country A had no way to counteract it, the United Kingdom deduction would be at risk, because the United Kingdom money feeds the mismatch.

As first enacted, this chapter was very wide. It could reach a mismatch that another country's own hybrid rules were already dealing with. The Finance Act twenty twenty one narrowed it, generally from the tenth of June, twenty twenty one. Now it bites only on a mismatch that is not capable of being counteracted under the law of a territory whose hybrid rules match the O E C D's.

That twenty twenty one package was the United Kingdom correcting its own over-reach. It also carved out certain debt relief situations, and dealt with transparent funds. Since then, Part six A has not been amended by the Finance Act twenty twenty five or the Finance Act twenty twenty six.

Chapter thirteen of Part six A adds a targeted anti-avoidance rule, for arrangements designed to escape the other chapters. And a counteraction can be revisited, under chapter twelve, if income later turns out to be included after all.

Project Undertow.

Back to Group Year Four, and Tom Hesketh's memo for the board. Everything in it is part of our invented story.

The adviser's paper made a simple claim. Tarnmoor Finance would deduct five point six million pounds a year, saving one point four million pounds of corporation tax. Marrovia would exempt the coupon as a dividend. To fund the notes, Tarnmoor plc would subscribe eighty million pounds of new shares in Tarnmoor Capital.

The paper admitted one cost. Tarnmoor Capital is a controlled foreign company, so a C F C charge would follow. But the adviser assumed the finance company exemption, which taxes only a quarter of qualifying loan profits. On that basis the charge would be three hundred and fifty thousand pounds, and the net saving one point zero five million pounds a year.

Tom's memo started somewhere the deck had skipped. Would the United Kingdom even see debt?

Perpetual notes held by a company associated with the issuer are what the distribution rules in the Corporation Tax Act twenty ten call equity notes. Interest on them is treated as a distribution. A distribution is not deductible at all. H M R C's own manual gives perpetual securities as its example.

The adviser had an answer. The notes would let Tarnmoor Finance defer or cancel the coupon, they would have no other significant equity features, and Tarnmoor Finance would elect to treat them as hybrid capital instruments. An instrument within that regime is not an equity note, so its coupon stays interest.

But the rules give the election no effect where arrangements have a main purpose of obtaining a tax advantage. Undertow had no other purpose. So Tom's first conclusion was blunt. There was a serious risk that the coupon would be a distribution, with no deduction from the start.

Suppose the election stood. Then the hybrid rules took over.

Tom's memo.

Tom worked through the rules in the order a marker would want.

First, the hybrid rules. The payment is under a financial instrument. Tarnmoor Finance is within corporation tax. The mismatch arises from the notes' terms, because Marrovia reads their perpetual, subordinated features as equity. And the two companies are related, both wholly owned by Tarnmoor plc. Chapter three applies. The whole five point six million pounds is a mismatch, and the primary response falls on Tarnmoor Finance as payer. On the hybrid rules alone, the deduction worth one point four million pounds is denied.

Second, withholding. If the coupon is interest, it is yearly interest paid by a company. The notes would not be listed, so they are not quoted Eurobonds. Marrovia has no treaty with the United Kingdom. And the private placement exemption needs a lender in a territory with a suitable treaty, and a security entered into for genuine commercial reasons. So the withholding rules in Part fifteen of the Income Tax Act two thousand and seven would require Tarnmoor Finance to deduct income tax at twenty per cent, one point one two million pounds a year. Coming next, the rate becomes twenty two per cent from twenty twenty seven to twenty eight, which would make it one point two three two million pounds.

Third, the C F C charge. The finance company exemption needs a qualifying loan relationship, and a loan to a United Kingdom debtor is not one. The coupon would come from capital invested from the United Kingdom, so it would pass the gateway. The charge on Tarnmoor plc would be twenty five per cent of five point six million pounds, one point four million pounds. The income tax withheld counts as creditable tax, leaving two hundred and eighty thousand pounds to pay.

Now the interaction. H M R C's guidance says income that has suffered a C F C charge may be treated as ordinary income. It is treated as the income of a company holding at least a quarter of the controlled foreign company, to the extent the statute allows. On that reading, the C F C charge could stand in for the missing Marrovian tax, and the deduction might survive. Tom's memo did not need to settle the point, because neither answer helps.

Fourth, the other gates. Transfer pricing would ask whether an independent finance company would issue perpetual notes at seven per cent at all. The corporate interest restriction counts interest paid to group companies abroad. In a year when the group's interest was already restricted, as in Group Years One and Two, the extra five point six million pounds would simply be disallowed. And section four four one, the unallowable purpose rule of the Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, would ask why Tarnmoor Finance was party to the notes. The only answer was tax.

So the memo set out the outcomes. If the coupon is a distribution, there is no deduction at all, and the scheme has nothing to offer. If the deduction survives because the C F C charge counts as inclusion, the one point four million pound saving is matched by one point four million pounds of withholding and C F C charge. If any rule denies the deduction while the C F C charge still bites, the group pays one point four million pounds a year more than if it had done nothing.

The best case was break-even. The worst case was a loss every year, plus the cost of breaking the group's published promise not to use tax avoidance schemes. The board rejected Undertow in a single meeting.

The minute recorded three reasons. The scheme could not beat doing nothing. It would have created an annual dispute with H M R C over several regimes at once. And it conflicted with the tax strategy. Tarnmoor Capital went on lending to Tarnmoor Vallaria, as before, and Tarnmoor Finance's funding stayed plain.

Notice how Tom built the memo. He did not stop when he found the first problem. A board needs to know whether a scheme fails on one ground or on six, because a scheme that fails on one ground invites the adviser to come back with a fix. A scheme that fails on six does not. That is also how an examiner rewards an answer about a cross-border financing. Each regime is a separate set of marks.

A low rate is not a hybrid.

Undertow worked as a test case because it had a genuine hybrid element. Many questions do not, and that is where candidates go wrong.

The examiners reported, after the November twenty twenty five sitting, that some candidates thought a low-tax territory on its own made something a hybrid, and wrote generic anti-avoidance answers instead. A payment to a company taxed at nine per cent is not a hybrid mismatch. If it is fully included in that company's income, there is no mismatch, however low the rate.

The low rate is a matter for other regimes. Transfer pricing tests the price. The C F C rules test whether profits have been diverted from the United Kingdom. The global minimum tax in chapter thirty tops up low effective rates. The hybrid rules have a narrower job. They catch a difference in characterisation, and only that.

So the first question in any hybrid answer is always, where is the hybrid? Is there an instrument two countries read differently? An entity one sees as a person and the other sees through? A permanent establishment one country recognises and the other ignores? A company resident in two places? If you cannot point to one, the rules do not apply, and the examiners have twice criticised candidates who raised them anyway.

The interactions follow a sequence, and H M R C has said how it sees it. Its guidance says it would expect the hybrid rules to apply before the corporate interest restriction. It also says transfer pricing and the hybrid rules must be applied together. Companies cannot compare them and pick the better answer, or choose the order. The arm's length test is run with the hybrid rules fully applied to both the actual and the arm's length position, and then the hybrid rules are checked again.

The unallowable purpose rule stands alongside, and an amount it disallows cannot come back in under any other rule.

Administration and the tax function.

Because the rules are self-executing, the work falls on the company.

The counteraction goes into the corporation tax computation. A denied deduction is added back, and any taxable amount under a secondary response is brought in as income. There is no H M R C notice to wait for.

The return asks about it. The supplementary pages called C T six hundred B cover controlled foreign companies, the foreign branch exemption, and hybrid and other mismatches. They ask whether the company has made a counteraction under Part six A, the totals counteracted, and any claims to allocate or consent to an allocation of surplus dual inclusion income. They are completed only where a company has something to report.

The tax function's tasks follow. Keep a register of instruments and entities whose treatment may differ abroad. Track dual inclusion income and carried-forward restricted deductions, company by company. Ask overseas advisers how each payment is treated in the payee's country, because the United Kingdom answer often turns on foreign law. H M R C also offers non-statutory clarification on how the rules apply.

Acquisitions deserve special care. A target may hold instruments or entities that are harmless in its old group but hybrid in the new one, once the relationships change. Due diligence should map them before completion.

And one loose end is worth knowing. The definition of tax in Part six A still mentions diverted profits tax, at least in the text we checked. Yet that tax has been replaced by the unassessed transfer pricing profits charge, for accounting periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six. Chapter thirty tells that story.

Living with the rules.

The best hybrid planning is not to need the rules. A tax function in a group like Tarnmoor works on that basis.

Keep instruments plain. An intra-group loan that is debt everywhere, with a fixed term and an ordinary coupon, rarely creates a mismatch. Perpetual, subordinated or convertible features invite a different reading abroad. Before a group company issues anything unusual to a related party overseas, the tax function asks how the payee's country will treat it, and gets that answer in writing.

Watch entity elections. Some countries let a parent choose to treat a foreign subsidiary as transparent. That choice, made abroad for local reasons, can turn a plain United Kingdom company into a hybrid entity overnight. The double deduction and hybrid payer chapters then follow. So the group's register should record every entity whose classification differs between countries.

Remember who is related. A twenty five per cent investment is enough. Joint ventures and consortium companies, such as Helmside in our invented story, can be related to their investors for these purposes. And a company that joins or leaves the group changes its relationships on the day it moves.

Document the analysis. Where the answer depends on foreign law or on a judgement about whether a mismatch arises from an instrument's terms, the file should show who advised, what they said and when. Where a position is uncertain and large, the uncertain tax treatment rules in chapter four may require the group to notify H M R C.

And keep the ethics in view. Tarnmoor publishes a tax strategy saying it does not use tax avoidance schemes. Undertow would have broken that promise even if every computation had worked. Tom's memo said so, in one line, at the top.

How the examiner tests it.

Hybrids have appeared once as a whole question in recent sittings. November twenty twenty five, question six, was a ten mark discursive question. It covered hybrid entities and instruments, deduction non-inclusion and double deduction mismatches, primary and secondary responses, and the return. The answers were mixed.

The other appearance was a warning. In November twenty twenty four, a question about a group buying a United Kingdom company carried no hybrid trigger, yet some candidates brought hybrids in anyway.

Expect hybrids to arrive alongside other regimes. The favourite companions on this paper are transfer pricing, the interest restriction and the C F C rules, exactly the regimes Undertow touched. When a scenario describes an intra-group financing across a border, run the same checklist Tom ran. Is it debt under United Kingdom law? Is there a hybrid element? What does transfer pricing say about the amount? Does the interest restriction bite? Is there withholding? Does a C F C charge follow? Is there an unallowable purpose?

So the technique is this. Find the hybrid element and name it. Name the mismatch, deduction non-inclusion or double deduction. Name the chapter. Say which country responds first and what the response is. Compute the amount if numbers are given, including dual inclusion income and carry forward. Finish with the administration. Keep it tight, because a ten mark question earns about twenty one minutes.

What to take away.

Hybrid mismatches arise when two countries characterise the same payment, entity or establishment differently. The O E C D's final report of the fifth of October, twenty fifteen, targeted outcomes, not motives. The United Kingdom's rules are in Part six A of the International Act, for payments made on or after the first of January, twenty seventeen. They replaced notice-based arbitrage rules and apply automatically.

There are two outcomes. A deduction non-inclusion mismatch is a deduction here with no ordinary income there. A double deduction is one amount deducted twice. Primary and secondary responses make sure the mismatch is neutralised once.

Most chapters need related parties, meaning a control group or a twenty five per cent investment, or a structured arrangement. Between related parties there is no purpose test. Withholding tax is ignored, and a C F C charge counts as tax.

Chapter three covers financial instruments. The payer's deduction is reduced first, and the payee is taxed second. An under-taxed coupon is scaled. One million pounds taxed at five per cent against a full rate of twenty per cent gives a mismatch of seven hundred and fifty thousand pounds.

Chapters five, seven and eight cover hybrid payers, hybrid payees and multinational payees. Chapter six covers permanent establishments. Chapters nine and ten cover double deductions, with deductions allowed against dual inclusion income and the rest carried forward. Two million pounds of expenses against one point four million pounds of dual inclusion income leaves six hundred thousand pounds to carry forward. Chapter twelve A allows surplus dual inclusion income to move around a group.

Chapter eleven covers imported mismatches, narrowed in twenty twenty one to mismatches not capable of counteraction in a territory with matching rules. Part six A was not changed by the Finance Acts of twenty twenty five or twenty twenty six.

In our invented case, Project Undertow failed every test. Perpetual notes held by an associated company risk being equity notes, and the hybrid capital instrument election fails where tax is a main purpose. If the coupon is interest, the hybrid rules deny a deduction worth one point four million pounds. Withholding at twenty per cent takes one point one two million pounds. A full C F C charge applies, because a loan to a United Kingdom debtor is not a qualifying loan relationship. Transfer pricing, the interest restriction and the unallowable purpose rule all wait behind. The best case was break-even, the worst a loss of one point four million pounds a year.

And remember the examiners' lesson. A low tax rate is not a hybrid. No hybrid element, no hybrid answer.

The global minimum tax deals with the low rates themselves, and it is where corporation tax goes next. Chapter thirty turns to the global minimum and the end of diverted profits tax.

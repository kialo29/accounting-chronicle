Chapter two: Where corporate tax law lives.

On the eighteenth of March, twenty twenty six, the Finance Act twenty twenty six received Royal Assent. Sixteen years earlier to the day, on the eighteenth of March, twenty ten, the Taxation, International and Other Provisions, Act twenty ten had received its own. That earlier Act was the last product of a fourteen year project to rewrite the United Kingdom's direct tax statutes in a clearer, better ordered form. Its aim was to restate the law, not to change it.

The twenty twenty six Act went a step further than any rewrite Act. In its transfer pricing and permanent establishment rules, it points to texts written by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, the O E C D, and it tells us to read them as they stand from time to time, not as they stood on the day Parliament voted.

So where does corporate tax law actually live? In Acts and regulations, certainly. But also in treaties, in accounting standards that Parliament never wrote, in judgments, and now in international texts that change without a vote at Westminster. Around all of that sits a large body of H M R C guidance that is not law at all, yet shapes every enquiry.

This is the second chapter of Part One, and it is the book's how we know chapter. The Living Law, chapter two, explained statute, interpretation, the tribunals and the status of guidance. Here we go further, into the sources a larger company's tax function uses every week. No single row of the syllabus grid is owned by this chapter. But the grid treats the application of the O E C D Model and supplied treaty extracts as core, and the impact of accounting standards on taxable profits as core too. We finish in the exam room, with what the screen gives you and what it does not.

The statute book a group lives in.

Start with the Acts. As The Living Law, chapter two, explained, statute is the law, and everything else either interprets it or applies it. For a larger company, the statute book is a handful of big Acts, and most of them come from the Tax Law Rewrite Project. That project began with a blueprint paper in July nineteen ninety six. It produced seven Acts, from the Capital Allowances Act two thousand and one to the International Act in twenty ten. Its explanatory notes say the rewritten Acts do not generally change the meaning of the law. They restate it, in a new order.

Here is the map, and the names I will use for each statute.

The Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, which I will call the two thousand and nine Act, is the computing Act. It contains the charge to corporation tax and company residence, the profits of permanent establishments, the foreign branch exemption, trading income, loan relationships, derivative contracts, intangible fixed assets and research and development relief.

The Corporation Tax Act twenty ten, which I will call the twenty ten Act, is the reliefs and structure Act. Losses, group relief, the loss restriction, change of ownership, distributions and demergers live there, with the definitions the rest of the code borrows, including the meaning of a permanent establishment and of control.

The Taxation, International and Other Provisions, Act twenty ten, which I will call the International Act, holds the cross-border and anti-avoidance machinery. Double tax relief, transfer pricing, advance pricing agreements, hybrid mismatches, controlled foreign companies and the corporate interest restriction all sit there. The twenty twenty six Act added a new Part for unassessed transfer pricing profits.

Then come the older Acts that were never rewritten. The Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act nineteen ninety two, the Gains Act, governs company gains. The Taxes Management Act nineteen seventy, the Management Act, still carries much of the administration, alongside the company return rules in Schedule eighteen to the Finance Act nineteen ninety eight. The Capital Allowances Act completes the set. And the global minimum tax lives in the Finance Number Two Act twenty twenty three, which I will call the Pillar Two legislation.

Each year's Finance Act mostly amends these Acts, so the amended Acts are what you read. But some rules never move into the main Acts. The senior accounting officer rules still live in Schedule forty six to the Finance Act two thousand and nine. The tax strategy rules live in Schedule nineteen to the Finance Act twenty sixteen. The uncertain tax treatment rules live in Schedule seventeen to the Finance Act twenty twenty two. A researcher who searches only the Corporation Tax Acts will never find them.

Beneath the Acts sit statutory instruments, made by ministers under powers the Acts give them. They carry the same force of law and often hold the detail that costs money. Five matter all through this book. The instalment payment regulations decide when large and very large companies pay. The Disregard Regulations keep certain exchange and hedging movements out of taxable profit for the time being. The change of accounting practice regulations govern what happens to loan relationships and derivatives when a company changes its accounting framework. The country-by-country reporting regulations set the seven hundred and fifty million euros revenue threshold. And the transfer pricing records regulations require the master file and local file, for accounting periods beginning on or after the first of April, twenty twenty three.

Treaties arrive by yet another route. Section two of the International Act lets the Crown give effect to a double taxation agreement by Order in Council, and section five requires a draft of that Order to be approved by the House of Commons first. So a treaty is negotiated by governments, but it reaches a company's tax return only because Parliament has said it may.

When Parliament points at Paris.

For most of the history of transfer pricing in this country, the O E C D's Transfer Pricing Guidelines entered U K law through a narrow, fixed door. Section one six four of the International Act told the reader to construe the transfer pricing rules consistently with the guidelines. But the guidelines meant one particular version, frozen in time, and a newer version counted only when the Treasury designated it by order. The twenty seventeen guidelines were designated in twenty eighteen, for accounting periods beginning on or after the first of April that year. The twenty twenty two guidelines were designated later, with effect for corporation tax accounting periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty three.

The Finance Act twenty twenty six opened the door wide. For chargeable periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six, the law refers to the O E C D's materials in three places, and in the two most important it refers to them as amended or replaced from time to time.

The first is transfer pricing. Section one six four now refers to Article nine of the O E C D Model Tax Convention, in the version approved by the O E C D Council on the eighteenth of November, twenty twenty five, and to the Transfer Pricing Guidelines published on the twentieth of January, twenty twenty two.

The second is the attribution of profits to a permanent establishment. New subsections in section twenty of the two thousand and nine Act require attribution to be read consistently with Article seven of the twenty twenty five Model and its Commentary, the O E C D's twenty ten Report on the Attribution of Profits to Permanent Establishments, the twenty twenty two Guidelines and further guidance published in March twenty eighteen. Chapter twenty three calls the method the authorised O E C D approach and owns it.

The third is the definition of a permanent establishment itself. A new purpose clause, section eleven forty A of the twenty ten Act, ties the whole domestic definition to Article five of the twenty twenty five Model and its Commentary.

In the transfer pricing and attribution rules, the reference is to the documents as later amended, subject to the reservations the United Kingdom has entered, and the Treasury may make regulations so that a particular update does not apply. For transfer pricing, H M R C's International Manual says the Model and the Guidelines are now interpretative aids whether or not a treaty is in place, and the references are to their most recent versions.

Think of the statute as the rulebook and the O E C D materials as the official commentary that the rulebook now tells the referee to read. The referee still applies the rulebook. But when a rule could bear two meanings, the referee must prefer the one that fits the commentary, and the commentary is updated in Paris.

Why would Parliament do this? Because transfer pricing and permanent establishment disputes are fought between tax authorities, under treaties, through mutual agreement procedures. A U K rule that drifted from the international reading would invite double taxation. The cost is a loss of control. Parliament no longer votes on each change of reading, so the Treasury's power to switch off an update is the safeguard.

That brings us to this chapter's misconception. It runs like this. The O E C D cannot legislate for the United Kingdom, its documents call themselves guidelines and commentaries, so they are just guidance, like H M R C's manuals, to be quoted when helpful and ignored when not.

People believe it for good reasons. The O E C D is not a parliament. And for most of the corporation tax code it remains true that nothing outside the statute and the cases binds you.

For transfer pricing it has not been true for a long time, because section one six four has long required consistency with the designated Guidelines. What the twenty twenty six Act adds is reach and currency. The permanent establishment rules now carry the same kind of instruction, and the transfer pricing reference follows the texts as they change. Ignoring them is not choosing a different view of the guidance. It is misreading the statute. An argument about the arm's length price for a group's intra-group loan, or about how much capital a branch should be treated as having, must now be built from two things together, the words of the Act and the O E C D text the Act points to.

Two limits keep the picture honest. First, the O E C D texts guide interpretation. They do not override clear statutory words, and the United Kingdom's reservations and any Treasury opt-out still apply. Second, the texts are long and they change. This book could not open the O E C D's own website while it was being written, so where it describes the Guidelines it relies on the statute and on H M R C's descriptions, and says so.

There is also an exam wrinkle. The examination screen gives you the O E C D Model as a P D F of the twenty seventeen articles, while the twenty twenty six Act points to the version approved in twenty twenty five. In the exam, use what you are given, and use a supplied treaty extract in preference to either.

Treaties, and the judges who read them.

A double tax treaty is an agreement between two states. In the main it allocates taxing rights and limits what each state may take. It is not a free-standing source of tax charges. Its words were negotiated by officials, usually following the O E C D Model, and they are read differently from a U K statute. A treaty is meant to mean the same thing in both countries, so its words take an international meaning rather than a purely domestic one.

The leading modern authority is Fowler versus H M R C, decided by the Supreme Court on the twentieth of May, twenty twenty. Mr Fowler was a diver resident in South Africa who worked in the U K sector of the continental shelf. U K domestic law deems an employed diver to be carrying on a trade. Mr Fowler argued that the deeming carried through into the treaty, so that his income fell under the article for business profits, which South Africa alone could tax. The Supreme Court unanimously disagreed. Lord Briggs, giving the judgment, held that the domestic deeming rule did not change which treaty article applied. Treaty words take their ordinary international meaning, and the income fell under the employment article, so the United Kingdom could tax it.

On the O E C D Commentaries, Lord Briggs said they carry persuasive force, as the cogency of their reasoning deserves, even where a Commentary post-dates the treaty. That phrase is worth remembering. It describes the general position for treaties. The twenty twenty six Act goes further for transfer pricing and permanent establishments, because there the statute itself directs you to the O E C D texts.

Treaties also change in bulk now. The Multilateral Instrument, in force for the United Kingdom from the first of October, twenty eighteen, modifies many treaties at once, and H M R C publishes synthesised texts showing how each covered treaty reads after the modification. The U K and Ireland text is an example. One of the Instrument's most important additions is a principal purpose test. It denies a treaty benefit where obtaining it was one of the principal purposes of an arrangement, unless granting it would accord with the object and purpose of the treaty. So a tax function reading a treaty asks three questions. Which text applies after the Instrument? What do its words ordinarily mean in context? And could a purpose test take the benefit away? Chapter twenty four owns treaties in depth.

The accounts as a source of tax numbers.

The largest source of numbers in a corporation tax computation was not written by Parliament at all. It was written by accounting standard setters.

Section forty six of the two thousand and nine Act says that the profits of a trade must be calculated in accordance with generally accepted accounting practice, subject to any adjustment required or authorised by law. The loan relationship and derivative rules go further. The amounts brought into account are, in general, those recognised in profit or loss under that practice. The twenty ten Act defines generally accepted accounting practice to include both U K standards and international accounting standards. In practice that means the Financial Reporting Council's standards, chiefly Financial Reporting Standard one hundred and two and the reduced disclosure framework in Financial Reporting Standard one hundred and one, or full international standards for listed group accounts.

Why build tax on accounts? Because they exist already, they are audited, and they follow a consistent logic from year to year. A second, tax-only set of books for every company in a group would be costly and easy to manipulate. So the law starts from the accounts and then adjusts them where policy demands, replacing depreciation with capital allowances, for example, or refusing a deduction for entertaining.

The courts have generally held to this line. In H M R C versus N C L Investments, decided by the Supreme Court in twenty twenty two, H M R C argued that accounting debits for employee share options should be disallowed. It lost on every ground, and the debits followed the accounts. The line has limits, though. In G D F Suez Teesside versus H M R C, decided by the Court of Appeal in twenty eighteen, an older rule that the accounts must fairly represent the profits required a credit that compliant accounts did not show.

The consequence is easy to miss. When a standard changes, taxable profit can change without any Finance Act. Commentators at the Institute of Chartered Accountants in England and Wales have pointed out that the periodic review of Financial Reporting Standard one hundred and two, effective for periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six, brings most leases onto the balance sheet and can shift taxable profits for many companies. A tax function therefore reads accounting standard updates as closely as Finance Bills. Chapter five owns the detail, and chapter six shows how the tax charge itself is reported in the accounts.

H M R C's view, and asking first.

H M R C publishes thousands of pages of manuals. For a larger company the main ones are the International Manual, the Corporate Finance Manual, the Company Taxation Manual, the Corporate Intangibles Research and Development Manual, the Capital Gains Manual, the Business Income Manual, the Business Leasing Manual and the Senior Accounting Officers Guidance. Around them sit policy papers, consultation outcomes and tax information and impact notes, which explain why a measure was introduced.

Their status is simple to state. A manual is H M R C's view of the law. It is not law. A tribunal is not bound by it, and where it conflicts with the statute, the statute wins. Manuals also date. Some International Manual pages still carry a banner explaining that the transfer pricing rules were reformed and pointing to new draft guidance beginning at page four one four thousand, for periods from the first of January, twenty twenty six.

Yet for a larger company, H M R C's view has a sharper edge than for a small one. Under the uncertain tax treatment rules, a large business that takes a position contrary to H M R C's known position, including its published guidance, must notify H M R C if the tax at stake passes five million pounds. Chapter four tells that story. So the manuals are not law, but departing from them can create a legal duty to say so.

The practical lesson is to read the manual as the argument your inspector will make, test it against the statute and the cases, and record why you agree or disagree.

Sometimes the best source of law is an answer from H M R C in advance. There are three main routes.

Statutory clearances are written into particular Acts for transactions where the law turns on purpose. A demerger can be cleared under the twenty ten Act. A share exchange or reconstruction can be cleared under the Gains Act. Transactions in securities have their own clearance, with thirty days for H M R C to respond and thirty more if it asks for information. A statutory clearance binds H M R C on the facts disclosed.

The Non-Statutory Clearance Service covers other uncertainties. H M R C's guidance is firm that it gives written confirmation only where there is genuine uncertainty about how the law applies. It is not a confirmation service for positions you are already comfortable with, and it will not opine on tax planning or arrangements designed to avoid tax. Where a statutory clearance exists, that route comes first.

The newest route is the advance tax certainty service for major projects, in sections two hundred and sixty six to two hundred and seventy four of the Finance Act twenty twenty six. It is open to a qualifying investment project expected to involve at least one billion pounds of U K expenditure over its life. A clearance binds H M R C for up to five years, provided the facts and the law stay the same. H M R C opened the service on the first of July, twenty twenty six. Its published scope leaves out transfer pricing, valuations, purpose tests and the general anti-abuse rule.

For transfer pricing, the route to certainty is an advance pricing agreement under Part five of the International Act, which chapter twenty seven explains. The judgement call for a tax function is when to ask. A clearance costs time and discloses your thinking. Not asking leaves the risk on the balance sheet.

Case law, and the echo of Europe.

The tribunal ladder is as The Living Law, chapter two, described it. Appeals go to the First-tier Tribunal, then the Upper Tribunal, the Court of Appeal and the Supreme Court. A First-tier decision binds no later tribunal, though it can persuade. The Upper Tribunal and the courts above it make precedent.

Since April twenty twenty two, the National Archives has published judgments free of charge through its Find Case Law service, searchable by neutral citation, party and judge. Most of the corporate cases in this book, from BlackRock in the prologue to the unallowable purpose cases in chapter twelve, can be read there in full, and so can the First-tier Tribunal decisions, such as D S G Retail on transfer pricing, that this book cites. Read the judgment, not a summary. The paragraph that decides your point is rarely the one a newsletter quotes. Two habits help. Note the court, because a Court of Appeal judgment outranks a First-tier decision however recent. And check what happened next. A tribunal win can be reversed on appeal, and a pending appeal, such as Syngenta's before the Upper Tribunal on unallowable purpose, can change the answer.

Corporate tax law also carries the echo of European Union law. While the United Kingdom was a member state, the Court of Justice tested U K tax rules against freedom of establishment. In Cadbury Schweppes, decided on the twelfth of September, two thousand and six, it held that rules taxing profits of foreign subsidiaries could be justified only against wholly artificial arrangements. Marks and Spencer, the cross-border group relief litigation that The Living Law, chapter twenty four, described, shaped the rules on foreign losses. The present controlled foreign company regime was built in twenty twelve partly in answer to that case law. And its finance company exemption then spent years in a State aid dispute that ended, as reported, with the European Commission's decision being annulled by the Court of Justice in twenty twenty four.

Brexit has not erased that history. Since the end of twenty twenty three, the principle of the supremacy of European Union law is no longer part of U K domestic law, and what was retained European Union law is now called assimilated law. But the rules shaped by those judgments remain on the statute book, and the judgments still explain why they look as they do. Chapters twenty two, twenty five and twenty six return to them.

Reading the law on the right day.

A section of the International Act does not have one meaning. It has a meaning on a date, and the online text you read may not be the text that applies to your client's period.

The free official source is legislation dot gov dot uk. Each page carries a box headed Changes to legislation. It tells you the date up to which the text is up to date with changes known to be in force, and it lists effects that the editorial team has not yet applied. When that box shows outstanding effects, the text on your screen may be out of date. The site also keeps point-in-time versions. You can read section eleven forty one of the twenty ten Act as it stood on the thirty first of December, twenty twenty five, with the old dependent agent test, beside today's version with the new one.

Commencement wording then decides which text applies. Most of the twenty twenty six Act's international changes apply to chargeable periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six. Take three real companies, as an illustration outside our story. A company with a thirty first of December year end is on the new rules for its period starting on the first of January, twenty twenty six. A company with a thirty first of March year end stays on the old rules for the year to the thirty first of March, twenty twenty six, and moves to the new rules from the first of April. A company whose year began on the first of October, twenty twenty five, is on the old rules for the whole of that year, until the thirtieth of September, twenty twenty six.

The site also holds each Act as enacted, exactly as Parliament passed it. The revised text shows the law as amended. The enacted text shows what the amending Act did and from when. Commencement rules often sit at the end of a Schedule, far from the section you are reading. For the U K to U K exemption in our story, the date is in paragraph thirty two of Schedule six to the Finance Act twenty twenty six.

So the same section, read on the same day, can give different answers to three companies. The discipline is to ask three questions every time. Which version applies to this period? Has the site applied every change? And what does the Act as enacted say about commencement?

Tom Hesketh's question.

Now to our invented group, Tarnmoor, the listed U K engineering group we met in chapter one. Every company and person in it is invented. In the first weeks of Group Year One, its group head of tax, Tom Hesketh, receives a question from the chief financial officer, Nadia Kerr.

The group's treasury company, Tarnmoor Finance, has lent four hundred and thirty million pounds to four U K companies in the group, all at six per cent. Two hundred million pounds went to Tarnmoor Engineering, one hundred and twenty million to the parent, Tarnmoor plc, seventy million to Tarnmoor Estates and forty million to Tarnmoor Water Systems. That is twenty five point eight million pounds of interest a year. Tarnmoor Finance has also lent one hundred and twenty million pounds to the group's subsidiary in Vallaria, an invented country. And the parent has lent twenty million pounds, interest free, to Tarnmoor Ireland, which is incorporated in Ireland but, on our invented facts, managed from Leeds and so resident in the United Kingdom.

Tarnmoor's revenue is far above seven hundred and fifty million euros, so its U K companies already keep a master file and local file under the transfer pricing records regulations. Those regulations already leave transactions between two U K companies out of the local file. Nadia's question goes further. Does the pricing of the U K loans still have to be tested at all? Tom does not answer from memory, because the law on this point was rewritten by the twenty twenty six Act. He sets his team a research memo, and it follows the order of this chapter.

First, why the question arises at all. The transfer pricing rules once applied only to cross-border dealings. The Finance Act two thousand and four extended them to transactions between two U K companies, for chargeable periods beginning on or after the first of April, two thousand and four. That change followed a two thousand and two judgment of the European Court of Justice, Lankhorst-Hohorst, which held that national thin capitalisation rules could in principle breach freedom of establishment. The usual explanation is that the United Kingdom chose to apply its rules at home too. Treat that link as the common account, not something the statute says. Two decades later, the government's Corporate Tax Roadmap of twenty twenty four said that removing U K to U K transfer pricing would reduce the compliance burden. The Finance Act twenty twenty six did it.

Testing the exemption.

The team's memo works in five steps.

Step one is the statute. The exemption is section one six four A of the International Act, inserted by Schedule six to the Finance Act twenty twenty six. It switches off the transfer pricing adjustment for qualifying U K to U K provision.

Step two is commencement. The exemption applies for chargeable periods commencing on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six. In our story every period runs on the twenty twenty six law, so it is available from the start.

Step three is the conditions. Both parties must be U K resident companies within the charge to corporation tax on the relevant profits. They must pay at the same rate and use the same reference currency. The provision must not involve a one-sided derivative, a foreign branch exemption adjustment or patent box profits. And neither party may be an excluded company, a list that includes banks, real estate investment trusts, investment trusts, oil ring fence companies and qualifying asset holding companies. Two points go on the memo's list of things to check, not assume. Tarnmoor Finance is a treasury company that takes no deposits, so the team reads the statutory definition of a banking company rather than relying on the label. And the parent has no taxable profits in most years, so the team reads the exact wording of the rate condition before relying on it.

Step four is the off-switches. A company can elect, irrevocably, for the exemption not to apply. And H M R C can give a transfer pricing notice if that is needed to prevent a loss of tax, judged across both parties together, and normally only after opening an enquiry.

Step five is the guidance. H M R C's new International Manual pages on the U K to U K exemption explain the purpose. Where both parties are taxed in the United Kingdom at the same rate, compensating adjustments usually make the pricing tax neutral, so the paperwork protected nothing.

A labelled illustration shows why. Suppose an arm's length rate for Tarnmoor Engineering's loan had been five and a half per cent, not six. Under the old rules, its deduction would be cut by one million pounds, costing two hundred and fifty thousand pounds of tax. Tarnmoor Finance could then claim a matching reduction in its interest income, saving two hundred and fifty thousand pounds. The net result is nil.

The memo's conclusion, in our invented case, is that the four U K loans need no transfer pricing adjustment. The Vallarian loan stays fully inside the rules, which is chapter twenty seven's territory. The loan to Tarnmoor Ireland qualifies only while Tarnmoor Ireland stays U K resident. And the exemption switches off one rule, not the code. Unallowable purpose, in chapter twelve, and the corporate interest restriction still apply to every one of these loans.

Research in the exam room.

On the day of the exam, the sources shrink. The paper is closed book, sat on R M Assessment Master, the system that replaced the old software from the October twenty twenty six sitting. Behind an open book icon you will find three things. The tax tables, which are also on your desk in hard copy. The O E C D Model Convention, as a P D F of the twenty seventeen articles, available only on screen. And links to student legislation from Croner-i or Tolley, which you must buy in advance, because a work subscription will not log in.

You cannot make notes in either legislation product. You may highlight, and you may tag. A tag may carry a section number or a topic name. It may not carry a pro forma or a formula, and invigilators may check.

That makes navigation a skill worth practising now. Know where each regime lives, without needing to search. Start that practice well before the exam. Transfer pricing is Part four of the International Act, controlled foreign companies Part nine A, the corporate interest restriction Part ten with Schedule seven A, hybrids Part six A. Practise with the product you will use on the day, because each publisher lays out the Acts and its search differently. Tag the contents page of each Part and the handful of sections you will need under pressure, such as the transfer pricing interpretation rule, the U K to U K exemption and the permanent establishment definition in the twenty ten Act.

The examiners have also told candidates how to use treaty material. After the November twenty twenty three sitting, they reported that some candidates ignored the supplied treaty article and used the O E C D Model instead. When a question supplies an extract, the extract is the treaty for that question. Use the Model only when nothing else is given, and quote the article number so the marker can follow you.

The exam does not test your ability to find an obscure page of H M R C's manual, and it rewards statute over guidance. It does test whether you know which rule applies to which period, so state the law year you are applying. For a sitting in twenty twenty seven, that will almost certainly be the Finance Act twenty twenty six, and the twenty twenty seven tax tables, when the Chartered Institute of Taxation publishes them, will show it.

What to take away.

Corporate tax law lives in several places at once, and a tax function needs to know which kind of source it is holding.

The statute comes first. The two thousand and nine Act computes profits. The twenty ten Act gives reliefs and structure. The International Act holds double tax relief, transfer pricing, controlled foreign companies, hybrids and the interest restriction. The Gains Act, the Capital Allowances Act, the Management Act and the company return rules in Schedule eighteen complete the core, with free-standing schedules for the senior accounting officer, tax strategy and uncertain tax treatment rules. Statutory instruments carry equal force, and treaties take effect by Order in Council under section two of the International Act.

For the financial year twenty twenty six, the O E C D's materials are written into U K law in three places. Section one six four for transfer pricing, section twenty of the two thousand and nine Act for attribution to a permanent establishment, and section eleven forty A of the twenty ten Act for the definition. They refer to the Model and Commentary approved on the eighteenth of November, twenty twenty five, and the twenty twenty two Guidelines, and for transfer pricing and attribution they follow those texts as amended from time to time, for chargeable periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six. So the guidelines are not just guidance there. Elsewhere, Fowler tells us, Commentaries persuade as the cogency of their reasoning deserves.

Accounts supply most of the numbers, through section forty six and the loan relationship rules, so a change of accounting standard can change taxable profit without a Finance Act.

H M R C's manuals are its view, not law. But departing from its known position can trigger a notification under the uncertain tax treatment rules once five million pounds is at stake.

To get certainty in advance, use a statutory clearance where one exists, the non-statutory service only for genuine uncertainty, the new service for projects of at least one billion pounds, binding for up to five years, and an advance pricing agreement for transfer pricing.

Read the law on the right day. Check the Changes to legislation box, use point-in-time versions, and apply commencement words precisely.

And in our invented case, Tarnmoor's four U K loans, four hundred and thirty million pounds at six per cent, fall within the U K to U K exemption in section one six four A, while the Vallarian loan stays inside transfer pricing.

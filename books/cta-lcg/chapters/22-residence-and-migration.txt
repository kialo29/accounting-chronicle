Part Four: Across Borders.

Chapter twenty two: Where a company lives, and how it leaves.

On the first of March in Group Year Four, a letter left Leeds for H M R C. It came from Tarnmoor Ireland, a company in our invented group, Tarnmoor. Tarnmoor, its companies, its people and its numbers are invented for teaching, as they are throughout this book. Ireland is real. The letter said three things. Tarnmoor Ireland intended to stop being resident in the United Kingdom. It would do so at the end of the thirtieth of June. And here was a statement of the tax it owed, with a proposal for how that tax would be secured before it went.

Tarnmoor Ireland was incorporated in Ireland. Its customers were Irish. Yet for years it had paid corporation tax in the United Kingdom on every euro it earned, because its directors met in Leeds. Now the board had decided to move its meetings, and its real decisions, to Dublin. A company cannot simply walk out of the United Kingdom tax net. It must tell H M R C first, account for what it owes, and pay tax on gains it has never realised.

This chapter asks two questions. Where does a company live for tax purposes? And what happens when it leaves? It opens Part Four of this book, on profits that cross borders. Company residence, and the taxation of non-resident and dual resident companies, are core topics on the paper. The migration of a company, under the rules in force since the first of January, twenty twenty, is non-core. The chapter builds on The Living Law, chapter twenty, which introduced company residence and the De Beers case, and chapter twenty six, which introduced the treaty tie-breaker for companies resident in two countries. Here we take both to Advanced Technical depth, and then follow Tarnmoor Ireland out of the door.

The law sits in several Acts. The Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, which I will call the two thousand and nine Act, holds the residence rules and most income exits. The Corporation Tax Act twenty ten, the twenty ten Act, holds group relief. The Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act nineteen ninety two, the Gains Act, holds the exit charge on gains. The Capital Allowances Act two thousand and one deals with plant. And the Taxes Management Act nineteen seventy, the Management Act, holds the rules on notice, security and payment plans. We apply the law for the financial year twenty twenty six. Finance Act twenty twenty six made no change to company residence or to the migration rules.

Two tests, one question.

Residence matters because it decides how much of a company's income the United Kingdom claims. A company resident here is charged on all its profits, wherever they arise, subject to reliefs such as the branch exemption in chapter twenty five and foreign tax credit in chapter twenty four. A company resident elsewhere is charged only on narrow categories of United Kingdom profit, such as a trade carried on through a permanent establishment here, or United Kingdom property income. Chapter twenty three owns that list. Residence is the first switch in every international answer.

There are two tests, and a company needs to pass only one of them to be resident. The first is statutory. Under section fourteen of the two thousand and nine Act, a company incorporated in the United Kingdom is resident here. It does not matter where its directors live, where its board meets or where it trades. A company registered at Companies House carries its residence with it like a birth certificate.

The second test is older and comes from the judges. A company incorporated abroad is resident in the United Kingdom if its central management and control is exercised here. The courts developed this test in the nineteenth century and settled it in the House of Lords in nineteen oh six, in De Beers Consolidated Mines versus Howe. The Living Law, chapter twenty, told that story. A diamond company registered in South Africa, with its mines in South Africa, was held resident in the United Kingdom because the directors who made its real decisions met in London.

The two tests work as a ratchet. Incorporation keeps a United Kingdom company resident, whatever happens to its board. Central management and control can make a foreign company resident too, and a foreign company can drift in and out of residence as its decision making moves. Only one route takes residence away from a United Kingdom company, and it runs through a treaty. We reach it shortly.

Hold on to the misconception this chapter exists to correct. Many people believe that a United Kingdom company can move its tax residence abroad by moving its board. It cannot. Moving the board of a company incorporated here leaves it resident here under section fourteen. At most, it makes the company resident in two countries at once. Whether it then becomes non-resident depends on a treaty tie-breaker, and on whether the two tax authorities agree. A board meeting in Paris does not change a birth certificate issued by Companies House.

Tarnmoor Ireland shows the other side of the ratchet. It was incorporated in Ireland, so section fourteen never applied to it. It was resident here only because its central management and control was in Leeds. Move the control to Dublin, genuinely and completely, and it stops being resident here as a matter of domestic law.

Where the mind of a company lives.

Central management and control is a phrase that sounds precise and is not. The courts have always treated it as a question of fact. In De Beers, the Lord Chancellor, Lord Loreburn, put the principle in words that are still quoted. A company resides where its real business is carried on, and the real business is carried on where the central management and control actually abides.

Think of a company as having a body and a mind. The body is its factories, offices, staff and customers. The mind is the small group of people who decide its strategy, approve its major contracts, raise its finance, declare its dividends and appoint its managers. Residence follows the mind, not the body. A company can run its whole operation in Ireland and still be resident in the United Kingdom, if the mind lives in Leeds.

The test looks for the highest level of control, not day to day management. A factory manager in Cork who runs production, hires staff and negotiates with local suppliers is managing. That is not central management and control. The board in Leeds that decides whether to build the factory, how to fund it and whether to sell it is exercising central management and control. The two can be in different countries, and often are.

In most companies the highest level of control is exercised by the board, acting as a board. So the usual question is where the board meets and decides. But that is a starting point, not an answer. H M R C's published approach is its Statement of Practice one of nineteen ninety, now reproduced in its International Manual. It says that the place of board meetings is important in the normal case but not necessarily conclusive. In its own words, the place of directors' meetings is significant only insofar as those meetings constitute the medium through which central management and control is exercised.

That sentence carries two warnings. A board which meets abroad but merely signs decisions made elsewhere is not exercising control there. The meeting is theatre. And a board can exercise control without meeting at all, through written resolutions, calls and informal decisions. If the directors who really decide all live and work in the United Kingdom, the company may be resident here even if its formal meetings are held offshore.

H M R C's guidance also recognises the messiness of modern boards. It says it would not usually review residence just because a director based in the United Kingdom habitually joins overseas meetings by video link. And its pandemic guidance accepted that a few board meetings or decisions here, for a short time, do not necessarily make a company resident. These are statements of H M R C's practice, not law. They describe where H M R C will look, not what a court would find.

Before Group Year Four, Tarnmoor Ireland was resident in the United Kingdom on the plainest facts imaginable. In our invented case, its board was made up of Tarnmoor executives based in Leeds. It met in Leeds. It approved the Irish budgets, the purchase of a United Kingdom warehouse and the acquisition of an Irish distributor's business. Its residence was no accident. It kept the company inside the United Kingdom group for group relief and the gains rules.

Usurped, or merely influenced.

Most foreign subsidiaries of United Kingdom groups have boards abroad that listen closely to the parent. So the cases that matter most to a group tax function ask one question. When a parent tells a subsidiary's board what it wants, where is the subsidiary's mind?

The first answer came in Unit Construction Company versus Bullock, decided by the House of Lords in nineteen sixty. A United Kingdom company had African subsidiaries. Their constitutions required their boards to meet, and their business to be managed, outside the United Kingdom. In practice the local boards had stood back, and the parent's board in London had taken over the real decisions. The House of Lords held that the subsidiaries were resident in the United Kingdom. Lord Radcliffe treated the location of control as a question of actuality. It did not matter that the parent's conduct broke the subsidiaries' own constitutions. Residence follows what happens, not what the documents say should happen.

The second answer came in Wood versus Holden, decided by the Court of Appeal in twenty oh six, with Lord Justice Chadwick giving the leading judgment. One warning before the story. The Mr Wood in this case was a businessman who had built a greetings card company. He was not the guitarist. In nineteen ninety six, shares in Ron Wood Greetings Card Holdings, held through an offshore company in the family's structure, were sold for twenty three point seven million pounds to a Dutch company, Eulalia Holding, whose sole director was a Dutch trust company. Eulalia sold them on to a trade buyer a few months later. The tax planning depended on Eulalia being resident outside the United Kingdom.

H M R C argued that Eulalia's director simply did what Mr Wood and his advisers wanted. No real decisions were taken in the Netherlands, they said, so its central management and control was in the United Kingdom. The Special Commissioners agreed. The High Court disagreed, and the Court of Appeal upheld the High Court. Eulalia was resident in the Netherlands.

The distinction the court drew is the one every group tax function lives by. A board that is bypassed, that stands aside while someone else decides, has been usurped, as in Unit Construction. Its company lives wherever the usurper decides. A board that is advised, proposed to and influenced by outsiders, but that itself considers and takes the decision, still exercises central management and control. Eulalia's director had been steered. But the court held that it had taken the decisions itself, in the Netherlands, rather than standing aside. Influence is not control.

A client who always follows a solicitor's advice still makes the decision. Only a client who hands over the pen has stopped deciding. The legal rule is narrower than the analogy. The court asks whether the board actually considered and took the decisions that matter, at the highest level of the company's affairs, and where it did so.

For a parent, Wood versus Holden is reassuring only up to a point. A parent may propose and expect its subsidiaries to follow. A board that never asks a question will not necessarily be respected, as the next case shows.

When the parent really decides.

H M R C versus Development Securities, decided by the Court of Appeal in twenty twenty, is the modern counterweight. Development Securities, a United Kingdom property group, used a two thousand and four scheme designed by a large accountancy firm to make use of indexation allowance on assets standing at a loss. New Jersey subsidiaries with Jersey resident directors were to buy assets from the group under options, at prices well above market value, and later become United Kingdom resident. The scheme depended on their being resident in Jersey when they bought.

The First-tier Tribunal found that the Jersey boards had not acted improperly. They had taken advice and checked that what they were asked to do was lawful. But they had not really engaged with the fact that paying far more than market value made no commercial sense for their own companies. They were implementing a decision made by the parent in London. The Upper Tribunal disagreed. The directors had applied their minds, it said, and had not abdicated their responsibilities. The Court of Appeal allowed H M R C's appeal and restored the First-tier Tribunal's decision. The Jersey companies were resident in the United Kingdom when they exercised the options.

The case did not overrule Wood versus Holden. Both cases ask the same question, which is by whom and where the real decision was taken. What changed was the answer on the facts. Eulalia's director took a decision that was, for Eulalia, an ordinary one. The Jersey directors approved transactions that were against their own companies' interests and made sense only for the group. A board that signs something uncommercial for its own company, because the parent wants it, is hard to describe as the company's mind. The real decision was made where the parent's executives sat.

The reasoning was not unanimous. One member of the Court of Appeal expressed very considerable reservations about the First-tier Tribunal's approach, while agreeing with the outcome, and commentators still debate how much engagement is enough. Two lessons are clear. A subsidiary board that is asked to do something uncommercial will be scrutinised hard. And evidence that the board understood, questioned and could have refused a proposal is far more persuasive than evidence that it met in the right place.

Tarnmoor Ireland's migration turned this lesson around. To leave the United Kingdom, its control had to move to Dublin in fact, not just on paper. In our invented case, the board was reconstituted with effect from the end of the thirtieth of June in Group Year Four. A majority of Irish resident directors with real industry experience joined, the Leeds executives stepped down, and board meetings moved to Dublin. The board was given authority over budgets, contracts and financing, and it used it. Tom Hesketh, the group head of tax, wrote the new governance protocol himself. Its first line read, in our invented story, decisions are made in the room, not before it.

Two countries, one company.

Residence tests are national, and nothing stops two countries claiming the same company. A company incorporated in one country and managed in another will often be resident in both. That is dual residence, and on its own it means double taxation.

Double tax treaties solve the problem in two steps, and The Living Law, chapter twenty six, introduced both. First, Article four of a treaty based on the model published by the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, the O E C D Model, defines a resident as a person liable to tax in a country by reason of domicile, residence, place of management or a similar criterion. A company resident in both countries under that definition then meets the second step, the tie-breaker, which decides which country it is resident in for the purposes of the treaty.

Older treaties used a fixed rule. A dual resident company was resident only where its place of effective management was situated. That is a treaty concept, not the same words as central management and control, though on most facts the two point to the same place. H M R C's International Manual says that, typically, the tie-breaker awards residence to the country in which the effective management of the company is located. Chapter twenty four, on treaties, returns to the wording, and to an exam warning that matters here too. When a question supplies a treaty article, apply that article, not the version you remember.

The Multilateral Instrument, in force for the United Kingdom from the first of October, twenty eighteen, offered countries a different tie-breaker in its Article four. Under it, the two competent authorities, which for the United Kingdom means H M R C, shall endeavour to determine residence by mutual agreement. They have regard to the place of effective management, the place of incorporation and any other relevant factors. If they do not agree, the company gets no treaty relief, except to the extent the authorities agree. The United Kingdom and Ireland both adopted this article, and it has had effect for United Kingdom corporation tax from the first of April, twenty twenty.

Now the step that connects treaties to domestic law. Section eighteen of the two thousand and nine Act says that a company which would be resident in the United Kingdom, but which is treated by a treaty as resident only in the other country, is treated as not resident in the United Kingdom for all purposes of the Taxes Acts. That rule has applied since the thirtieth of November, nineteen ninety three. It is the only route by which a company incorporated here can become non-resident. And because of the Multilateral Instrument, for many treaties it now depends on an agreement between tax authorities, not on a rule a company can engineer by moving its meetings.

Timing matters in a tie-breaker. H M R C versus Smallwood, decided by the Court of Appeal in twenty ten, concerned a trust scheme in which a trust was moved to Mauritius and back within a single tax year, so that the gain arose while it was there. The majority held that the tie-breaker could not be applied as a snapshot taken at the moment of disposal. Looked at in the context of the whole scheme, the place of effective management was in the United Kingdom, and the United Kingdom could tax. Smallwood involved trustees, not a company, but the lesson carries over. A tie-breaker is not a stopwatch that can be started and stopped around a transaction.

Tarnmoor Ireland was a dual resident company before it migrated. It was resident in the United Kingdom through central management and control in Leeds. In our invented case, Ireland also treated it as resident, and the two competent authorities had agreed, before our story begins, that it was resident in the United Kingdom for the purposes of the treaty. This book does not discuss Irish domestic law. When control moved to Dublin, Tarnmoor Ireland ceased to be resident in the United Kingdom under domestic law, and the authorities agreed that it was resident in Ireland from the migration date. The ordinary United Kingdom consequences of leaving then followed.

What dual residence costs.

A company that stays resident in the United Kingdom while also being resident abroad, because the tie-breaker favours the United Kingdom or because no treaty applies, is inside the United Kingdom charge. But Parliament has long been wary of it. A company taxed in two countries can sometimes deduct the same expense in both, and use one loss twice. So a cluster of rules stops dual residents acting as conduits for relief.

The sharpest is in group relief. Under section one hundred and nine of the twenty ten Act, a dual resident company cannot surrender losses to its group if it is a dual resident investing company. That means, broadly, a dual resident company that is not a trading company, or whose trade consists mainly of holding shares, making loans or similar group finance activities. The examiners tested this in November twenty twenty four, in a twenty-mark group relief question, and reported that many candidates missed it.

The gains rules follow. A dual resident investing company cannot receive assets on a no gain, no loss transfer within a group. Group roll-over relief is denied where such a company would be the one applying the proceeds. And in the capital allowances code, the elections that let connected companies transfer plant at tax written down value, rather than market value, are barred where a dual resident investing company is involved.

Dual residence also cuts across the branch exemption, the election that lets a United Kingdom company exempt the profits of its foreign permanent establishments. H M R C's guidance says that while a company is dual resident it cannot exempt the profits of a permanent establishment in the territory in which it is treaty resident. Chapter twenty five deals with the election in full.

Tarnmoor Ireland escaped all of this. It was a trading company, so it was not a dual resident investing company. While resident here, it could surrender and claim group relief, as chapter one explained, and receive assets at no gain, no loss. Its dual residence was a fact to manage. Tarnmoor's tax function kept the competent authority agreement on file, reviewed the board minutes each year, and made sure no Irish board meeting quietly took a decision that should have been taken in Leeds.

One more consequence is worth knowing, because the exam likes it. The senior accounting officer rules apply to companies incorporated in the United Kingdom. Tarnmoor Ireland, incorporated in Ireland, was never inside them, even when it was resident here. Its migration changes nothing on that front. Chapter four explained why.

Leaving, and why leaving costs money.

When a company stops being resident in the United Kingdom, three things happen at once. Its accounting period ends. The United Kingdom loses its claim to tax the company's future foreign profits and gains. And the company may be carrying assets that grew in value while it was resident here. Those gains have not been realised, so nothing has yet been taxed.

The first consequence is mechanical. Under section ten of the two thousand and nine Act, an accounting period ends when a company ceases to be resident in the United Kingdom, as chapter three noted. For Tarnmoor Ireland, an accounting period that began on the first of January in Group Year Four ended on the thirtieth of June. Its return for those six months is the migration return, and every exit charge falls into it.

The second and third consequences explain the exit charges. A resident company is taxed on its worldwide gains when it realises them. If it could leave just before selling, gains built up over years of residence would escape. So the law treats departure itself as a moment of realisation. Picture a hotel guest who has been helping themselves from the minibar all week. The hotel does not wait for the guest to finish the bottles at home. It presents the bill at checkout. The precise rule is that the departing company is treated as disposing of its assets at market value immediately before it leaves, and as reacquiring them at the same value.

For years the United Kingdom softened that rule for groups. A company leaving as a seventy five per cent subsidiary of a United Kingdom resident parent could, by joint election, postpone the gains charge on its foreign trading assets until it sold them within six years, or until the parent relationship ended. That was section one eight seven of the Gains Act. It has been repealed. Finance Act twenty nineteen removed it for companies ceasing to be resident on or after the first of January, twenty twenty. For migrations since then, the charge cannot be postponed in that way. It can only be paid over time, under a payment plan, where the company qualifies.

The shape of today's rules comes from European Union law. In National Grid Indus, decided by the Court of Justice of the European Union in twenty eleven, a Dutch company moved its place of effective management to the United Kingdom, and the Netherlands demanded immediate tax on its unrealised gains. The Court held that a member state may tax gains accrued while a company was within its jurisdiction, but that demanding immediate payment was disproportionate. Offering deferral, possibly with interest, would be acceptable. The United Kingdom introduced exit charge payment plans soon afterwards, with effect from the eleventh of December, twenty twelve. Then the European Union's Anti Tax Avoidance Directive set a common model, and the United Kingdom's twenty twenty changes, made while it was still bound, turned the plans into fixed instalments over five years.

That is the post-Brexit oddity of this chapter. Rules shaped by European Union freedoms are still on the United Kingdom statute book, in their European Union form. We will see what that means for who can use a payment plan.

The gains exit charge, and the land that waits.

Section one eight five of the Gains Act is the main exit charge. When a company ceases to be resident in the United Kingdom, it is deemed to dispose of all its assets at market value immediately before the moment it ceases, and to reacquire them at that value. Every chargeable asset is caught, wherever it is in the world, unless an exception applies. Indexation allowance still applies up to December twenty seventeen, where the asset was held before then. Losses on the deemed disposals are allowable in the ordinary way.

The main exception protects assets that stay inside the United Kingdom charge. Assets situated in the United Kingdom and used or held for a trade that the company carries on here through a permanent establishment after it leaves are excluded. There is no need to tax them now. The company remains chargeable on them as a non-resident with a United Kingdom permanent establishment, and chapter twenty three explains that charge.

Two smaller rules close gaps. Roll-over relief cannot carry a gain from an asset disposed of before migration into an asset acquired after it, unless the new asset is itself a United Kingdom permanent establishment asset. And a company that ceases to exist has not ceased to be resident. The charge is about departure, not death.

Now the part that candidates missed in November twenty twenty five. Since the sixth of April, twenty nineteen, non-resident companies have been chargeable on gains on United Kingdom land, and on certain indirect interests in it, which chapter twenty three explains. So there is no need to tax United Kingdom land at the moment of departure, because the United Kingdom will still be able to tax it when it is actually sold. Section one eight seven B of the Gains Act gives effect to that logic. Where an interest in United Kingdom land is deemed disposed of under section one eight five, the gain or loss does not accrue at migration. Instead, when the company later disposes of the land, or part of it, the postponed amount is treated as accruing at that later time, in addition to the gain or loss on the actual disposal.

Postponement is automatic. No claim is needed. But the company may elect for a particular deemed disposal to be excluded from the postponement, within two years after the day of the deemed disposal. Why would anyone choose to pay tax earlier? The usual reasons are a loss on the land, which the company may want to use in its migration period against other gains, or capital losses already available in that period to absorb the gain now.

The mechanics produce a two-layer gain on a later sale. The base cost after migration is the market value at migration, because of the deemed reacquisition. So the later actual gain measures growth after departure. The postponed amount measures growth before it. Added together they equal the gain from the original cost, so nothing is lost and nothing is taxed twice.

Tarnmoor Ireland held one United Kingdom asset of this kind. In Group Year One it had bought a warehouse in England for three million pounds, and let it to an unconnected logistics company. At migration it was worth three point eight million pounds. The deemed gain of eight hundred thousand pounds was postponed under section one eight seven B. Nothing was taxed at migration. Suppose, for illustration only, that Tarnmoor Ireland later sold the warehouse for four and a half million pounds. Its actual gain would be seven hundred thousand pounds, from the migration value, and the postponed eight hundred thousand pounds would accrue on the same day. Together that is one and a half million pounds, exactly the gain over the original cost.

The other exits.

Gains are only one kind of value a departing company carries. Each of the main income codes has its own exit, and an Advanced Technical answer lists them all. The examiners' question in November twenty twenty five expected candidates to cover trading stock, machinery, derivatives, land and intangible assets, as well as gains.

Start with the trade. Section forty one of the two thousand and nine Act says that when a company ceases to be within the charge to corporation tax in respect of a trade, it is treated as permanently ceasing to carry it on. A company that migrates and keeps no permanent establishment here is in exactly that position. Its trade carries on in the real world, but for United Kingdom tax it has stopped.

That deemed cessation has two consequences. First, trading stock on hand is valued under the cessation rules in section one hundred and sixty two. Unless the stock is sold to someone who will carry on a trade in the United Kingdom and deduct its cost, it is brought in at the amount it would have fetched on the open market at cessation. The unrealised profit in the stock is taxed. Second, under the Capital Allowances Act, the end of the qualifying activity is a disposal event for the plant and machinery pool. The plant is not sold, so its disposal value is its market value, and a balancing charge or allowance follows in the final period. No writing down allowance is given for that period.

Intangible fixed assets have their own exit in section eight five nine of the two thousand and nine Act. Immediately before a company ceases to be resident, it is treated as realising its chargeable intangible assets at market value and reacquiring them at that value. The credit is the market value less the asset's tax written down value. Goodwill, customer lists, brands and know-how built or bought while resident are caught.

Loans and derivatives follow the same pattern. Section three hundred and thirty three treats the company as assigning its loan relationship assets and liabilities for fair value immediately before it ceases to be resident, and reacquiring them at once. Section six hundred and nine does the same for derivative contracts. Each excludes items held for a United Kingdom permanent establishment, a United Kingdom land dealing trade or a United Kingdom property business that continues after migration. One trap from that same November twenty twenty five question deserves a warning. The derivatives exit is a fair value measurement on departure. It has nothing to do with the regulations on hedging, which chapter twelve covered, and the examiners reported that some candidates wandered into them.

Shares deserve a moment of their own. A deemed disposal of shares is a disposal for gains purposes, so the first question for any shareholding is whether the substantial shareholding exemption applies. Chapter eighteen owns that exemption. Its basic threshold is a holding of at least ten per cent. Below that, the deemed gain is simply chargeable.

The pattern is simple. Every exit turns value accrued under United Kingdom jurisdiction into taxable profit, at market or fair value, on the last day of residence. A good answer walks through the balance sheet asset by asset, asking which code taxes each one and whether an exception keeps it in the United Kingdom.

Tarnmoor Ireland's last six months.

Now the full picture for Tarnmoor Ireland, in our invented case. Its accounting period ran from the first of January to the thirtieth of June in Group Year Four. Its ordinary trading profit for those six months was one point two million pounds. Its warehouse rents are left out of the figures, for simplicity. Then Tom Hesketh's team walked through the balance sheet as it stood immediately before the board's control moved to Dublin.

Trading stock first. It stood in the books at one point one million pounds, and would have fetched one point four million pounds on the open market. The deemed cessation of the trade brings in the difference, three hundred thousand pounds.

Next, the customer list Tarnmoor Ireland had bought in Group Year Two with an Irish distributor's business, for three million pounds. Chapter eleven explained why it had never produced a single tax deduction. The business came with no qualifying intellectual property, so no debits were allowed, and its tax written down value was still its cost. At migration the list was worth four point two million pounds. Section eight five nine produces a credit of one point two million pounds.

Then an eight per cent shareholding in an unconnected Irish valve distributor, bought in Group Year One for nine hundred thousand pounds, and now worth one point five million pounds. Eight per cent is below the threshold for the shareholding exemption, and indexation stopped long before Group Year One. The deemed disposal under section one eight five gives a chargeable gain of six hundred thousand pounds.

The plant and machinery pool stood at four hundred thousand pounds. The plant was worth five hundred thousand pounds. The deemed end of the trade gives a balancing charge of one hundred thousand pounds.

The English warehouse, as we saw, produced a deemed gain of eight hundred thousand pounds that does not accrue now, because section one eight seven B postpones it until a later sale.

Finally the loans. Tarnmoor Ireland owed Tarnmoor plc twenty million pounds, interest free and, in our invented case, repayable on demand. A loan repayable on demand is worth its face value, so the deemed assignment at fair value produced neither a credit nor a debit. It had no derivatives.

Add it up. Trading profit of one point two million pounds, plus stock of three hundred thousand, plus the customer list credit of one point two million, plus the balancing charge of one hundred thousand, gives trading profits of two point eight million pounds. Add the gain on the shares of six hundred thousand, and taxable total profits are three point four million pounds. At twenty five per cent, corporation tax for the migration period is eight hundred and fifty thousand pounds.

Look at where that tax comes from. Only three hundred and twenty five thousand pounds relates to the ordinary trading profit and the balancing charge. The other five hundred and twenty five thousand pounds is tax on value never turned into cash. The stock is still on the shelves, the customer list is still in use, and the shares are unsold. That cash problem is the reason payment plans exist.

Tarnmoor Ireland's profits were far above the very large company threshold for a six-month period, which on the group's count of associated companies was one million pounds. So it had been paying its corporation tax by instalments during the period, under the rules for short periods that chapter three explained. The full working is in the reading edition.

Settling up before you go.

A company that has left the United Kingdom is hard to chase. Its assets, directors and bank accounts may all be abroad. So the Management Act requires a migrating company to settle its affairs before it leaves, not after. The rules are in sections one hundred and nine B to one hundred and nine F, and the examiners have found answers on them thin.

Section one hundred and nine B sets four conditions, and all must be met before the company ceases to be resident. Condition A is that the company gives H M R C notice of its intention to cease to be resident. Condition B is that the notice specifies the time when it will cease, the migration time. Condition C is that the company gives H M R C a statement of the tax it owes, or will owe, for periods beginning before the migration time, together with details of the arrangements it proposes for securing payment. Those arrangements may include a proposal for an exit charge payment plan. Condition D is that arrangements for securing payment have been made and approved by H M R C. Condition D falls away to the extent payment is secured by a payment plan. If the company and H M R C disagree about the amount in the statement, the dispute goes to the tribunal, whose decision is final. And an approval is void if the company did not fully disclose the facts that mattered.

The word tax here is wide. Section one hundred and nine F includes corporation tax, amounts the company should have deducted under pay as you earn and the construction industry scheme, income tax it should have deducted from interest and other payments, and interest on all of them. A migrating company has to clear every one.

The rules have teeth, and some of them bite people rather than companies. If a company migrates without meeting the conditions, section one hundred and nine C allows a penalty on the company of up to the amount of tax unpaid for periods before migration. Section one hundred and nine D allows a penalty of the same maximum on any person who knowingly took part. That includes directors, and controlling companies and their directors. There is a presumption that directors were party to it unless they show otherwise. Professional advisers acting only as advisers are excluded.

Then the recovery rule, which reaches into the rest of the group. If any of the tax is still unpaid six months after it became payable, or after the due date under a payment plan, H M R C may serve a notice on other people, broadly within three years of the tax being finally determined. The targets are companies that were in the migrating company's fifty one per cent group in the twelve months before migration, and anyone who was a controlling director of it, or of a company controlling it. They must pay within thirty days. They can recover the amount from the migrating company, but they cannot deduct it for tax.

So migration is a group event. Tarnmoor plc and Tarnmoor Engineering would be first in line if Tarnmoor Ireland failed to pay. Tom Hesketh's notice of the first of March gave H M R C four months before the migration time. It specified that time, gave a statement of tax, and proposed two kinds of security. The tax on the exit items that qualified would be covered by a payment plan. The rest would be paid through instalments, with a guarantee from Tarnmoor plc for anything outstanding. H M R C approved the arrangements in May, in our invented case, well before the migration time.

Paying over six years.

An exit charge payment plan, under Schedule three Z B to the Management Act, lets a migrating company pay its exit charges in instalments. The tax it covers is defined by a comparison. Take the corporation tax for the migration period, call it C T one. Then compute it again, ignoring the exit charge provisions, call it C T two. The provisions ignored are the gains exit charge, the stock valuation on the deemed cessation, the loan relationship and derivative exits, and the intangibles exit. C T one minus C T two is the tax the plan can cover. Capital allowances are not on the list, so tax on a balancing charge is outside the plan.

The company must apply within nine months after the end of the migration period, giving the details the Schedule requires. H M R C may ask for security where there is a serious risk to collection. The tax is then payable in six equal instalments. The first is due on the day after the end of the nine months that follow the migration period. The other five fall on the next five anniversaries of that date. Interest runs on the unpaid tax as if there were no plan, so the plan defers the cash, not the cost of money. The whole balance is accelerated by events such as insolvency, administration, liquidation, the company ceasing to be resident in a qualifying state, or twelve months of non-payment. Selling an asset brings forward the tax relating to that asset.

Before twenty twenty, a company could choose a realisation method, paying tax on each asset as it was sold. That option was removed for periods ending on or after the first of January, twenty twenty. Parts of H M R C's Company Taxation Manual still describe it, and are out of date.

Now the catch. Only an eligible company can have a plan. The Schedule defines one as a company with the freedom of establishment guaranteed by the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union or the European Economic Area Agreement, which ceases to be resident in the United Kingdom and becomes resident in a relevant state, essentially a European Union member or a European Economic Area state with equivalent arrangements for recovering tax debts. It must carry on business there. H M R C's manual reads this as meaning companies incorporated in a European Union or European Economic Area member state. After Brexit, a company incorporated in the United Kingdom no longer has those treaty freedoms, so in practice it cannot use a plan. The United Kingdom's own companies are shut out of a relief that still exists in United Kingdom law.

Tarnmoor Ireland fits the definition exactly. It is incorporated in Ireland, a member state, and it moved to Ireland, where it carries on its business. Its C T one was eight hundred and fifty thousand pounds. Its C T two, counting only the trading profit and the balancing charge, was three hundred and twenty five thousand pounds. The plan therefore covers five hundred and twenty five thousand pounds, payable in six instalments of eighty seven thousand five hundred pounds, plus interest. The first falls due on the first of April in Group Year Five, and the last on the first of April in Group Year Ten. The tax on the balancing charge, twenty five thousand pounds, stays outside the plan.

After the move, and what the examiner looks for.

From the first of July, Tarnmoor Ireland is a non-resident company. It is still within the charge to United Kingdom corporation tax, but only on its English warehouse rents, as a United Kingdom property business, and in time on the warehouse gain, including the postponed eight hundred thousand pounds. A new accounting period begins at once. Chapter twenty three explains how non-residents are taxed. Without a permanent establishment here, it can no longer surrender or claim group relief. It stays in Tarnmoor's gains group, because non-resident companies count as members, which chapter seventeen explains. And two other regimes switch on. Its interest-free loan from Tarnmoor plc loses the exemption for transactions between United Kingdom companies, so transfer pricing applies, which is chapter twenty seven's story. And it becomes a controlled foreign company, which chapter twenty six examines.

The same governance lesson runs the other way for the subsidiaries Tarnmoor wants to keep abroad. Its Vallarian and Marrovian companies must be resident where they are incorporated. Their boards need members who live there, meet there, receive papers in time to read them, and occasionally push back. The group can propose and influence, as in Wood versus Holden. It must not decide for them, as in Unit Construction and Development Securities.

For the tax function, migration is a project with a timetable. Months ahead, test whether control will really move, and document it. Value every asset at the migration time. Decide whether to elect out of the land postponement within two years. Model the exit tax, the plan and the interest. Give notice, with a statement and proposals, early enough for approval before the migration time. And remember that directors and group companies carry personal and joint exposure if any of it goes wrong.

Timing is the main planning lever. Migrating when asset values are low reduces the exit charge, and losses or reliefs available in the migration period can absorb it.

Company residence and non-resident and dual resident companies are core. Migration under the post twenty twenty rules is non-core, which means working knowledge and the main computation. The paper has tested this ground often. In November twenty twenty five a fifteen-mark question covered residence, the exit charges on stock, machinery, derivatives, land and intangibles, the automatic postponement for land, payment plans and the notice rules. Common errors were missing the land postponement, wrongly discussing the hedging regulations, and thin detail on administration and plans. In November twenty twenty three, a twenty-mark question tested the residence of a parent before and after board changes, with a treaty tie-breaker supplied, and some candidates ignored the supplied article. In November twenty twenty four, ten marks asked when a non-United Kingdom company setting up here becomes resident or chargeable, and answers tied to the facts scored well. The trap runs both ways. In May twenty twenty six, many candidates discussed residence and migration in a question about a foreign permanent establishment, where they earned nothing. Answer the question asked.

What to take away.

A company is resident in the United Kingdom if it is incorporated here, under section fourteen of the two thousand and nine Act, or if its central management and control is exercised here. Central management and control means the highest level of control, usually the board acting as a board. It is a question of fact.

A board that is advised and influenced but takes its own decisions keeps control, as in Wood versus Holden in twenty oh six. A board that stands aside, as in Unit Construction versus Bullock in nineteen sixty, or that rubber-stamps uncommercial decisions really made by its parent, as in Development Securities in twenty twenty, does not.

A United Kingdom company cannot leave by moving its board. It can become non-resident only under section eighteen, where a treaty tie-breaker makes it resident in the other country. Older treaties use the place of effective management. Under Article four of the Multilateral Instrument, including for the United Kingdom and Ireland, the tax authorities decide by agreement. Dual resident investing companies are barred from surrendering group relief and from no gain, no loss transfers.

When a company ceases to be resident, its accounting period ends. Section one eight five of the Gains Act deems it to dispose of and reacquire all its assets at market value, except assets of a continuing United Kingdom permanent establishment. The old postponement in section one eight seven was repealed for migrations from the first of January, twenty twenty. United Kingdom land is postponed automatically under section one eight seven B until it is actually sold, unless the company elects out within two years. Stock is valued on a deemed cessation, plant gives balancing adjustments, and intangibles, loans and derivatives are deemed realised at market or fair value.

Before leaving, the company must give notice, name the migration time, state its tax and have payment arrangements approved. Penalties up to the unpaid tax can fall on the company and its directors, and group companies and controlling directors can be made to pay.

An eligible company moving to a European Union or European Economic Area state can pay C T one minus C T two in six equal annual instalments, with interest, applying within nine months. In practice that excludes companies incorporated in the United Kingdom.

For Tarnmoor Ireland, taxable profits of three point four million pounds gave corporation tax of eight hundred and fifty thousand pounds. Five hundred and twenty five thousand pounds of it went into a plan of six instalments of eighty seven thousand five hundred pounds. The eight hundred thousand pound gain on the warehouse waits for a sale.

So where does a company live? Where its mind is, unless it was born here. And how does it leave? Only by paying for what it takes with it.

Tarnmoor Ireland now has a United Kingdom foothold as a non-resident. Chapter twenty three, on inbound companies, permanent establishments and withholding, asks what the United Kingdom taxes when a foreign company does business here.

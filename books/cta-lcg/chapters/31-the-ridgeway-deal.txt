Part Five: Putting It Together.

Chapter thirty one: The Ridgeway deal.

Completion day.

On the first of April, some years after the events of the last thirty chapters, Tarnmoor P L C bought a bicycle company. The price was four and a half million pounds, paid in cash. The seller was Jess Hartley, and the company was Ridgeway Holdings, the parent of Ridgeway Cycles. Our invented group, Tarnmoor, its people and its numbers are invented for teaching, as they have been throughout this book. So is Jess, whose story The Living Law told. Ireland, which will matter later, is real.

By Tarnmoor's standards it was a small deal. Tarnmoor's revenue runs to more than a billion pounds a year. Ridgeway's taxable profit is about six hundred thousand. Yet when Tom Hesketh, the group head of tax, opened the file that morning, the list of tax points ran to two pages. That is the question for this chapter. Why does a small acquisition by a large group touch almost every rule in this book, and how do you find them all, in order, without missing the ones that carry marks?

This is chapter thirty one, the first chapter of Part Five. It is the capstone. It teaches no new rule. Every rule it uses was taught earlier, and each time I will name the chapter that owns it. What it adds is the method, the order and the interactions. The examiner calls this the interaction of taxes, and it is how most twenty mark questions are built. It also closes the last open thread of The Living Law, the question Jess never settled about her Dublin showroom.

The statutes are the familiar ones. The Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, the two thousand and nine Act. The Corporation Tax Act twenty ten, the twenty ten Act. The Taxation, International and Other Provisions, Act twenty ten, the International Act. And the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act nineteen ninety two, the Gains Act. As always, the numbers apply the law for the financial year twenty twenty six, whatever year the story has reached.

The road to Ridgeway.

The Living Law followed Jess Hartley from a cycle business she ran herself, through incorporation and a holding company structure, to a group with two companies. Ridgeway Holdings owns Ridgeway Cycles. Jess owns all of Ridgeway Holdings, and her shares cost her one hundred and sixty two thousand pounds. Along the way the group bought and later sold another business, Wharfe Wheels. Ridgeway Cycles opened a second unit in Skipton, and then a showroom in Dublin.

The Dublin showroom is a branch, a permanent establishment of Ridgeway Cycles in Ireland, not a separate company. In its first year it lost eighty thousand pounds and in its second, thirty thousand. Both losses reduced Ridgeway's United Kingdom profits at once. In its third year it made one hundred and twenty thousand pounds, and in its fourth, two hundred thousand. Ireland taxed that at its trading rate of twelve and a half per cent, twenty five thousand pounds. The United Kingdom taxed it again, as part of Ridgeway's profits, and gave credit for the Irish tax. Jess never made the election that would take the branch out of United Kingdom tax. The Living Law, chapter twenty six, left that question open.

Chapter thirty one of The Living Law imagined an offer of four million pounds for the group, if a buyer came back tomorrow. It was a hypothetical, and no sale happened then. This is the real one, at its own price, four and a half million pounds.

Today, Ridgeway Cycles makes about four hundred thousand pounds a year from its United Kingdom trade and about two hundred thousand from Dublin. On six hundred thousand pounds, corporation tax at twenty five per cent is one hundred and fifty thousand. Less the Irish credit of twenty five thousand, it pays one hundred and twenty five thousand. Ridgeway's year ends on the thirty first of March, so the deal falls on the first day of an accounting period. That is a gift for the analysis, and you will see why.

Jess's own side takes one paragraph, because this paper does not examine it. She is an individual selling shares. Her gain, before costs, is four and a half million pounds less one hundred and sixty two thousand, about four point three four million pounds. It is charged to capital gains tax, with business asset disposal relief if she still meets its conditions, as The Living Law, chapters eighteen and thirty one, explained. No corporation tax, no shareholding exemption, nothing for the buyer to compute. Note it in one line and move on, which is exactly what an answer to this paper should do.

Walking every floor.

Think of an Advanced Technical question as a building inspection. A good surveyor does not wander the house looking at whatever catches the eye. She walks every floor, in the same order, with the same checklist, and writes down what she finds on each one, including the floors where she finds nothing. The checklist is what stops her missing the damp in the cellar while admiring the roof.

Here is Tom's checklist for a share acquisition, and it is the one this chapter follows. First, the transaction itself, meaning stamp duty and the buyer's costs. Second, the target's tax attributes, such as losses, capital losses and past intra-group transfers. Third, the thresholds that move, starting with associated companies and instalments, and then every size test the group already passes. Fourth, the group regimes the target now joins. Group relief, the deductions allowance, the annual investment allowance, research and development. Fifth, governance and compliance. The senior accounting officer, the tax strategy, uncertain tax treatments, transfer pricing, the interest restriction and the global minimum tax. Sixth, the target's own special features, here the Dublin branch. Seventh, the accounts, which means deferred tax in the consolidation. Eighth, the people. Who stays, what they are paid, and when it is deductible. And last, a recommendation, because if the question asks for one, the marks for it are there to be taken.

Two exam rules shape the walk. The first concerns the words tax consequences. The chief examiner has said, after the November twenty twenty five and May twenty twenty six sittings, that those words mean every tax in the syllabus, including stamp duty and stamp duty land tax. A named tax means only that tax. The second is that excluded means excluded. If the question says ignore transfer pricing, a paragraph on it earns nothing.

Now the misconception that this chapter exists to kill. A small acquisition is a small tax job. It is not. The size of the target barely matters. What matters is the size of the group it joins. Ridgeway goes from a two company family business to a member of a listed group of eleven companies that count as associated, a worldwide group for the interest restriction, a multinational group for the global minimum tax, a group whose transfer pricing exemption for small companies no longer applies, and a group with a senior accounting officer. Almost every compliance setting changes on completion day, and some change a year later. The tax on Ridgeway's profits hardly moves. Everything around it does.

The price of the paper.

Start on the ground floor, with the transaction. Tarnmoor P L C buys shares, so the tax on the purchase is stamp duty on the stock transfer form. The rate is half of one per cent, rounded up to the nearest five pounds. Half a per cent of four and a half million pounds is twenty two thousand, five hundred pounds, which needs no rounding. The buyer pays. The form must be presented for stamping within thirty days of execution, or interest and penalties run. Chapter twenty one owns these rules.

There is no stamp duty land tax, even though Ridgeway Cycles occupies property in Skipton. The land stays where it was, inside the company. Only the shares changed hands. And there is no stamp duty group relief, because Jess is an individual. Relief needs a seventy five per cent relationship between transferor and transferee, and an individual is never in a group.

The twenty two thousand, five hundred pounds is a cost of acquiring the shares. It is not deductible for corporation tax. It goes into Tarnmoor's base cost. That base cost will rarely matter, because after twelve months Tarnmoor's holding should qualify for the substantial shareholding exemption on any later sale, as chapter eighteen explained. But it goes in the computation, and it is half a mark.

Tarnmoor's other costs follow the line chapter thirteen drew for Calder, after the Supreme Court's decision in Centrica in twenty twenty four. Costs of appraising whether to buy anything at all can be management expenses. Once the board decides to buy this particular target, the costs of buying it are capital and give no relief. Tom asks for the date of that board decision, because the invoices fall either side of it.

Before any of this, the contract itself carries tax. Chapter twenty explained that a buyer of shares inherits the company's past, so tax that belongs to Jess's years must stay Jess's. Tarnmoor's due diligence looks hardest at three things. The profit Ridgeway has attributed to Dublin, because the Irish and United Kingdom figures must agree. The payroll, because employment taxes are where owner-managed companies most often slip. And the returns, to confirm that nothing is open with H M R C. The tax deed then makes Jess liable for any tax arising from the years before completion, with payments treated as a reduction in the price.

One line for value added tax, which this paper does not examine. Ridgeway Cycles might join Tarnmoor's value added tax group, and that is a decision for another adviser on another day.

Counting the family again.

The third floor is where most candidates lose marks, so walk it slowly. Ridgeway Cycles now has associated companies. Companies are associated if one controls the other, or both are under common control. Overseas companies count. Dormant companies do not. Chapter three owns the rules.

Ridgeway Holdings is the first question. It controls Ridgeway Cycles, so it should count. But the twenty ten Act ignores a company that carries on no trade or business, and section eighteen F extends that to certain passive holding companies. To qualify, the holding company must carry on no trade, have at least one fifty one per cent subsidiary, and be passive throughout its accounting period. That means it holds no assets except shares in those subsidiaries, and has no income except dividends that it passes straight up to its own shareholders. In our invented case Ridgeway Holdings meets those conditions. So the Tarnmoor tax team writes a rule into the integration plan. Ridgeway Holdings must not keep a bank balance, must not lend money, and must pay on any dividend it receives in the same period. Break any of those, and it starts to count.

That leaves Tarnmoor's own family. After the demerger, the sale of Tarnmoor Actuators and the strike off of the dormant pumps company, Tarnmoor has ten companies that count, and Ridgeway Cycles makes eleven.

Now the two timings. For marginal relief, a company is associated if it is associated at any time in the accounting period. So for Ridgeway's first year in the group, its marginal relief limits are divided by eleven at once. That changes nothing in practice. Its profits were already far above the upper limit, so it already paid at the main rate.

Instalments are different. H M R C's manual says associated companies are counted as at the day before the accounting period begins. For Ridgeway's first group year, that day is the thirty first of March, the day before completion. On that day Ridgeway Cycles was owned through Jess's holding company, which is passive, and Jess owned nothing else that counts. Its divisor is one. The large company threshold is the full one and a half million pounds. Profits of six hundred thousand pounds are nowhere near it. So in its first year in the group, Ridgeway is not large and pays no instalments. Its tax is due nine months and one day after the year end, as before.

Its second year is the trap. On the day before that year begins, Ridgeway has ten associated companies. Its divisor is eleven. The large threshold becomes one and a half million pounds divided by eleven, one hundred and thirty six thousand, three hundred and sixty four pounds. The very large threshold is twenty million divided by eleven, about one point eight two million pounds. Ridgeway's profits are above the first and well below the second. So it is large. But the instalment regulations give a large company a year of grace. It does not pay by instalments if it was not large in the previous twelve months and its profits do not exceed ten million pounds, also divided by eleven, nine hundred and nine thousand and ninety one pounds. Ridgeway passes both tests. Still no instalments.

The third year is when the cash moves. The grace year still counts as a year in which the company was large. H M R C's manual and practitioners' worked examples read the regulation that way. So in its third group year Ridgeway pays as a large company. On profits like today's United Kingdom profits, its tax would be about one hundred thousand pounds, paid in four instalments of twenty five thousand, in months seven, ten, thirteen and sixteen. Treasury needs those dates two years in advance.

The same counting ripples back through the group. Every Tarnmoor company with a December year end has eleven associates for marginal relief in the Ridgeway year. For instalments, the count changes only from the period that begins the following first of January. A six hundred thousand pound business has moved every instalment threshold in a group of eleven companies. That is the misconception, refuted in one paragraph.

What the shares bring with them.

The second floor is the target's tax history, and here the inspection is short. Ridgeway Cycles has no trading losses to bring forward. The Dublin losses were relieved years ago, against its own profits. So the change of ownership rules in Part fourteen of the twenty ten Act, which chapter fourteen used on Brackenwell, have nothing to bite on. Ridgeway has no capital losses either, so the pre-entry loss rules of chapter seventeen are silent. Write one line saying so. A nil answer with a reason is still an answer.

What about past transfers between Ridgeway Holdings and Ridgeway Cycles? If either had moved an asset to the other at no gain, no loss within the last six years, the degrouping charge would be the worry. It does not arise. When the parent of a small group joins a larger one, the Gains Act treats the two groups as the same group, so neither Ridgeway company leaves a group at all. And even if one did, the two leave Jess's ownership together, and chapter seventeen explained why associated companies leaving together escape the charge.

Then the group regimes switch on. Ridgeway Cycles joins Tarnmoor's group for group relief on completion. Before that day nothing could pass, and from that day it can. Its year runs to March, while most of Tarnmoor's companies end in December. So any relief between them follows chapter fifteen's overlapping period rule, time-apportioned on each side.

Both new companies countersign the standing nomination that lets Tarnmoor P L C allocate the group's single five million pound deductions allowance, as chapter fourteen described. Ridgeway has no losses, so this costs it nothing. But a company left out of the nomination gets no allowance at all, and the paperwork is cheaper now than later.

The annual investment allowance is now shared. Chapter eight explained that a group has a single one million pound allowance for all members' periods ending in the same financial year to the thirty first of March. Ridgeway's first group year ends on a thirty first of March. It shares the allowance with the December periods of Tarnmoor's companies that ended three months earlier. Ridgeway gets whatever the group allocates to it, and if nobody allocates, it gets nothing.

Research and development changes too. Ridgeway is now linked to a large enterprise, so it is not a small or medium sized enterprise for any purpose. Any claim it makes is under the merged scheme's expenditure credit, at twenty per cent, taxable and set against its liability. The extra relief for loss-making intensive companies is out of reach. Chapter ten owns these rules.

The compliance settings change.

The fifth floor is governance, and it is the one candidates under-sell. In November twenty twenty four, question five set exactly this scenario. A multinational bought a small United Kingdom company, and candidates had to explain its new obligations. The marking guide put six and a half marks on transfer pricing alone.

Transfer pricing first. In Jess's hands Ridgeway was a small enterprise, so transfer pricing did not apply to it, as chapter twenty seven explained. That exemption measures size across linked enterprises. Linked to Tarnmoor, Ridgeway is large, and transfer pricing applies from completion. Its dealings with other United Kingdom group companies fall inside the exemption for United Kingdom to United Kingdom transactions, for chargeable periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six. Hold that thought, because the branch decision will test it.

The senior accounting officer rules turn on United Kingdom incorporation. Both Ridgeway companies are English. But a company joins the senior accounting officer's certificate only for a financial year in which it was a group member at the end of the previous one. Ridgeway's previous year ended on the thirty first of March, the day before completion. So its first year with the senior accounting officer, Nadia Kerr, is its second year in the group. The certificate for that year is due nine months after its end. Chapter four owns this.

Tarnmoor's published tax strategy now covers Ridgeway. Its returns must notify any uncertain tax treatment above the five million pound threshold, which is unlikely at this size, but the question must be asked. And the group's procedures to prevent the facilitation of tax evasion, the defence to the corporate criminal offence, must now reach a showroom in Dublin, whose staff are associated persons of Ridgeway Cycles. A foreign tax evasion facilitation offence exists for exactly that case.

Tarnmoor's country-by-country report and its transfer pricing master file now include Ridgeway, and the Dublin showroom's results appear among the group's Irish figures. Records that a family company kept for its own use must now meet a listed group's standard.

Administration tightens. Ridgeway is now a member of a group that is not small, so H M R C's enquiry window runs to twelve months after the filing date, not twelve months after delivery. And it cannot join Tarnmoor's group payment arrangement while its year end differs from the others'. Calder had the same problem until it moved to December.

The corporate interest restriction now sees Ridgeway as part of Tarnmoor's worldwide group. Tarnmoor's reporting company files for the whole group, and under the Finance Act twenty twenty six rules it must be appointed for each period by more than half of the eligible United Kingdom companies. Ridgeway Holdings and Ridgeway Cycles are now two of those. Tarnmoor still carries disallowed interest forward from earlier years. So every pound of tax E B I T D A has value. That is tax earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, and chapter twenty eight explained it. Ridgeway's profits add a little interest capacity. Keep that in mind too.

Last on this floor, the global minimum tax. Tarnmoor's revenue is far above seven hundred and fifty million euros, so Ridgeway's companies become constituent entities of the group at once. In the United Kingdom, domestic top-up tax covers them. The Dublin showroom is a permanent establishment, and the global anti-base erosion rules, the GloBE rules, treat it as a separate entity located in Ireland. That too will matter in a moment.

The Dublin question.

Now the sixth floor, which Jess left unfinished. Should Ridgeway Cycles elect to exempt its foreign branch profits? Chapter twenty five owns the rules, and three options are on the table. Elect for the exemption. Turn the branch into an Irish subsidiary. Or move the branch into Tarnmoor's existing Irish company, Tarnmoor Ireland.

Begin with the election, under section eighteen A of the two thousand and nine Act. Once it takes effect, the profits and losses of every foreign permanent establishment of the company are left out of its taxable profits. The branch's profit is measured as the United Kingdom and Ireland treaty would attribute it. Irish tax on exempt profits is not creditable, because there is no United Kingdom tax to credit it against.

Three features decide the answer. First, the election covers every branch of the company, now and in future, in every country. Second, it takes effect from the start of the company's next accounting period, which the statute calls the relevant day. Third, once that day arrives, it can never be revoked.

Then the trap The Living Law flagged, the opening negative amount. A company cannot take United Kingdom relief for branch losses and then elect just as the branch turns profitable. The law looks back six years before the election takes effect. Net branch losses in that window that later profits have not matched become an opening negative amount. Until that amount is matched by later branch profits, those profits stay taxable.

For Ridgeway the look-back is harmless. The branch lost one hundred and ten thousand pounds in its first two years, and made three hundred and twenty thousand pounds in the next two alone. By the end of its third year the losses were already matched, with ten thousand pounds to spare. Every year since has been profitable. Whatever the six year window catches, the branch's net result inside it is a profit. There is no opening negative amount.

So the arithmetic is simple. Today Ridgeway pays United Kingdom tax of one hundred and twenty five thousand pounds a year. With the election, its United Kingdom profit is four hundred thousand pounds and its tax is one hundred thousand. Ireland still collects its twenty five thousand. Total tax falls from one hundred and fifty thousand pounds to one hundred and twenty five thousand. The saving is twenty five thousand pounds a year, the United Kingdom top-up on the Irish profit.

Now the interactions, because a capstone answer must test that figure against the rest of the building. The full working is in the reading edition.

First, the interest restriction. Tax E B I T D A is built from taxable profits. Once the branch is exempt, its two hundred thousand pounds leaves Ridgeway's taxable profits and so leaves the group's tax E B I T D A. At thirty per cent, that is sixty thousand pounds of interest capacity. If the group is restricted, or is still reactivating old disallowed interest, that capacity is worth up to fifteen thousand pounds of tax. If the group has capacity to spare, it is worth nothing.

Second, the global minimum tax. Without the election, the United Kingdom tax on the branch's profit is treated as tax of the branch, so the Irish branch bears tax at the full United Kingdom rate. With it, the branch bears only twelve and a half per cent, below the fifteen per cent floor. On a simplified view that ignores the carve-out for real substance, that is up to five thousand pounds a year of top-up tax. Who collects it depends on Irish law, which this book does not teach. Either way, it is a cost to the group.

Third, transfer pricing. The exemption for United Kingdom to United Kingdom transactions does not apply to activities that the branch exemption takes out of charge. So if Tarnmoor ever charges Ridgeway for services used in Dublin, that charge must be at arm's length.

Fourth, the guard rail. An exempt branch could be a place to park mobile income, so the law withholds the exemption for branch profits that would pass the controlled foreign company gateway. A showroom selling bicycles is nowhere near that rule. Its profits sit below the low profits limit that the rule borrows from the controlled foreign company code.

Fifth, attribution. Without the election, the split of profit between Skipton and Dublin only moved the credit around. With it, every pound attributed to Dublin is taxed at twelve and a half per cent, not twenty five. H M R C will care about that split, so it must be documented on arm's length principles.

So the honest answer is that the election saves something between five thousand and twenty five thousand pounds a year, depending on the group's interest position and the minimum tax computation. It never costs money, on these facts, while the branch is profitable.

Two other doors.

The second option is to turn the branch into a company. Section one four oh of the Gains Act lets a United Kingdom company transfer a trade it carries on abroad through a permanent establishment to a non-resident company, with all its assets or all except cash, wholly or partly for shares. If it then holds at least a quarter of the new company's ordinary share capital, the net gain on the transfer is postponed, in proportion to the share part of the consideration. Ridgeway Cycles would hold all the shares of a new Irish subsidiary, so the conditions can be met. Chapter twenty five owns the rule.

Postponement is all it gives. The deferred gain returns if Ridgeway sells the shares, or if the subsidiary sells the transferred assets within six years. The branch's goodwill and customer relationships fall under the intangibles rules, not the gains rules, so chapter eleven's regime applies to them. That regime has its own matching claim, in section eight hundred and twenty seven of the two thousand and nine Act, which postpones the intangibles credit on similar conditions, plus a test that the transfer is genuinely commercial. Its plant leaves Ridgeway's pools with balancing adjustments.

Then the new company has to live somewhere. It is resident in Ireland only if its board really manages it from Ireland, as chapter twenty two explained. If Tarnmoor's executives took its decisions in Leeds, it would be United Kingdom resident, and the relief would fail at the first condition.

And it would be a controlled foreign company. Walk chapter twenty six's order of attack. It is a non-resident company controlled from the United Kingdom, so it is a controlled foreign company. Take the entity exemptions next. The tax exemption fails. Irish tax at twelve and a half per cent is below seventy five per cent of the United Kingdom rate, which is eighteen point seven five per cent. Do not rely on the excluded territories exemption either. Ireland's place on that list was not confirmed for this book. But the low profits exemption works. Profits of two hundred thousand pounds are within the five hundred thousand pound limit, and almost none of them are non-trading. So there is no charge today. If the showroom grew, the next stop would be the trading profits gateway, and that would need a careful look at where its people and decisions really sit.

So the subsidiary reaches the same twelve and a half per cent as the elected branch, but by a longer road. It means a transfer, a new company, residence management, transfer pricing on every bicycle Skipton sells to Dublin, and controlled foreign company monitoring every year. Its one advantage is that it leaves Ridgeway Cycles free of an irrevocable election.

The third option fails at once. If Ridgeway Cycles transferred the Dublin trade to Tarnmoor Ireland for new shares, it would hold a tiny fraction of that much larger company, far below a quarter, so section one four oh is unavailable. Nor can the transfer be at no gain, no loss within the gains group, because Tarnmoor Ireland has been resident in Ireland since Group Year Four, and the Dublin assets would be outside the United Kingdom charge in its hands. Gains, and intangibles credits at an arm's length value, would all crystallise now, to move a bicycle showroom into a valve distributor.

Ireland, unlike the invented Vallaria of chapter twenty five, is a member state of the European Union. So the old cross-border relief in section one four oh C is at least in view. Chapter twenty five explained why its wording after Brexit needs care, and Tom does not rely on it.

The recommendation.

Tom's paper to the boards of Tarnmoor and Ridgeway Cycles, in our invented case, recommends the election. Three reasons. It removes the United Kingdom top-up on the Dublin profit, a saving of up to twenty five thousand pounds a year. Its costs are small and fit the facts. Ridgeway has no other foreign branches and no plans for any, and if Tarnmoor ever wants a loss-making venture abroad, it can put it in a different company. And unlike incorporation, it needs no transfer, no new company and no annual controlled foreign company review.

Timing comes next. Ridgeway Cycles makes the election in its first year in the group. The relevant day is the first of April after that, the start of its next accounting period. So in its first group year the Dublin profit is still taxed in the United Kingdom, with credit, and the exemption runs from its second year. Tom did notice, while the heads of terms were being drafted, that an election made by Jess's company before completion would have started on completion day, a year earlier. He chose not to ask for it. It would have been an irrevocable choice made before Tarnmoor's due diligence had finished, for a prize of twenty five thousand pounds. In our invented case the board minute records that reasoning, and the election is made only after the board has approved it. The election is the company's own act, so the minute and a copy of the election go on the permanent file, where a finance director ten years from now can find out why the Dublin profits are missing from the return.

The paper also records what is coming. Draft legislation for the Finance Bill twenty twenty six to twenty seven, published in July twenty twenty six, would make the branch exemption compulsory for accounting periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty seven. It would replace the opening negative amount rules with restrictions on carrying branch losses forward. That is proposed, not law. Under the Finance Act twenty twenty six, which your exam tests, the exemption is still an election. Ridgeway has no branch losses to carry forward, so even if the draft became law, its answer would not change.

One last point on the branch. If Tarnmoor later moves Ridgeway's year end to December, as it did with Calder, the election timing and the instalment tests both need to be run again, because both key off the dates of accounting periods.

The group accounts.

The seventh floor is not tax law at all, and it is where the examiner's tax accounting marks hide. Tarnmoor's consolidated accounts must record Ridgeway's assets at fair value on the acquisition date. The valuers find a brand and customer relationships that Ridgeway's own accounts never showed, worth eight hundred thousand pounds between them.

A share purchase changes nothing in Ridgeway's tax position. Ridgeway Cycles built that brand itself, so it has no tax cost, and buying the shares gives no step up. In the group accounts, then, the assets carry eight hundred thousand pounds more than their tax base. That is a taxable temporary difference, and at twenty five per cent it needs a deferred tax liability of two hundred thousand pounds. The liability is part of the acquisition accounting. It increases goodwill by the same two hundred thousand pounds. No deferred tax is recognised on goodwill itself, because goodwill is the residual, and grossing it up would only increase it again. Chapter six owns these rules.

As the group accounts amortise the customer relationships, the liability unwinds through the deferred tax charge, and the group's effective tax rate is unaffected. If the brand is treated as having an indefinite life and is not amortised, its share of the liability stays. None of this touches Ridgeway's own accounts or its tax return. Two more accounting points belong on the list. The accounting standard for business combinations allows up to a year after the acquisition to finalise the fair values, so the deferred tax figure can move during that year, and goodwill moves with it. And any top-up tax that the Dublin branch might bear under the global minimum rules creates no deferred tax at all, because the accounting standards carry a specific exception for it, as chapter six explained.

The planning point is documentation. The purchase price allocation, meaning the split between net assets, identified intangibles and goodwill, drives the deferred tax, the impairment tests and later the gain on any sale. Tom files the valuers' report with the acquisition papers, alongside the board minute on the election. In an answer, the deferred tax needs three points, not one. The temporary difference and why it arises. The rate and the figure. And where the other side of the entry goes. Candidates who stop at the liability leave the last of those on the table.

Keeping Jess.

The eighth floor is people. Tarnmoor wants Jess to stay, so she remains managing director of Ridgeway Cycles for two years, on a salary of ninety thousand pounds. On top, she gets a retention bonus of sixty thousand pounds, payable twenty four months after completion if she is still there. Both are paid by Ridgeway Cycles, whose trade she serves. Chapter twenty explained why that matters. If Tarnmoor P L C paid instead, it would be paying for services to another company and might get no deduction.

The salary is an ordinary trading deduction. The bonus is a timing puzzle, and the rule is the one chapter seven taught. If remuneration charged in the accounts is not paid within nine months after the end of the period of account, it is deductible only when paid.

Suppose Ridgeway accrues the bonus evenly, thirty thousand pounds in each of its first two group years. The bonus is paid on the first of April two years after completion, the first day of its third group year. The first year's thirty thousand is paid twelve months after that year ends, too late. So it is deducted in the third year, when paid. The second year's thirty thousand is paid one day after that year ends, well within nine months. So it is deducted in the second year. Same sixty thousand pounds, two different years.

One adviser's check. The bonus must really be pay for staying and working, at a commercial level, conditional on service. If the facts showed it was really part of the price for Jess's shares, Ridgeway Cycles would be paying a capital sum on its owner's behalf and would get no deduction at all. The service condition and the market level of the pay are the evidence. The same thinking applies to any payment to Jess for loss of office if she leaves early. H M R C's view, as chapter twenty explained, is that a payment connected with a change of ownership is unlikely to be wholly and exclusively for the trade.

And one line for the other Hartley in these pages. Dan Hartley, made redundant when Calder closed his division in Group Year Three, is working elsewhere now. This book leaves his later career where it found it, open.

How the examiner would mark it.

Imagine this deal set as a twenty mark question. You have about forty two minutes, at roughly two point one minutes a mark. The marking guide will give half a mark to a mark for each correct point. That means twenty to forty separate, findable points.

The guide for November twenty twenty four's question five is the model. It gave four marks to filing and instalments, three to country-by-country reporting, six and a half to transfer pricing, three and a half to the interest restriction, and three to stamp duty. The examiners reported that most candidates found transfer pricing, but some wrote at length on it and ignored easier points, and some raised hybrid mismatches that nothing in the question triggered.

So the order of work matters. Bank the quick marks first. Stamp duty of twenty two thousand, five hundred pounds, payable by the buyer within thirty days. The associated company count and the two timings. No instalments in the first year, the grace year in the second, instalments from the third. The senior accounting officer from the second year. Transfer pricing switched on because the exemption for small companies is lost. Each is one line with a reason. Add the points that score for saying nothing changes. No losses to restrict. No degrouping charge. No stamp duty land tax. A marker can only tick what is on the page, and a correct nil, with its reason, is a point like any other.

Two final disciplines. Do not restate Jess's history. The marker knows it. And if the question asks about corporation tax only, Jess's capital gains tax and the stamp duty are outside it, however much you know about them.

What to take away.

A small acquisition is not a small tax job. The size of the group the target joins sets the compliance load. So walk every floor in the same order. The transaction, the target's history, the thresholds, the group regimes, governance and compliance, the special feature, the accounts, the people, and then the recommendation.

In our invented case, Tarnmoor P L C bought Ridgeway Holdings from Jess Hartley on the first of April, some years after Group Year Seven, for four and a half million pounds. Stamp duty was twenty two thousand, five hundred pounds, paid by the buyer within thirty days and added to the cost of the shares. There was no stamp duty land tax, and no group relief from an individual.

Ridgeway Holdings is a passive holding company under section eighteen F of the twenty ten Act, so it does not count as an associated company. Keep it passive. Ridgeway Cycles has ten associated companies. For marginal relief they count at once. For instalments they count on the day before each accounting period begins. So its divisor is one in its first group year, and it is not large. In its second year the divisor is eleven and the large threshold one hundred and thirty six thousand, three hundred and sixty four pounds, but the year of grace applies. It pays instalments from its third year, about twenty five thousand pounds a quarter.

No losses come with the shares, and there is no degrouping charge. Group relief, the deductions allowance and a share of the annual investment allowance start on completion. Transfer pricing applies, because the small company exemption is lost. The senior accounting officer covers Ridgeway from its second group year. The interest restriction and the global minimum tax now include it.

Ridgeway Cycles should elect for the branch exemption, from the start of its second group year. There is no opening negative amount, because one hundred and ten thousand pounds of early losses were matched by three hundred and twenty thousand pounds of profit. United Kingdom tax falls from one hundred and twenty five thousand pounds to one hundred thousand, a saving of up to twenty five thousand a year. Lost interest capacity, up to fifteen thousand pounds, and minimum top-up tax, up to five thousand, may take some back. The election is irrevocable and covers every future branch. Incorporation only postpones gains and creates a controlled foreign company, exempt today on low profits. A transfer to Tarnmoor Ireland fails the twenty five per cent condition.

In the group accounts, the eight hundred thousand pound uplift on the brand and customer relationships carries a deferred tax liability of two hundred thousand pounds, which increases goodwill. Jess stays for two years on ninety thousand pounds a year, and her sixty thousand pound retention bonus is deducted thirty thousand in the second group year and thirty thousand in the third, because of the nine month rule.

So why does a small deal touch every rule in the book? Because a company joining a group inherits the group's thresholds, its regimes and its obligations, some at once and some one period later. The answer to Jess's last open question is yes, elect. Under the law your exam tests, it is still a choice, and on these facts it is a good one.

Chapter four: Governance and the anti-avoidance architecture.

In August twenty twenty five, H M R C charged a company with a crime that no company in Britain had ever been charged with before. The company was an accountancy firm in Stockport. The charge was failing to prevent the criminal facilitation of tax evasion, under section forty five of the Criminal Finances Act twenty seventeen. According to the published reports, it was linked to an alleged fraud involving research and development tax credit repayments, and several individuals were charged with separate offences. These are allegations only. Nothing has been proved, and the reports say the trial is listed for September twenty twenty seven, after your exam.

The offence had been on the statute book since the thirtieth of September, twenty seventeen. For almost eight years it sat unused, and commentators called it a paper tiger. Then it had teeth. The striking thing about it is this. A company can commit the offence without any director knowing anything, without the company gaining a penny, and without the company itself evading any tax. Its crime is to have failed to prevent someone acting for it from helping someone else to evade.

This chapter asks a question that sounds managerial but is pure law. Who, inside a large group, answers to H M R C for the tax, and what does the law make them do? It closes Part One of this book. On the Taxation of Larger Companies and Groups paper, the senior accounting officer rules, the publication of a tax strategy and the corporate criminal offence are all core. The disclosure of avoidance schemes, follower notices, accelerated payment notices, the general anti-abuse rule and the line between avoidance and evasion are non-core. Notification of uncertain tax treatment and alternative dispute resolution are awareness topics. The chapter builds on The Living Law, chapter three, which told the story of avoidance, evasion and the courts. As always, we apply the law for the financial year twenty twenty six.

The ladder of accountability.

For most of the twentieth century, H M R C's question to a company was simple. Is this return right? The modern question to a large group is different. Are your systems the kind that produce right returns, and who will put their name to that?

The shift happened because large groups are too big to audit line by line. A group like the one we follow in this book files dozens of returns across many taxes every year. An inspector cannot check every journal. So the law moved upstream. It now regulates the people and processes that produce the numbers, and it makes the group say publicly how it approaches tax. Does transparency actually change behaviour? Supporters point to better resourced tax functions and fewer surprises on enquiry. Critics point to box-ticking certificates and boilerplate strategies. The book returns to that debate in its conclusion.

Think of the rules as a ladder with four rungs. On the first rung, a named individual, the senior accounting officer, must take reasonable steps to make sure the company's tax accounting arrangements are sound, and must certify them every year. On the second, the group's board publishes its attitude to tax for anyone to read. On the third, the group must flag to H M R C, unprompted, any large tax position it treats as uncertain. On the fourth, the company itself becomes criminally liable if it fails to prevent people acting for it from facilitating tax evasion.

Beside the ladder stands a second structure, aimed at avoidance rather than governance. Promoters and users must disclose certain schemes before H M R C has even looked. H M R C can demand payment of disputed tax up front. It can tell a taxpayer to drop a position that the courts have already rejected in someone else's case. And the general anti-abuse rule sits over the whole of tax law as a final backstop.

The thresholds that switch on the ladder are the ones chapter one placed on the threshold ladder. The senior accounting officer rules, the tax strategy rules and the uncertain tax treatment rules all start at a turnover of more than two hundred million pounds or a balance sheet total of more than two billion pounds. But, and this is the point the examiner loves, each regime adds up different companies to reach those figures.

Our running case is the invented Tarnmoor group, a widely held, London listed engineering group with its head office in Leeds. Every person and company in it is invented for teaching. Its chief financial officer, Nadia Kerr, is the senior accounting officer for every United Kingdom company in the group. Its group head of tax, Tom Hesketh, runs the tax function. Tarnmoor's consolidated revenue in Group Year One was one thousand one hundred and eighty million pounds, and its main trading company, Tarnmoor Engineering, turned over six hundred and thirty million pounds on its own. Every rung of the ladder applies.

Who must have a senior accounting officer.

The senior accounting officer rules are in Schedule forty six to the Finance Act two thousand and nine. They apply for financial years beginning on or after the twenty first of July, two thousand and nine. In these rules, financial year means the company's own accounting year under company law, not the corporation tax financial year.

The rules bite on a qualifying company. Two conditions must both be met. First, the company must be incorporated in the United Kingdom under the Companies Act two thousand and six or its predecessors. Tax residence is irrelevant. H M R C's guidance says so in terms, because the regime hangs on company law, not tax law. Second, in the preceding financial year, the company's relevant turnover must have been more than two hundred million pounds, or its relevant balance sheet total more than two billion pounds, or both.

The group rule is where the work lies. If the company was a member of a group at the end of its preceding financial year, its figures are aggregated with those of the other United Kingdom incorporated companies in that group. Group here means a company and its fifty one per cent subsidiaries, using the definition in section eleven fifty four of the Corporation Tax Act twenty ten. H M R C's guidance adds two refinements. Intra-group turnover is included in the aggregate. But in adding up balance sheets, investments in other United Kingdom group companies are left out, so the same assets are not counted twice.

Notice what this does. Once the aggregate passes the threshold, every United Kingdom incorporated company in the group is a qualifying company, however small. A dormant subsidiary is a qualifying company. A company with a turnover of ten thousand pounds is a qualifying company. Each needs a senior accounting officer, and each must be named to H M R C.

Now apply it to Tarnmoor. In Group Year One, the qualifying companies are Tarnmoor P L C, Tarnmoor Engineering, Tarnmoor Estates, Tarnmoor Finance, Tarnmoor Water Systems and the dormant Tarnmoor Pumps. Four companies fall outside. Tarnmoor Vallaria and Tarnmoor Capital are incorporated abroad, in the invented countries of Vallaria and Marrovia. Tarnmoor Ireland is incorporated in Ireland, even though its central management and control sits in Leeds and it is resident in the United Kingdom for corporation tax. For the senior accounting officer rules, that residence counts for nothing. And Helmside Energy, the hydrogen joint venture, is only forty five per cent owned, so it is not in the fifty one per cent group.

Then the acquisitions. Tarnmoor buys Calder Valve Engineering on the first of April in Group Year One. Calder has a thirty first of March year end. For Calder's financial year starting that very day, the preceding financial year ended on the thirty first of March, the day before the deal, when Calder belonged to the Oldroyd family and to no group. Its own turnover was far below two hundred million pounds. So Calder is not a qualifying company for its first year in the group. For its next financial year, starting on the first of April in Group Year Two, the test looks at the end of the year just finished, when Calder was firmly inside Tarnmoor. Calder qualifies from then on. Brackenwell Sensors, bought on the first of July in Group Year Two, with a December year end, follows the same logic. It is outside the regime for Group Year Two and inside it from Group Year Three.

This is exactly the kind of reasoning the examiner rewards. A November twenty twenty three question gave candidates a list of companies, including a fifty per cent subsidiary and a company incorporated in Greece but resident in the United Kingdom, and asked which counted.

The main duty, the certificate and the penalties.

The senior accounting officer is the director or officer who has overall responsibility for the company's financial accounting arrangements. In a group, one person can hold the role for many companies, as Nadia Kerr does.

The main duty has three parts. The senior accounting officer must take reasonable steps to ensure that the company establishes and maintains appropriate tax accounting arrangements. The officer must take reasonable steps to monitor those arrangements. And the officer must identify any respects in which they fall short. Appropriate tax accounting arrangements are, in H M R C's words, arrangements that allow the company's tax liabilities to be calculated accurately in all material respects. The taxes covered are wide. Corporation tax, including the quarterly instalment payments, value added tax, P A Y E, insurance premium tax, stamp duty land tax, stamp duty reserve tax, customs and excise duties, and a few specialist taxes.

Here is an analogy. The senior accounting officer is the person who signs the boiler's safety certificate, not the person who lights it. Nadia does not prepare the returns. Tom's team and the finance teams in each company do. Her job is to make sure there is a properly designed, properly tested system for producing them, and to say honestly whether it worked.

Then the paperwork. For each financial year, the company must notify H M R C of the name of its senior accounting officer. The officer must give H M R C a certificate stating whether the company had appropriate tax accounting arrangements throughout the year, and if not, explaining the shortcomings. A qualified certificate is not a failure. It is the regime working. Both the notification and the certificate are due by the end of the period allowed for filing the company's accounts under company law. That is six months after the year end for a public company and nine months for a private company. Groups may file combined notifications.

The penalties are fixed at five thousand pounds. The company is liable if it fails to notify. The senior accounting officer is personally liable for breaching the main duty, for failing to give a certificate on time, and for giving a certificate that is inaccurate through carelessness or deliberately. The penalties are not reduced for disclosure or cooperation. A reasonable excuse can defeat them, except for an inaccurate certificate.

Two tribunal decisions show the regime in action. In Thathiah versus H M R C, decided by the First-tier Tribunal in twenty seventeen, H M R C lost the first senior accounting officer penalty appeal. As reported, the tribunal held that H M R C had asked the wrong question. It had focused on whether the officer had a reasonable excuse, rather than on whether he had breached the main duty at all. Errors, even repeated ones, do not automatically prove that the officer failed to take reasonable steps. In Castlelaw, decided in twenty twenty, the tribunal, again as reported, upheld penalties on a company and its senior accounting officer for leaving a dormant company off the group notification. It also said that H M R C should have considered its discretion on the facts. H M R C's guidance was later updated to say that penalties should not normally be charged where a dormant company is omitted and the risk is low.

So in our invented case, Tarnmoor Pumps, dormant and forgotten by most of the group, stays on Nadia's list every year.

Publishing a tax strategy.

The second rung is public. Schedule nineteen to the Finance Act twenty sixteen, which I will call the tax strategy rules, requires large groups and companies to publish their tax strategy on the internet, free of charge, every year. The rules apply for financial years beginning after the fifteenth of September, twenty sixteen.

There are two routes in. The first is size. A United Kingdom group, sub-group, company or partnership qualifies if, in its previous financial year, its turnover was more than two hundred million pounds or its balance sheet total more than two billion pounds. For a group, the figures of all the United Kingdom members are added together, and group again means a fifty one per cent group. The second route is the country-by-country reporting threshold. United Kingdom entities of a multinational group with global revenue of at least seven hundred and fifty million euros qualify. H M R C's guidance treats the routes as mutually exclusive, and it draws a striking conclusion. A United Kingdom company in a multinational group with global turnover below seven hundred and fifty million euros need not publish, even if it turns over more than two hundred million pounds itself.

The duty falls on the head of the group, the company that is not a fifty one per cent subsidiary of another group member. Where the head is incorporated abroad, it falls on the top United Kingdom company. Any group member may actually publish.

The strategy must cover four things. The group's approach to risk management and governance for United Kingdom tax, its attitude to tax planning, the level of risk it is prepared to accept, and its approach to dealing with H M R C. It must say that it is published in compliance with the statutory duty. Timing has two limits. It must be published before the end of the financial year to which it relates. And once a group has published, each new strategy must appear within fifteen months of the last.

The penalties escalate. Seven thousand five hundred pounds for failing to publish a compliant strategy. Another seven thousand five hundred pounds if it is still missing six months after it was due. Then seven thousand five hundred pounds for each further month. H M R C's guidance says it will usually warn first.

The trap the examiners found in May twenty twenty five concerns year ends. When group members have different year ends from the head company, which figures go into the test? H M R C's guidance answers that each member uses its latest financial year ending before the end of the head company's financial year. Overlapping periods are not apportioned. The examiners reported that many candidates apportioned subsidiary figures instead of using the accounts for the year ending in the parent's financial year, and lost marks for it.

In our invented case, Tarnmoor qualifies by both routes. Its consolidated revenue, at the story's assumed rate of one point one five euros to the pound, is about one point three six billion euros, and its United Kingdom turnover alone is far above two hundred million pounds. Tarnmoor P L C, as head of the group, publishes the group's strategy by the thirty first of December each year. It says, among other things, that the group does not use marketed tax avoidance schemes. Remember that sentence. It will matter later in this chapter.

Uncertain tax treatment, two triggers and five million pounds.

The third rung is the newest. Schedule seventeen to the Finance Act twenty twenty two, the uncertain tax treatment rules, requires large businesses to tell H M R C when a return relies on a position that is uncertain. It applies to returns required to be filed on or after the first of April, twenty twenty two. The idea is simple. H M R C would rather hear about a large disputed position when the return is filed than find it years later.

The size test uses the same numbers, but a different list of companies. A company qualifies if, in its previous financial year, its relevant United Kingdom turnover was more than two hundred million pounds or its United Kingdom balance sheet total more than two billion pounds. Those figures are aggregated across the members of its fifty one per cent group that are within the charge to corporation tax. So the uncertain tax treatment rules count Tarnmoor Ireland, which is resident and taxed here, even though the senior accounting officer rules ignore it because it was incorporated in Dublin. Do not carry one regime's list of companies into the other.

There are two triggers, and only two. The first is the provision trigger. An amount in the return is uncertain if the company has made a provision in its accounts to reflect the probability that a different tax treatment will be applied. The second is the known position trigger. An amount is uncertain if it relies on an interpretation or application of the law that is not in accordance with the way H M R C is known to interpret or apply it, whether from published guidance or from dealings with the company.

Even then, notification is needed only where the tax advantage from the uncertain amount, in the relevant period, is more than five million pounds. Related amounts are aggregated. And there is an exemption where it is reasonable for the company to conclude that H M R C already has all, or substantially all, of the information. That might be because the company has already discussed the point with its H M R C contact, or because the arrangements were disclosed under another regime.

For corporation tax, the notification is due by the later of the return's filing date and the deadline for filing the company's accounts. The penalties are five thousand pounds for a first failure, twenty five thousand pounds for a second within the preceding three years, and fifty thousand pounds for a further failure.

The regime is under review. A consultation published on the twelfth of March, twenty twenty six, closing on the fourth of June, proposed extending the rules to more taxes and to wealthy individuals and trusts, and adding a further trigger. That is proposed, not law.

A notification in practice.

Here is the misconception. Many people believe that the uncertain tax treatment rules mean telling H M R C whenever you are unsure. They do not. Uncertainty alone triggers nothing. The statute requires one of two specific triggers, an advantage above five million pounds, and no exemption. Interestingly, the draft legislation once had a third trigger, based on a substantial possibility that a tribunal would disagree with the treatment. It was dropped before the Act was passed. The twenty twenty six consultation would bring a further trigger back. For your exam, there are two.

Now the invented story. In Group Year Two, Tarnmoor Engineering finishes implementing a new enterprise resource planning system, the software that runs its finance, purchasing and production. The implementation costs are twenty four million pounds. Tom's team concludes that the costs are revenue expenditure and deducts them. But the question is genuinely arguable. The auditors agree that the company should book a provision against the risk that the deduction is denied.

Work through the tests. Tarnmoor Engineering is a qualifying company, because its own United Kingdom turnover is six hundred and thirty million pounds. The provision is the first trigger. The tax advantage for the year is twenty four million pounds at twenty five per cent, which is six million pounds. That is above five million pounds. H M R C has not been told about it, so the exemption does not help. Notification is required.

The deadline follows the corporation tax return. Tarnmoor Engineering's year ends on the thirty first of December. Its accounts are due nine months later and its return twelve months later, so the notification is due by the thirty first of December in Group Year Three. Tom files it with the return.

The notification itself is made on H M R C's online form. H M R C's statutory notice says what it must contain, including the company's name, the tax concerned, the tax references and the periods affected, and it warns that an incomplete notification will not be accepted as valid. It describes the treatment and the amount in question. It is not a full technical paper. Notice too that most of the advantage here is about timing. If the costs are capital, Tarnmoor Engineering may get relief later, through capital allowances or the intangibles rules. But the statutory question is the tax advantage in the period of the return, and in Group Year Two that is six million pounds.

Two practical points follow. First, notification is not a concession. It records that the position is uncertain. It does not admit that it is wrong. Second, the provision that triggered the notification also feeds the accounts. Chapter six explains how the tax charge and the uncertain tax provision appear in the financial statements.

The corporate criminal offence.

Back to the offence that opened the chapter. Part three of the Criminal Finances Act twenty seventeen created two corporate offences. They came into force on the thirtieth of September, twenty seventeen. They are modelled on the failure to prevent bribery offence in the Bribery Act twenty ten, and they share its logic.

The law asks for three stages. First, a taxpayer, which might be a customer, a supplier or anyone else, commits a criminal tax evasion offence. Second, a person associated with the company criminally facilitates that evasion while acting in that capacity. Third, the company failed to prevent the associated person from doing it.

An associated person is an employee, an agent, or anyone else who performs services for or on behalf of the company, acting in that capacity. That reaches far beyond the payroll, to sales agents, distributors, consultants and contractors.

Section forty five covers the evasion of United Kingdom tax. Section forty six covers foreign tax, with two extra conditions. There must be dual criminality, meaning the conduct is criminal under the foreign law and would be criminal here. And there must be a United Kingdom connection. The company is incorporated under United Kingdom law, or carries on business here, or part of the facilitation happens here.

The defence is reasonable prevention procedures. A company is not guilty if, when the facilitation happened, it had in place such prevention procedures as it was reasonable in all the circumstances to expect, or if it was not reasonable to expect it to have any. The burden is on the company to prove it. The penalty on conviction is an unlimited fine. The offences apply to a relevant body, which means a body corporate or a partnership. The individuals involved, the evader and the facilitator, remain liable for their own offences, which are prosecuted separately.

Notice what the offence does not require. The company need not benefit. The board need not know. The company need not have been dishonest in any way. That is why the offence matters so much to a large group's governance. The only protection is to have the procedures in place before anything goes wrong.

This is a core topic for your paper, but the examiners have not set a question on it in the sittings from May twenty twenty three to May twenty twenty six. When it comes, expect the three stages, the meaning of associated person, the foreign tax conditions and the defence.

Reasonable prevention procedures.

What counts as reasonable? H M R C's official guidance is not law, but it is the yardstick a court is likely to use. It sets out six guiding principles. Risk assessment, which drives everything else. Proportionality of procedures to the risk. Top level commitment from senior management. Due diligence on associated persons, proportionate to risk. Communication, including training. And monitoring and review.

The guidance points to the risk factors a group should look at, among them the countries it operates in, its sector, and the kinds of transactions and business opportunities it pursues. A group that sells through agents in jurisdictions with weak tax administration has a different risk from one that sells only to United Kingdom utilities.

Now the invented incident. In Group Year Three, a customer of Tarnmoor Engineering in the invented country of Marrovia rings the group's whistleblowing line. Tarnmoor Engineering's local sales agent, a self-employed individual paid on commission, has offered to split an invoice so that part of the price can be paid offshore and kept out of the customer's Marrovian tax return.

Tom's team works through the stages. The agent performs services for Tarnmoor Engineering, so he is an associated person. The tax is foreign, so section forty six is the relevant offence. Tarnmoor Engineering is a United Kingdom company, so the connection condition is met. Whether a facilitation offence was committed would depend on whether the conduct is criminal under Marrovian law and would be criminal here, and whether the customer went on to evade. In our story the customer refused and reported it at once.

What protects Tarnmoor is what it had done earlier. Its risk assessment had flagged agents in Marrovia as high risk. Its agency contracts prohibited facilitation and allowed termination. Its agents had completed training. And its whistleblowing line worked. The agent is dismissed. The group reviews its other Marrovian agents and records what it found. Tom takes legal advice on whether to report the matter to the authorities. That is a judgement for lawyers, and the book does not pretend to answer it here.

The incident also shows how the rungs of the ladder connect. The same risk assessment that protects Tarnmoor under the Criminal Finances Act is evidence for Nadia that the group's systems are sound. The procedures are only as good as the evidence of them, so Tom keeps the records of training, contracts and the investigation. If a prosecutor ever asked what Tarnmoor had in place, the answer would be in the files, not in anyone's memory.

Avoidance, evasion and the disclosure rules.

The Living Law, chapter three, drew the line between evasion and avoidance. Evasion is illegal. It involves dishonesty, such as concealing income or inflating costs. Avoidance uses the law to reduce tax, sometimes in ways Parliament never intended. It is lawful, but it often fails. Since the Ramsay case in nineteen eighty one, the courts have read tax statutes purposively and looked at transactions realistically, and the Supreme Court confirmed that approach in the Rangers case in twenty seventeen. The profession's own standards, in Professional Conduct in Relation to Taxation, go further. They forbid advisers to create, encourage or promote planning that is contrary to the clear intention of Parliament, or that is highly artificial or highly contrived and exploits shortcomings in the legislation.

Parliament added machinery on top of the courts. The first piece is the disclosure of tax avoidance schemes regime, called DOTAS, in Part seven of the Finance Act two thousand and four. Its logic is speed. If H M R C learns about a scheme when it is first marketed, it can close it by legislation or challenge it before it spreads.

Arrangements are notifiable if they fall within one of the prescribed descriptions, called hallmarks, and are expected to give a tax advantage as one of their main benefits. The hallmarks include confidentiality, where a promoter would want to keep the scheme secret from H M R C or from other promoters, and the premium fee, where the fee is attributable to a significant extent to the tax advantage or is contingent on it. Others cover standardised tax products and particular kinds of loss, leasing and employment schemes. Both of those hallmarks are tested hypothetically, by asking what a promoter might reasonably be expected to want. Even so, in a twenty twenty two case brought by H M R C against a promoter, the First-tier Tribunal held that the premium fee hallmark was not met where no such fee was in fact paid.

The promoter must normally notify H M R C within five days of making the scheme available or of its first implementation. H M R C issues a scheme reference number. The promoter passes it to clients, and each user reports it to H M R C on its return. Where there is no promoter, as with a scheme devised in-house, the user notifies.

Failure is expensive. The Finance Act twenty twenty six rebuilt the penalty regime in a new section three fifteen of the Finance Act two thousand and four. The core is a daily penalty of up to six hundred pounds, rising to five thousand pounds a day once H M R C has obtained an order that the arrangements are notifiable, and up to one million pounds where a lower penalty would be inappropriately low. And if a user's tax loss is connected with a failure to comply with DOTAS, H M R C can make a discovery assessment up to twenty years after the end of the accounting period.

Follower notices and accelerated payment notices.

Disclosure tells H M R C about a scheme. It does not collect the tax. For years, users of failed schemes kept the money while their appeals ran, sometimes for a decade. Part four of the Finance Act twenty fourteen changed the economics with two notices.

A follower notice can be given where a return or claim relies on particular arrangements, an enquiry or appeal is open, and H M R C is of the opinion that a final judicial ruling in another case, on arrangements like these, would deny the advantage. The taxpayer is told to take corrective action, which means giving up the advantage. If it refuses and later loses, it pays a penalty of thirty per cent of the denied advantage, reduced to twenty per cent in one statutory case.

The Supreme Court set a limit on this power in Haworth versus H M R C, decided on the second of July, twenty twenty one. H M R C had issued a follower notice relying on the Court of Appeal's earlier decision in Smallwood, about a scheme that moved a trust round the world. The court unanimously upheld the quashing of the notice. It held, as the commentators summarise it, that H M R C may give a follower notice only where it considers there is no real room for a reasonable person to disagree that the earlier ruling defeats the taxpayer's claim. A notice cannot be used where the earlier case is merely helpful to H M R C.

An accelerated payment notice goes further. It requires the disputed tax to be paid while the dispute continues. H M R C can issue one where an enquiry or appeal is open and a follower notice has been given, or the arrangements are notifiable under DOTAS, or a counteraction notice has been given under the general anti-abuse rule. The tax must be paid within ninety days, or thirty days after H M R C has considered any representations, if later. There is no appeal against the notice itself. If the taxpayer later wins, the money comes back with interest.

For a large group, the effect is direct. A marketed scheme now carries a cash cost from the start. The tax saving is no longer an interest-free loan from the Exchequer while the case runs. And a group that receives a follower notice must decide quickly. Taking corrective action means amending the return and accepting the tax. Refusing means betting the penalty on beating a ruling that H M R C, after Haworth, must believe is decisive.

The general anti-abuse rule.

The general anti-abuse rule, the GAAR, is in Part five of the Finance Act twenty thirteen, and it has applied since the seventeenth of July, twenty thirteen. The Living Law, chapter three, told how it grew out of Graham Aaronson's report for the government. Here we need its mechanics.

It works in two stages. First, there must be tax arrangements, meaning that it would be reasonable to conclude that obtaining a tax advantage was the main purpose, or one of the main purposes, of the arrangements. Second, those arrangements must be abusive. That is the double reasonableness test. Arrangements are abusive if entering into them or carrying them out cannot reasonably be regarded as a reasonable course of action in relation to the relevant tax provisions, having regard to all the circumstances. The circumstances include whether the result is consistent with the principles and policy of the provisions, whether the arrangements involve contrived or abnormal steps, and whether they exploit shortcomings in the legislation. The statute lists indicators of abuse, such as income or profits for tax purposes far lower than the economic reality, and indicators that point the other way, such as arrangements that accord with established practice H M R C has accepted.

If the GAAR applies, the advantage is counteracted on a just and reasonable basis. Before that, the case must go to the independent GAAR Advisory Panel, whose opinion the tribunal must take into account. And since the Finance Act twenty sixteen, a penalty of sixty per cent of the counteracted advantage applies.

Why does it exist alongside the hundreds of targeted anti-avoidance rules in the corporate code? Because targeted rules can only reach the schemes their drafters foresaw. The GAAR is designed for the egregious scheme nobody foresaw. It is deliberately narrow. Ordinary tax planning, such as choosing between debt and equity, claiming an allowance, or electing for a relief, is not abusive.

The GAAR is not limited to income tax. It applies to corporation tax, including amounts charged as if they were corporation tax, and to the other main direct taxes and stamp duty land tax. It also works alongside the courts' own approach. The Ramsay line of cases asks what the statute, read purposively, means for the facts viewed realistically. The GAAR asks a different question, whether the arrangement is abusive even if it works on a purposive reading. In a large group, the targeted rules usually decide the case first. The specific purpose tests you will meet in this book, such as the unallowable purpose rule for loans in chapter twelve and the main purpose tests on share exchanges in chapter eighteen, catch most corporate schemes long before the GAAR is needed.

The loss refresh pitch.

Now watch the whole architecture work at once, in our invented story. In Group Year Three, a promoter approaches Tom with a proposal for Brackenwell Sensors. Brackenwell's losses from before Tarnmoor bought it, eight point six million pounds of them, are fenced off by the rules on changes of ownership, which chapter fourteen explains. At twenty five per cent they would be worth about two point one five million pounds. The promoter offers a way, in his words, to refresh them. The structure would route income and deductions around the group in a pre-packaged series of steps. His fee would be a percentage of the tax saved. And he asks Tom to sign a confidentiality agreement before he will explain the steps.

Tom does not need to see the steps to know the answer. The fee contingent on the saving and the demand for secrecy are DOTAS hallmarks, so the scheme is very likely notifiable. That means a scheme reference number on Tarnmoor's return, a target for an accelerated payment notice, and a twenty year discovery window. Part fourteen B of the twenty ten Act, the targeted rule against using arrangements to unlock carried-forward losses, is aimed at exactly this kind of plan, and chapter fourteen teaches it. The GAAR stands behind it all. Then the governance ladder. Tarnmoor's published tax strategy says the group does not use marketed avoidance schemes. Nadia would have to consider whether the scheme fitted the group's tax accounting arrangements. And a position like this, if provided for in the accounts and worth more than five million pounds, would need an uncertain tax treatment notification.

The board declines the proposal in a single meeting. Tom's paper to the board is short. It sets out the hallmarks, the targeted rule, the GAAR, the cash cost of an accelerated payment notice, and the conflict with the published strategy. It recommends that the group decline, that the approach be recorded, and that the promoter receive a short written refusal. That is the purpose of the architecture. It is not only that the rules defeat the scheme. It is that they make the scheme unattractive before anyone has to defeat it.

The Finance Act twenty twenty six continues this pressure on the promoters themselves. Its Part six adds a prohibition on promoting certain marketed avoidance arrangements and new promoter action notices. This is non-core detail for your paper. Know that it exists.

Working with H M R C when things are disputed.

Large groups do not meet H M R C as strangers. H M R C's large business directorate gives many of the largest groups a named Customer Compliance Manager, and H M R C's uncertain tax treatment guidance refers to businesses raising uncertain issues through that contact. A good relationship means early conversations, agreed positions, and fewer surprises on enquiry.

When disputes do arise, H M R C resolves them within its Litigation and Settlement Strategy, set out in its internal guidance. As H M R C describes it, each dispute is resolved on its own merits, not as part of a package deal, and H M R C will not settle for less than the tax it believes the law requires simply to save costs. A strong case is settled for what a tribunal would be likely to award. A weak one is conceded.

Alternative dispute resolution is the awareness-level tool. It is non-statutory. An H M R C mediator, not involved in the case, helps the two sides narrow the issues or reach agreement. It can be used during a compliance check when talks have stalled, or after an appealable decision. It does not affect the taxpayer's rights of appeal or review. H M R C also has guidance on using it in large or complex cases.

Remember the formal route that sits behind all of this, which The Living Law, chapter two, set out. A company that receives an appealable decision, such as a closure notice or an assessment, can ask for a statutory review by an H M R C officer not involved in the decision, or appeal to the First-tier Tribunal. Alternative dispute resolution does not replace either. It can run alongside them, and if it fails the appeal continues. For a large group, the choice between negotiating, mediating and litigating is a commercial one as well as a technical one. Litigation is public, slow and expensive, and a precedent may affect other companies in the sector. Settlement is private but must follow H M R C's strategy, which means that a group cannot trade one issue against another. Each issue stands or falls on its own merits.

In our invented story, H M R C opens an enquiry into Calder's return for its nine month period in Group Year Three. That dispute, about the price Calder charged its Vallarian sister company, is told in chapter twenty seven.

How the examiner tests governance.

The examiners like a ten or fifteen mark administration question, and governance is a favourite subject for it. In November twenty twenty three, a ten mark question asked which companies in a group counted towards the senior accounting officer thresholds, and then about the main duty, the certificate, notification and penalties. The examiners reported that candidates knew the thresholds but misapplied them. They also reminded candidates to show their workings, because marks are available even when the conclusion is wrong. In May twenty twenty five, a fifteen mark question on the tax strategy rules turned on group companies with different year ends, and candidates apportioned figures they should have taken from the accounts.

So build every answer the same way. List each company. State whether it is incorporated in the United Kingdom, whether it is in the fifty one per cent group, and which year's figures you are using. Then aggregate and conclude. And do not confuse the regimes. The senior accounting officer rules aggregate United Kingdom incorporated companies. The uncertain tax treatment rules aggregate group members within the charge to corporation tax. The tax strategy rules add the country-by-country route.

The anti-avoidance material, DOTAS, the GAAR and the notices, has not been examined in the sittings from May twenty twenty three to May twenty twenty six. When it comes, it is more likely to be a few marks within a scenario, such as a promoter's proposal, than a whole question. The reading edition sets out the grades and the traps in its Exam lens boxes.

A word on style. These questions ask you to explain and apply, not to write a letter. A typical marking guide gives half a mark or a mark for each correct point. Half a mark for stating that the company must be incorporated in the United Kingdom. A mark for applying that to the foreign incorporated company. Half a mark for the deadline. A mark for each penalty correctly stated. So short, precise sentences, each carrying one rule applied to one fact, score better than long general paragraphs. And where the question gives you the group's companies, deal with every one of them, even if only to say why it does not count. Finally, read the requirement for its tax. If it asks about the senior accounting officer, the uncertain tax treatment rules earn nothing, however well you know them.

What to take away.

The law regulates how large groups govern tax through a ladder of accountability, with an anti-avoidance structure beside it.

First rung, the senior accounting officer, under Schedule forty six to the Finance Act two thousand and nine. A qualifying company is incorporated in the United Kingdom, whatever its tax residence, and in its preceding financial year had turnover above two hundred million pounds or a balance sheet above two billion pounds. The figures are aggregated with the other United Kingdom incorporated companies in its fifty one per cent group at the end of that preceding year. Once the group is over the line, every United Kingdom incorporated member qualifies, dormant ones included. A company joining a group is outside the regime for its first financial year in the group if it did not qualify on its own. The officer takes reasonable steps to ensure appropriate tax accounting arrangements, and certifies them. Notification and certificate are due within the accounts filing period, six months for a public company and nine for a private one. Penalties are five thousand pounds, fixed and not reduced.

Second rung, the tax strategy, under Schedule nineteen to the Finance Act twenty sixteen. It applies to groups over two hundred million pounds of turnover or two billion pounds of balance sheet, or in a seven hundred and fifty million euro multinational group. It is published by the head of the group before the end of the financial year, and within fifteen months of the last. Penalties start at seven thousand five hundred pounds and build monthly. Members with different year ends use their latest year ending in the head's year, unapportioned.

Third rung, uncertain tax treatment, under Schedule seventeen to the Finance Act twenty twenty two, an awareness topic. It has the same two hundred million and two billion pound thresholds, aggregated across corporation tax paying members of the fifty one per cent group. There are two triggers, a provision or a departure from H M R C's known position, and a five million pound advantage threshold, with an exemption where H M R C already has the information. Penalties are five thousand, twenty five thousand and fifty thousand pounds.

Fourth rung, the corporate criminal offence, in sections forty five and forty six of the Criminal Finances Act twenty seventeen. There are three stages, evasion, facilitation by an associated person, and failure to prevent. Foreign tax needs dual criminality and a United Kingdom connection. The only defence is reasonable prevention procedures, guided by H M R C's six principles. The fine is unlimited.

Beside the ladder, DOTAS makes promoters disclose hallmarked schemes within five days, with penalties rebuilt by the Finance Act twenty twenty six. Follower notices carry a thirty per cent penalty, and Haworth limits them to cases where the earlier ruling plainly defeats the claim. Accelerated payment notices demand the tax within ninety days. The GAAR uses the double reasonableness test, goes through the Advisory Panel, and carries a sixty per cent penalty.

The chapter's question was who answers for the tax inside a large group. The answer is that the law gives everyone a part. A named officer answers for the systems, the board for the published attitude, the company for its uncertain positions, and the company again, in the criminal courts, for the people who act for it.

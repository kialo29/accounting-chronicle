# Chapter twenty nine: Hybrid mismatches.

The slide deck arrived in **GY4**. In our invented story, an outside adviser had a proposal for our invented group, Tarnmoor, and its treasury company, **Tarnmoor Finance Ltd (TFL)**. It would issue **£80m of perpetual subordinated notes** to **Tarnmoor Capital Ltd (TCM)**, the group's finance company in the invented country of **Marrovia**. The coupon would be **7%: £5.6m a year**.

The pitch rested on one fact. The UK would see the notes as debt, so the coupon would be deductible here. Marrovia, under its (invented) law, would see the same notes as shares, so the coupon would arrive as an exempt dividend. One payment, two readings: a deduction in one country, nothing taxed in the other. The project even had a name: **Project Undertow**.

Tom Hesketh, the group head of tax, recognised it at once. This was exactly the kind of arrangement the OECD had set out to stop. On **5 October 2015** it published its final report, *Neutralising the Effects of Hybrid Mismatch Arrangements* (BEPS Action 2), the report to which the UK statute itself refers (TIOPA 2010 s 259KA(7D)). The UK turned the report into law for payments made on or after **1 January 2017**.

So this chapter asks one question: **when two countries describe the same payment differently, which of them gets to tax it, and what happens if neither does?**

This is the eighth chapter of Part Four, Across Borders. Hybrid mismatches are **core (grade 1)** on the 2026 LCG grid, falling to grade 2 in the 2028 grid. *The Living Law* (TKS) only signposted hybrids, so this chapter builds from first principles. The law is that for **FY2026**, under Finance Act 2026.

## One coin, two readings.

Every tax system has to decide what a payment is: interest or dividend; a person or a pass-through; here or there. Each country answers by its own law, and the answers need not match.

Picture one coin tossed between two countries. If both call it heads, both give a deduction for the same expense. If both call it tails, neither taxes the income. A **hybrid mismatch** is that gap. The rules exist to make one country call the coin fairly.

BEPS Action 2 made two design choices that the UK adopted.

1. **Outcomes, not motives.** The rules ask what happened to the payment, not why. They name two outcomes:
   - **Deduction/non-inclusion (D/NI)**: a payment is deductible for the payer but not brought in as ordinary income by the payee (or brought in but under-taxed).
   - **Double deduction (DD)**: the same amount is deductible in two territories.
2. **Linked responses.** A **primary response** tells one country to act first (usually the payer's, or for double deductions the investor's); a **secondary response** tells the other country to act if the first does not. Whichever moves, the mismatch is neutralised once, and only once.

**The UK's earlier attempt.** Anti-arbitrage rules were enacted in F(No.2)A 2005 (ss 24–31, Sch 3) and rewritten as TIOPA 2010 Part 6 ("Tax arbitrage"). They had to be **activated by an HMRC notice** (deduction notices, receipt notices). FA 2016 Sch 10 para 15 omitted Part 6 for accounting periods beginning on or after 1 January 2017.

**The replacement.** TIOPA 2010 **Part 6A** (ss 259A–259NF), inserted by **FA 2016 Sch 10**, applies to payments made (and quasi-payments whose payment period begins) on or after **1 January 2017** (Sch 10 Part 3). It is **self-executing**: the company applies it in its own return. From rules HMRC switched on, to rules that switch themselves on.

**Misconception: "the hybrid rules are anti-avoidance rules with a motive test."** Between related parties they are mechanical: if the mismatch exists and arises from the hybrid feature, the counteraction follows. Purpose matters only for unrelated parties, through the structured arrangement test, and then only in the "designed to secure" sense.

## Financial instruments.

Chapter 3 (ss 259CA–259CE) is the chapter the examiner meets most often, and it is Project Undertow's chapter.

**Conditions (s 259CA).** A payment under (or connected with) a financial instrument; the payer or payee within the charge to CT; a D/NI mismatch that arises **by reason of the terms or other features of the instrument**; and payer and payee **related** or the arrangement **structured**.

**Two cases (s 259CB).**
- **Case 1:** the relevant deduction exceeds the ordinary income arising to the payee in a **permitted taxable period** (classic case: a coupon the payee's country treats as an exempt dividend).
- **Case 2:** ordinary income arises but is **under-taxed** because of the instrument's terms. The mismatch is measured as **UTA × (FMR − R) / FMR**, where UTA is the under-taxed amount, FMR the payee's full marginal rate and R the rate actually applied.

**Responses.**
- **Primary (s 259CD):** a UK payer's deduction is reduced by the amount of the mismatch.
- **Secondary (s 259CE):** where the UK is the payee's territory and the payer's territory has not counteracted, the UK payee is charged on the relevant amount as income (CTA 2009 Part 10 Ch 8).

> **Worked example 29.1: a hybrid financial instrument (labelled hypothetical, not story)**
>
> UK Co pays a coupon of **£1,000,000** to its 100% parent in Territory X under an instrument that the UK treats as debt.
>
> | £ | Case 1: X exempts the coupon as a dividend | Case 2: X taxes it at 5% (full rate 20%) |
> |---|---|---|
> | Deduction claimed by UK Co | 1,000,000 | 1,000,000 |
> | Under-taxed amount | 1,000,000 | 1,000,000 |
> | Mismatch | 1,000,000 | 1,000,000 × (20 − 5) / 20 = 750,000 |
> | Deduction allowed (s 259CD) | nil | 250,000 |
> | Extra UK CT at 25% | 250,000 | 187,500 |
>
> Why scale? The coupon was taxed at a quarter of the full rate, so a quarter of it is treated as properly included and three quarters as not.

**Timing.** A Case 1 mismatch can arise purely from timing: the payer accrues and deducts each year, the payee is taxed only on receipt years later. If the income falls into a permitted taxable period, there is no mismatch; if later, the deduction is denied now and Chapter 12 (s 259LA) allows the position to be adjusted when the income is included. Relief is delayed, not lost.

**Chapter 4 (hybrid transfers, ss 259DA–259DG)** applies the same thinking to repos, stock lending and other transfers of financial instruments, where two countries disagree about who owns the instrument. Payer first, payee second.

**Boundary.** The mismatch must come from the instrument. If the payee is simply exempt because of its status (a pension fund, a sovereign body), the shortfall is not a hybrid mismatch.

## When the UK is the payee.

Turn the coin over: a UK company receives a payment that its foreign payer deducts but that the UK would treat as an exempt dividend.

**The first defence is in the dividend exemption.** A distribution received by a large company is exempt only if, among other conditions, **no deduction is allowed to a resident of any territory outside the UK under the law of that territory in respect of the distribution** (CTA 2009 s 931D(c); s 931B(c) for small companies). That is a **linking rule**: if the payer abroad deducts, the UK recipient loses the exemption and is taxed.

**HMRC's view on the overlap (INTM551170).** Where a payment is deductible for the payer but a distribution in the payee's territory, Part 6A and Part 9A overlap. There is no statutory order, but the distribution exemption may be considered first. Where the payee's territory has an equivalent of s 931D(c) and taxes the receipt, the UK will not usually deny the payer's deduction under s 259CD; such cases go to a specialist HMRC team.

**Undertow shows the reverse.** Marrovia (invented) has no linking rule: it exempts the coupon whatever the UK does. That is why the UK, as the payer's country, must make the primary response.

**Answer technique.** Check both ends of the payment: how the payer's country treats it, how the payee's country treats it, and which country's rules respond first.

> **Worked example 29.5: the UK as payee (labelled hypothetical, not story)**
>
> UK Co (large) holds preference-style shares in a related company in Territory Y. Territory Y treats the instrument as debt and allows its company a deduction for a **£500,000** "dividend". The UK would otherwise treat the receipt as an exempt distribution.
>
> | £ | Amount |
> |---|---|
> | Receipt | 500,000 |
> | Exempt under Part 9A? | No: a deduction is allowed to a resident of Territory Y (CTA 2009 s 931D(c)) |
> | Taxable in UK Co | 500,000 |
> | CT at 25% | 125,000 |
>
> The mismatch is closed from the payee's side by the dividend exemption's linking rule. If instead the receipt had been within Part 6A Ch 3 and Territory Y had not counteracted, the secondary response (s 259CE) would bring the relevant amount into charge. Either way, the UK answer names both territories and both rules.

## Hybrid entities, payees and establishments.

The next group of chapters deals with mismatches caused not by the instrument but by the **entity in the middle**: a payer that one country sees as a person and another sees through; a payee that nobody taxes because each country thinks the other will; a branch that one country exempts and the other does not recognise. The questions are always the same: who is the hybrid, which territory deducts, which territory fails to include, and who responds first.

| Chapter | Mismatch | Primary response | Secondary response | Reference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 Hybrid payer | Payer is a hybrid entity: its own territory deducts; the investor's territory disregards the payment | Hybrid payer's deduction allowed only against **DII**; excess carried forward | Payee taxed (UK payee) | ss 259EA–259ED |
| 6 Transparent establishments | UK PE of a foreign company deducts deemed payments to head office that the parent's territory ignores (excludes the s 21(2) notional loan capital cost and intangibles amortisation) | Deduction only against **DII**; carry forward | — | ss 259FA–259FC |
| 7 Hybrid payee | Payee is a hybrid (e.g. a **reverse hybrid**: transparent in its own territory, opaque to its investors) | Payer's deduction reduced | UK investor taxed | ss 259GA–259GE; FA 2022 s 26 (relevant transparent entities, retrospective) |
| 8 Multinational payee | Payment to a PE that no territory taxes (head office exempts, branch territory does not recognise the PE) | Payer's deduction reduced | — | ss 259HA–259HC |

**CFC charges count.** HMRC's worked example (INTM555240) shows a payment to a reverse hybrid wholly brought into a CFC charge: recognition is given under s 259BD and no hybrid payee mismatch remains. Keep this in mind for Undertow.

The pattern is the same in every chapter: **identify the hybrid element, measure the mismatch, apply the chapter's response in the right country.**

## Double deductions.

**Chapter 9 (hybrid entity double deduction, ss 259IA–259IC).** A company is a taxable person in the UK but transparent to its foreign parent, so its expenses are deducted here and again against the parent's income abroad.
- **Primary (s 259IB):** the **investor**'s deduction is allowed only against DII; excess carried forward.
- **Secondary (s 259IC):** if the investor's territory does not counteract and the two are in the same control group (or structured), the **hybrid entity**'s deduction is restricted to DII.
- Stranded deductions can be released in some cases where no further DII can arise.

> **Worked example 29.2: hybrid entity double deduction (labelled hypothetical, not story)**
>
> UK Sub is owned by a parent in a territory with no hybrid rules, which treats UK Sub as transparent. UK Sub's expenses of **£2,000,000** are deductible in both territories. UK Sub has **£1,400,000** of income taxed in both (DII). The UK applies the secondary response (s 259IC).
>
> | £ | Amount |
> |---|---|
> | Expenses deductible in both territories | 2,000,000 |
> | Dual inclusion income this period | 1,400,000 |
> | Deduction allowed this period | 1,400,000 |
> | Restricted, carried forward against future DII | 600,000 |
> | Extra UK CT this period (timing) at 25% | 150,000 |

**Chapter 10 (ss 259JA–259JD)** applies the same restriction to **dual resident companies** (s 259JB) and to companies with **PEs** whose deductions are given twice (ss 259JC–259JD).

**Chapter 12A (FA 2021)** lets a group allocate **surplus DII** from one company to another (claims under s 259ZMB), for claimant APs beginning on or after **1 January 2021** (FA 2021 Sch 7 para 40).

> **Going further: tracking dual inclusion income**
>
> DII is not a number in the accounts. The tax function computes it for each relevant company and entity every period, records restricted amounts carried forward and when they arose, and decides whether a Chapter 12A allocation claim (or consent) is worthwhile. The CT600B pages ask for the totals (below). A register that survives staff changes is the real control.

## Imported mismatches.

**Chapter 11 (ss 259KA–259KF)** denies a UK payer's deduction where its payment funds, through a series of arrangements, a mismatch elsewhere, between members of the same control group or under a structured arrangement.

*Illustration (all countries invented):* UK Co borrows from a group finance company in country A, whose hybrid rules match the OECD's. The finance company funds itself with a hybrid instrument from a group company in country B, which has no hybrid rules. Country A can counteract that mismatch itself, so the UK does nothing. If country A could not, the UK deduction would be at risk to the extent it funds the mismatch.

**FA 2021 Sch 7** narrowed Chapter 11, generally for payments and periods from **10 June 2021** (Royal Assent): it now bites only on a mismatch **not capable of counteraction** under the law of an **OECD mismatch compliant** territory (s 259KA(7)–(7D)). The same package excluded non-taxable income of entities taxed through others from "foreign tax" (s 259B(3ZA)), added the **relevant debt relief circumstances** carve-out (ss 259NEB–259NEF, retrospective), Chapter 12A, and Chapter 13A (transparent funds). It was the UK correcting its own over-reach. **Part 6A was not amended by FA 2025 or FA 2026.**

**Why the narrowing mattered.** As first enacted, Chapter 11 could reach a mismatch that another territory's own hybrid rules were already dealing with, which sits uneasily with the BEPS aim of neutralising each mismatch once and only once. The 2021 test asks a narrower question: could the mismatch be counteracted under the law of a territory whose hybrid rules match the OECD's? If so, the UK leaves it alone.

Chapter 12 (ss 259LA–259LB) adjusts counteractions where suppositions prove wrong or income arrives late; Chapter 13 (ss 259MA–259MD) is a targeted anti-avoidance rule.

> **Exam lens: hybrid mismatches**
>
> - **Grade:** hybrid mismatch **1 (core)** on the 2026 grid; **2 (non-core)** from 2028. Check the 2027 grid when published (expected around January 2027).
> - **Past appearances:** **N25 Q6 (10 marks)**: hybrid entities and instruments, D/NI and DD mismatches, primary and secondary responses, and the CT600 administration. Answers were mixed: some candidates thought a low-tax territory alone made something a hybrid and wrote generic anti-avoidance answers. **N24 Q5 (20 marks)**: a US group buying a UK SME; no hybrid trigger, yet some candidates brought hybrids in.
> - **Style:** discursive with short computations; "Explain"; about 0.5–1 mark per point; no letter format. A 10-mark question is worth about 21 minutes.
> - **Layout:** (1) hybrid element; (2) mismatch type; (3) chapter and conditions (related/structured); (4) primary and secondary responses, with the amount (Case 2 formula; DII and carry forward); (5) administration (CT600B; self-assessment).
> - **Traps:** low tax is not a hybrid; no hybrid element, no hybrid answer; naming the response in the wrong country; forgetting withholding is ignored for "tax"; forgetting the CFC charge counts as tax; raising DPT or UTPP on a loan (M23 Q3: DPT does not apply to loan relationships).

## Project Undertow.

GY4, and Tom Hesketh's memo for the board. Everything below is part of our invented story.

> **Worked example 29.3: the adviser's pitch (invented)**
>
> | £ a year | Amount |
> |---|---|
> | Coupon: £80,000,000 × 7% | 5,600,000 |
> | TFL CT saving at 25% | 1,400,000 |
> | CFC charge on TPLC assumed by the adviser: Ch 9 75% exemption, so 25% × 25% × 5,600,000 | (350,000) |
> | Net saving claimed | **1,050,000** |
>
> Funding: TPLC to subscribe £80m of new shares in TCM. Withholding tax was not considered.

**Step 1: would the UK even see debt?** Perpetual notes held by a company associated with the issuer are **equity notes**, so they are **special securities** (CTA 2010 s 1015, Condition E; ss 1016–1017), and interest on them is a **distribution** (s 1000(1) para F): not deductible at all. HMRC's manual gives perpetual securities as the example (CTM15515).

The adviser's answer: draft the notes so that TFL can **defer or cancel** the coupon, give them **no other significant equity features**, and **elect** to treat them as **hybrid capital instruments** (CTA 2009 s 475C). A hybrid capital instrument is not a special security by reason of Condition E (CTA 2010 s 1015(1A)), and deferral or cancellation rights are ignored in deciding whether payments are distributions (s 420A). But the election (irrevocable, within six months of issue) has **no effect where there are arrangements a main purpose of which is to obtain a tax advantage** for any person. Undertow had no other purpose. **First conclusion:** a serious risk that the coupon is a distribution, with no deduction from the start.

**Suppose the election stood.** Then:

**Step 2: hybrids (Ch 3).** Payment under a financial instrument; TFL within CT; the mismatch arises from the notes' perpetual, subordinated terms, which Marrovia reads as equity; TFL and TCM are related (same control group: both 100% TPLC). Case 1 D/NI of **£5,600,000**; primary response on TFL (s 259CD): deduction denied, **£1,400,000** of CT.

**Step 3: withholding.** Yearly interest paid by a company (ITA 2007 s 874). Not listed, so not a quoted Eurobond (s 882). Marrovia has no UK treaty. The **qualifying private placement** exemption (s 888A; SI 2015/2002) requires a creditor resident in a qualifying territory (a treaty with a non-discrimination article: SAIM9360) and a security entered into for genuine commercial reasons, not as part of a tax advantage scheme. So TFL deducts **20%: £1,120,000** a year (2026/27). *Coming next (enacted):* 22% savings basic rate from 2027/28 (FA 2026 ss 5–6, Sch 1 para 29): **£1,232,000**.

**Step 4: the CFC charge.** The Ch 9 finance company exemption needs a **qualifying loan relationship**; a creditor relationship whose ultimate debtor is a **UK company** (without a s 18A election) is not one (TIOPA 2010 ss 371IG–371IH). The coupon derives from **capital invested from the UK** (TPLC's £80m subscription), so it passes the Ch 5 gateway (s 371EC). Charge on TPLC: 25% × £5,600,000 = **£1,400,000**, less **creditable tax** including UK income tax suffered by deduction (s 371PA): **£280,000** payable.

**Step 5: the interaction.** HMRC's guidance (INTM550570) says income that has given rise to a CFC charge may be treated as ordinary income of a relevant chargeable company, to the extent s 259BD allows. On that reading the CFC charge could stand in for the missing Marrovian tax and the deduction might survive. The memo did not need to settle the point (read the full text of s 259BD before relying on this), because neither answer helps.

**Step 6: the other gates.**
- **Transfer pricing (Part 4):** would an independent finance company issue perpetual notes at 7% at all? HMRC's view is that Part 4 and Part 6A are applied together, with Part 6A built into both the actual and the arm's length provision, and then Part 6A checked again; companies cannot choose the order (INTM550080, INTM550085).
- **CIR (Part 10):** interest paid to a group company abroad is tax-interest expense (it is excluded only from ANGIE). In a restricted year, such as GY1 or GY2 (chapter 28), the extra £5.6m would simply be disallowed. HMRC expects the hybrid rules to be applied before the CIR (INTM550080).
- **Unallowable purpose (CTA 2009 ss 441–442):** why is TFL party to the notes? The only answer was tax. An amount disallowed under s 441 cannot come back in under another rule (CFM38165). (*BlackRock HoldCo 5, LLC v HMRC* [2024] EWCA Civ 330: TP satisfied, s 441 still disallowed all the debits; chapter 12.)

> **Worked example 29.4: Tom's outcome table (invented; £ a year, compared with doing nothing)**
>
> | £ | A: adviser's pitch | B: coupon a distribution | C: deduction survives (CFC charge counts as inclusion) | D: deduction denied (hybrids, CIR or s 441) and CFC charge bites |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | TFL deduction at 25% | 1,400,000 | nil | 1,400,000 | nil |
> | Income tax withheld at 20% | not considered | — | (1,120,000) | (1,120,000) |
> | CFC charge before credit | (350,000) | — | (1,400,000) | (1,400,000) |
> | Creditable tax (income tax withheld) | — | — | 1,120,000 | 1,120,000 |
> | **Net effect on group tax** | **+1,050,000** | **no saving** | **nil** | **(1,400,000)** |
>
> At the 2027/28 withholding rate of 22% the withholding is £1,232,000 and the net CFC charge £168,000; the totals in C and D are unchanged. Withholding is a cash cost at the time of payment; the credit arrives with TPLC's CFC charge.

The best case was break-even; the worst a loss of **£1.4m a year**, plus breaking the group's published tax strategy (it does not use tax avoidance schemes: chapter 4). The board rejected Undertow in one meeting. The minute gave three reasons: it could not beat doing nothing; it would create an annual dispute on several regimes at once; it conflicted with the tax strategy. TCM went on lending to TVS; TFL's funding stayed plain.

> **Exam lens: cross-border intra-group financing**
>
> - **Grade:** hybrids 1; CFCs 1; TP 1; CIR 1; loan relationships (including s 441) 1; deduction of income tax 1 (2026 grid).
> - **What a marker rewards:** one point per regime spotted and explained, with the amount: D/NI denial (£1.4m), withholding (£1.12m and the absence of exemptions), the CFC charge and creditable tax (£1.4m less £1.12m), TP, CIR, s 441, and the debt/equity question. A recommendation if asked.
> - **Traps:** treating the CFC Ch 9 75% exemption as automatic (a UK debtor is not a qualifying loan relationship); forgetting that UK income tax withheld is creditable tax for the CFC charge; netting the CFC charge against the deduction denial without explaining the s 259BD point; DPT/UTPP on a loan.

**How Tom built the memo.** He did not stop at the first problem. A scheme that fails on one ground invites a fix; one that fails on six does not. An examiner rewards a cross-border financing answer the same way: each regime is a separate set of marks.

## A low rate is not a hybrid.

The examiners reported, after **November 2025** (Q6), that some candidates thought a low-tax territory alone made something a hybrid. A payment to a company taxed at 9% is **not** a hybrid mismatch if it is fully included in that company's income, however low the rate.

| Regime | What it tests | Chapter |
|---|---|---|
| Transfer pricing | The price | 27 |
| CFC rules | Whether profits are artificially diverted from the UK | 26 |
| Global minimum tax | Low effective rates | 30 |
| **Hybrid rules** | **A difference in characterisation, and only that** | 29 |

**The first question in any hybrid answer: where is the hybrid?** An instrument two countries read differently (debt/equity); an entity one sees as a person and the other sees through; a PE one country recognises and the other ignores; a company resident in two places. If you cannot point to one, the rules do not apply.

**Interactions (HMRC's view).** Hybrids before CIR; TP and hybrids applied together, without picking the better result (INTM550080, INTM550085, examples at INTM550086A–E); s 441 alongside, and an amount it disallows cannot be relieved under another rule.

## Administration and the tax function.

- **Self-assessment.** The counteraction is made in the CT computation: denied deductions added back; secondary-response amounts brought in as income. No HMRC notice is needed (contrast the old Part 6).
- **The return.** Supplementary pages **CT600B** (*Controlled foreign companies and foreign permanent establishment exemptions, hybrid and other mismatches*), boxes **B40–B85**: whether a Part 6A counteraction has been made (B65), total counteraction (B70), total s 259LA deduction (B75), DII surplus allocation claims made (B80) and consented to (B85). Completed only where there is something to report.
- **Clarification.** HMRC offers non-statutory requests for clarification on the rules (INTM561720).
- **Acquisitions.** A target's instruments or entities may become hybrid once relationships change. Map them before completion.
- **Loose end.** s 259B still lists DPT in the text checked; DPT is replaced by UTPP for APs beginning on or after 1 January 2026 (chapter 30).

> **Going further: living with the rules**
>
> - **Keep instruments plain.** Fixed-term, ordinary-coupon intra-group debt rarely creates a mismatch. Perpetual, subordinated or convertible features invite a different reading abroad: get the payee territory's treatment in writing before issue.
> - **Watch entity elections.** A classification election made abroad for local reasons can turn a plain UK company into a hybrid entity overnight (Chs 5 and 9 follow). Keep a register of every entity whose classification differs between territories.
> - **Remember who is related.** A 25% investment is enough: joint ventures and consortium companies (Helmside, in our invented story, while TPLC held 45%) can be related to their investors. Relationships change on the day a company joins or leaves.
> - **Document.** Where the answer depends on foreign law or on whether the mismatch arises from an instrument's terms, record who advised, what they said and when. A large uncertain position may need an uncertain tax treatment notification (chapter 4).
> - **Ethics.** Tom's memo put the tax strategy point in one line at the top.

## How the examiner tests it.

Hybrids have appeared once as a whole question (**N25 Q6**, 10 marks) and once as a warning (**N24 Q5**, where some candidates raised them without a trigger). Expect them alongside **TP, CIR and CFCs**. For any cross-border intra-group financing, run Tom's checklist:

1. Is it debt under UK law (distribution rules; hybrid capital instruments)?
2. Is there a hybrid element (Part 6A chapter, mismatch, response)?
3. Transfer pricing: amount and rate?
4. CIR?
5. Withholding (and exemptions)?
6. CFC charge on the recipient?
7. Unallowable purpose?

Then for the hybrid part: name the element, the mismatch, the chapter, the responding country and its response, compute (Case 2 scaling; DII and carry forward), finish with the CT600B administration. Keep it tight.

## What to take away.

- Hybrid mismatches arise when two countries characterise the same payment, entity or establishment differently. BEPS Action 2 (**5 October 2015**) targeted **outcomes, not motives**.
- UK rules: **TIOPA 2010 Part 6A**, payments from **1 January 2017** (FA 2016 Sch 10), replacing notice-based arbitrage rules; self-executing.
- **D/NI** (deduction, no ordinary income) and **DD** (one amount deducted twice); **primary and secondary responses** neutralise the mismatch once.
- Most chapters need **related** parties (control group or **25%** investment) or a **structured arrangement**; between related parties there is **no purpose test**. Withholding is ignored; a CFC charge counts as tax.
- **Ch 3** financial instruments: payer first (s 259CD), payee second (s 259CE). Case 2: £1m taxed at 5% against a 20% full rate gives a mismatch of **£750,000** (deduction allowed £250,000).
- **Chs 5, 7, 8**: hybrid payer, hybrid payee, multinational payee; **Ch 6** PEs; **Chs 9–10** double deductions against **DII**, excess carried forward (£2.0m expenses, £1.4m DII: **£600,000** carried forward); **Ch 12A** group allocation of surplus DII.
- **Ch 11** imported mismatches, narrowed by **FA 2021** to mismatches not capable of counteraction in an OECD-compliant territory. No FA 2025 or FA 2026 changes.
- The UK dividend exemption has its own **linking rule** (CTA 2009 s 931D(c)).
- **Project Undertow** (invented, GY4, rejected): equity note risk; HCI election ineffective with a tax main purpose; if interest, D/NI denies **£1.4m**; withholding **£1.12m** (20%; 22% from 2027/28); full CFC charge (UK debtor: no QLR); TP, CIR and s 441 behind. Best case break-even; worst **£1.4m a year** worse off.
- **A low rate is not a hybrid.** No hybrid element, no hybrid answer.

The question was which country taxes a payment two countries describe differently. The answer: the one the statute tells to respond first, and if it does not, the other, so that the mismatch is closed once. Where the problem is not a difference in description but simply a low rate, a different tool is needed. Chapter 30 turns to the global minimum and the end of diverted profits tax.

Chapter eighteen: Shares: the exemption, reorganisations and earn-outs.

During the sale of one of its businesses, a group managing director at Euromoney Institutional Investor plc, the company later renamed Delinian, set out the idea in an email. The deal was to have been paid in shares and cash. It became shares and redeemable preference shares in the buyer. The email explained why. The preference shares are the mechanism to avoid paying tax on the capital gain for the cash element of the transaction.

H M R C could hardly have asked for a clearer document. It denied the share exchange relief and amended Euromoney's return, claiming, as reports of the case record, a little over ten point four eight million pounds of corporation tax. The case went through three courts. And Euromoney won every time. The law then asked whether avoiding tax was a main purpose of the arrangements as a whole. The tribunal found that it was a purpose, but not a main one, because the preference shares were a small part of a large commercial deal. The Court of Appeal agreed in November twenty twenty three. Parliament has since rewritten the rule, and you will meet the new version later in this chapter.

The story asks the question this chapter answers. When a company parts with shares, the law can give three quite different answers. It can tax the gain. It can exempt the gain, and refuse the loss. Or it can say that nothing has happened at all, and carry the old cost into the new shares. Which answer applies, and in what order you test them, is one of the most examined skills on the paper.

This chapter belongs to Part Three, The Group. Share pooling and identification, gilts and qualifying corporate bonds, reorganisations, conversions, stock dividends, the substantial shareholding exemption and the earn-out case of Marren versus Ingles are all core topics. Two small sections on exchange gains brought into the gains code are non-core. The chapter builds on The Living Law, chapter twenty three, which taught company gains, the matching rules and the outline of the shareholding exemption, and on chapter sixteen of that book, which met the same ideas from the individual's side.

Most of the law is in the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act nineteen ninety two, which I will call the Gains Act. The shareholding exemption lives in Schedule seven A C to the Gains Act. A few rules sit in the Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, the two thousand and nine Act, and the Corporation Tax Act twenty ten, the twenty ten Act. Finance Act twenty twenty six rewrote the share exchange anti-avoidance rule, and made no change to the shareholding exemption. We apply the law for the financial year twenty twenty six throughout.

Three answers, and the order you ask the questions.

Start with a map, because the examiner rewards candidates who test things in the right order. A company that disposes of shares should run five questions.

First, which shares has it disposed of? A company may have bought shares in the same company at different times and prices. The matching rules decide which acquisition the disposal comes out of, and so which cost to use.

Second, is the asset really a share at all, or is it a debt dressed as one? For a company, almost every debt security is a loan relationship. Its profits and losses are income, under the rules in chapter twelve, and the gains code steps aside.

Third, does the substantial shareholding exemption apply? If it does, the gain is not a chargeable gain and a loss is not an allowable loss. The exemption is tested before anything else in the gains code, including the rules that say there is no disposal.

Fourth, if the exemption does not apply, is this a reorganisation, a conversion or a share exchange? If so, the law may say there is no disposal, and the old cost travels into the new holding.

Fifth, only if none of that applies, compute the gain or loss in the ordinary way, with indexation frozen at December twenty seventeen.

Notice the order of questions three and four, because it decides most share questions. Students who learnt reorganisations first, as most do, tend to reach for no disposal as soon as they see shares swapped for shares. For a company with a qualifying holding, that is the wrong answer. The exemption wins.

Throughout the chapter we follow our invented group, Tarnmoor. Tarnmoor plc, its companies, its people and every number about them are invented for teaching. Three of its stories run through these pages. The first is Tarnmoor plc's twelve per cent stake in an invented instrument maker quoted on the Alternative Investment Market, Coldwater Instruments plc, part of which it sells in Group Year Four. The second is the share exchange on the first of March in Group Year Five, when Tarnmoor plc issues its own shares to buy its joint venture partner's stake in Helmside Energy. The third is the cash earn-out on the sale of Tarnmoor Actuators in Group Year Six, and what happens when the extra money arrives in the two years after.

Which shares did you sell.

Companies and individuals do not match share disposals in the same way, and the difference is a favourite trap.

The rules are in sections one hundred and five to one hundred and ten of the Gains Act. First, shares of the same class bought and sold on the same day are matched with each other. Second, a company matches a disposal with shares it acquired in the ten days before, earliest first. This is the ten-day rule, and it applies only to companies. Third, what remains comes out of the section one oh four pool, the single pooled holding of everything else. An individual instead matches with shares bought in the thirty days after the disposal, the bed and breakfasting rule. A company has no thirty-day rule. If you apply it in a company question, you lose the marks.

The ten-day rule stops a company choosing which cost a quick sale comes out of. Shares bought just before a sale are matched with it, and they never enter the pool.

A company's section one oh four pool carries two figures, the cost and an indexed pool. Each time shares were added or removed, the indexed pool was first increased by the movement in the retail prices index since the last event. Since the first of January twenty eighteen, that indexation has stopped. The statute now fixes the end point at the index for December twenty seventeen. So a pool that was last updated before that date gets one final top-up to December twenty seventeen and is then frozen. Shares bought afterwards go in at cost with no indexation at all.

Two rules then govern the indexation that remains. It can reduce a gain to nil. It can never create a loss, or make a loss bigger. The examiners reported after the May twenty twenty five sitting that the weak spot in an otherwise good share question was candidates using indexation to enhance a loss. It cannot.

Here is the shape of an example, invented and not part of our story. Suppose a company bought a hundred thousand shares in a listed company in June two thousand and four for one hundred and fifty thousand pounds, and fifty thousand more after the freeze for two hundred thousand pounds. The June two thousand and four shares earn indexation of nought point four eight nine, about seventy three thousand pounds, and then freeze. Later it buys twenty thousand more, and five days after that sells sixty thousand. The twenty thousand bought five days earlier are matched first, under the ten-day rule. The other forty thousand come out of the pool, carrying a proportion of both the cost and the frozen indexation. If the price has fallen, the pool part may show a loss, and then the indexation simply drops away. The full working, with a profitable and a loss-making version side by side, is in the reading edition.

For Tarnmoor, matching is simple. Tarnmoor plc bought its Coldwater shares in two tranches before the story begins, both after the indexation freeze. The first was eight per cent of Coldwater for four point eight million pounds. The second, four per cent more, cost three point six million pounds. That makes a pool of twelve per cent with a cost of eight point four million pounds, and no indexation in it at all.

Bonds, gilts and the loan relationship wall.

Before the shareholding exemption, clear away something that looks like a share question and is not one.

Section one hundred and fifteen of the Gains Act says that gains on gilt-edged securities and qualifying corporate bonds are not chargeable gains. For an individual, a qualifying corporate bond is a defined kind of sterling debt. For a company, section one hundred and seventeen goes much further. For corporation tax, any asset representing a loan relationship of a company is a qualifying corporate bond. So when a company holds gilts, bonds, loan notes or any other debt claim, the gains code switches off. Every profit and loss on the debt is taxed as a loan relationship credit or debit, as chapter twelve explained, usually following the accounts.

That has a practical consequence that trips candidates. Suppose a company sells shares and takes loan notes from the buyer. For an individual, a share exchange for loan notes can sometimes be paper for paper. For a company, the loan notes are qualifying corporate bonds by definition. Section one hundred and sixteen then takes over from the ordinary reorganisation rules. The gain on the shares is worked out at the date of the exchange and held over, to come into charge when the notes are later disposed of or redeemed. The notes themselves are then taxed as loan relationships. And, as we shall see, if the shareholding exemption applies to the shares, it takes priority even over this frozen-gain rule. A frozen gain that would have been exempt is simply exempt.

The same logic covers a conversion of debt into shares. If a company converts a loan note into shares, the note is a loan relationship until that moment, and its profit or loss goes through the loan relationship rules. The new shares are treated as acquired for the market value of the note. Section one hundred and eight adds identification rules for these relevant securities, but for a company they rarely matter, because the debt is taxed as income anyway.

Two non-core sections complete this corner. Sections one hundred and fifty one E and one hundred and fifty one G of the Gains Act are Treasury powers, one to bring certain loan relationship exchange gains and losses into the gains code, the other to adjust the Gains Act when the conditions for non-qualifying shares change. Recognise them. You will not compute anything under them.

Why the exemption exists.

The Living Law, chapter twenty three, told the origin story. Briefly, the substantial shareholding exemption arrived in the Finance Act two thousand and two, for disposals from the first of April that year. The case made for it was competitiveness. Other European holding company locations already exempted gains on selling trading subsidiaries. Without an equivalent, the United Kingdom was a poor place to put a group's holding company, and groups sold assets or used complex structures to avoid taxing gains that, in economic terms, had already been taxed as profits inside the subsidiary.

The Finance Number Two Act twenty seventeen simplified the exemption for disposals from the first of April twenty seventeen. The selling company no longer has to be a trading company itself. The holding period window grew to six years. The company sold need only be trading immediately after the disposal in limited cases. And a separate exemption was added for certain institutional investors, which is outside the focus of this paper.

Think of the exemption as a wall around a qualifying holding. Gains cannot get out into the tax computation. Neither can losses. People remember the first half of that sentence and forget the second.

That leads to the misconception this chapter must correct. Many people think the shareholding exemption is a relief you claim. It is not. There is no claim, no election and no time limit. If the conditions are met, the gain is not a chargeable gain and the loss is not an allowable loss, whether the company wants that or not. A group sitting on a subsidiary that has lost value cannot choose to keep the loss. If the conditions are met at the date of disposal, the loss disappears.

There is a logic to the symmetry. A gain on shares in a trading company largely reflects profits the company has made, or will make, and those profits bear corporation tax inside the company. Taxing the gain again would tax the same profits twice. If gains are kept out on that reasoning, losses must be kept out too, or groups would sell their failures and keep their successes.

So the right way to think about the exemption is as a fact to be established, not a benefit to be taken. The tax function's job is to know, before every share disposal, whether the wall stands, and to plan around it either way.

The substantial shareholding.

The first condition is about the seller's holding. The investing company must have held a substantial shareholding in the company invested in throughout a twelve-month period beginning not more than six years before the disposal.

Substantial means at least ten per cent, measured three ways at once. The investing company must hold at least ten per cent of the ordinary share capital. It must also be beneficially entitled to at least ten per cent of the profits available for distribution to equity holders, and to at least ten per cent of the assets available to equity holders on a winding up. The three tests stop a company from holding ten per cent of the votes on a share class with almost no economic interest.

Holdings are aggregated across the group, and here the group is a fifty one per cent group, not the seventy five per cent group used for gains transfers. So if Tarnmoor plc held six per cent of a company and Tarnmoor Engineering held five, each would be treated as holding eleven per cent. The May twenty twenty five paper tested exactly this, with a holding spread across group members.

The holding period can be extended. If shares came from another group company on a no gain, no loss transfer, the time the transferor held them counts. Earlier holdings that became the present shares through a reorganisation or a demerger count too. And a special rule for hive-downs, paragraph fifteen A, which has its own section shortly, can count the time that a trade's assets were used in the group before the subsidiary owning them even existed.

Now the Coldwater story. Tarnmoor plc has held twelve per cent of Coldwater Instruments since before the story begins. It has an equal share of the profits and the assets, because Coldwater has one class of ordinary shares. In Group Year Four, on the thirtieth of September, Tarnmoor plc sells a third of its stake, one point six million shares, which is four per cent of Coldwater, at three pounds and ten pence a share. The proceeds are four point nine six million pounds. The pool gives a cost of two point eight million pounds, one third of eight point four million. The gain is two point one six million pounds.

That gain is exempt. Tarnmoor plc held at least ten per cent for far more than twelve months in the six years before the sale. Without the exemption, the tax would have been five hundred and forty thousand pounds.

The interesting part is what comes next. Tarnmoor plc now holds eight per cent, below the threshold. Is a later sale of that eight per cent still exempt? The answer is yes, for a while. The test does not ask whether the holding is substantial at the date of sale. It asks whether a substantial holding was held throughout some twelve-month period beginning not more than six years before the sale. The last such period ended with the partial sale in the autumn of Group Year Four, so it began in the autumn of Group Year Three. Tarnmoor plc can therefore sell the remaining eight per cent within the exemption until about the end of September in Group Year Nine, six years after that period began, provided Coldwater keeps trading.

After that date, a sale of the eight per cent is outside the exemption. Any gain is taxable, and any loss is allowable. That is why Tom Hesketh, the group's head of tax, keeps the Coldwater pool up to date even though every sale so far has been exempt. The remaining pool is three point two million shares with a cost of five point six million pounds. The exemption has a sell-by date, and the base cost is what the group will need on the day it expires.

The company you sell must trade.

The second condition is about the company sold. The company invested in must be a qualifying company, which in practice means a trading company or the holding company of a trading group or trading subgroup. It must be one throughout the period from the start of the latest twelve-month period that meets the holding test, until the disposal.

A trading company is one whose activities do not include, to a substantial extent, anything other than trading. Substantial is not defined. H M R C's view is that it means more than twenty per cent, measured by whatever indicators fit the facts, such as income, assets, expenses and the time spent by management and staff. In a group, the activities of all the members are looked at together, and intra-group activities are ignored, so a group finance company lending to its fellow subsidiaries does not taint the trading group.

There is a look-through for joint ventures. If a company in the group holds at least ten per cent of a joint venture company, the group is treated as carrying on a share of the joint venture's activities, rather than as holding an investment. Without that rule, a trading group with large joint venture stakes could look like an investment group.

Since twenty seventeen, the company sold need not still be trading immediately after the disposal in most cases. That requirement applies only if the buyer is connected with the seller, or if the holding period depends on the hive-down rule. The change made it easier to sell a company whose trade is being wound down by its new owner.

For Coldwater, Tom's problem is evidence. Tarnmoor plc owns twelve per cent of a quoted company, not a subsidiary. It cannot see the management accounts. So the tax file holds Coldwater's published annual reports, with a short note testing its cash, investment property and any non-trading income against H M R C's twenty per cent yardstick. Coldwater makes and sells instruments, and its cash is working capital. Coldwater is a trading company. The note is filed with the computation, because an enquiry into an exempt gain will start with exactly that question.

For a candidate, the lesson is the order of the trading tests. The seller no longer needs to trade. The company sold must trade throughout the qualifying period, and immediately after the sale only in the two special cases.

The wall stands in front of every door.

Now the rule that gives the exemption its priority. Paragraph four of Schedule seven A C says that, in deciding whether there is a disposal for the exemption, you ignore three no-disposal rules. They are section one two seven, the rule that a reorganisation is not a disposal, section one one six, the frozen-gain rule for qualifying corporate bonds, and the demerger rule in section one nine two. If the exemption then applies, those rules are disapplied.

The image is a wall standing in front of every other door. Section one two seven is a door to deferral. Without paragraph four, a company could walk through that door and keep its gain or its loss alive in the new holding. Paragraph four puts the wall in front of it. If the exemption applies, the gain is exempt now, and a loss is gone now, and the new shares are acquired at their value. There is nothing to defer.

The exemption has two subsidiary exemptions. Paragraph two extends it to assets related to shares, such as options over the shares or securities convertible into them, held by a company that also meets the main exemption. Paragraph three is a run-off rule. Suppose the main exemption would have applied had the seller sold at some time in the previous two years, and the company sold has since stopped trading, but the seller or its group had controlled it. A disposal now can still be exempt. Paragraph three A is the institutional investor exemption, outside our focus.

There is a targeted anti-avoidance rule in paragraph five. The exemption is denied if, under arrangements whose sole or main benefit is that a gain will be exempt, an untaxed gain accrues after the investing company acquired control of the company sold, or after a major change in that company's trading activities. The classic target is buying control of a company, moving a valuable asset into it without a tax charge, and selling the shares under the exemption.

And paragraph six keeps the exemption out of disposals that are already no gain, no loss, such as a sale of shares to another group company. Those stay inside the group rules of chapter seventeen.

Now the trap that the November twenty twenty three paper set. A retailer sold shares in an Irish subsidiary at a loss. The exemption applied, so the loss was simply not allowable. The examiners noted that many candidates computed the loss anyway. That costs time and earns nothing. State the rule and move on.

Can a group escape the wall when it wants the loss? It can fail a condition. It can sell before holding ten per cent for twelve months, for instance, or after the company sold has stopped trading. But the Gains Act contains a general anti-avoidance rule for capital losses, in section sixteen A, introduced in two thousand and seven. A loss is not allowable if it accrues in connection with arrangements, one of whose main purposes is to secure a tax advantage. Arranging to fail the exemption just to keep a loss is exactly the kind of purpose that rule looks at. A loss that arises because a group genuinely sold early for commercial reasons is a different matter. The evidence of purpose is what decides.

Hive-downs and the M Group lesson.

Groups often sell a business by first moving it into a new subsidiary, then selling the subsidiary. That is a hive-down. The buyer gets a clean company with only the trade it wants. The seller hopes for an exempt share sale instead of taxable asset sales. The problem is the twelve-month holding test. A new subsidiary formed last month has not been held for twelve months.

Paragraph fifteen A solves this. If the shares sold were acquired from, or the new subsidiary acquired its trading assets from, another member of the seller's group, the seller is treated as having held the shares throughout any period during which the asset was used in a trade by a member of that group. In plain terms, the clock runs from when the group started using the trade's assets, not from when the new company was formed. Where the holding period relies on this paragraph, the company sold must also be trading immediately after the sale.

The leading case shows where the edges are. In M Group Holdings Limited versus H M R C, decided by the Upper Tribunal on the thirty first of August, twenty twenty three, a company had carried on its business alone, with no subsidiaries. In June twenty fifteen it formed a subsidiary. In September it hived its trade down into it. In May twenty sixteen it sold the subsidiary for about fifty four million pounds, less than twelve months after the subsidiary was formed. The company argued that paragraph fifteen A let it count the years in which it had used the trade's assets itself.

The Upper Tribunal disagreed. The paragraph counts use by a member of the seller's group. Before the subsidiary existed, the seller was a single company, and a single company is not a group. So the earlier years did not count, the holding test failed, and a gain of about fifty three million pounds was taxable. The case was decided on the rules as they stood before the twenty seventeen reforms, but the point it turned on remains in the paragraph. Commentators have argued for a change in the law. Finance Act twenty twenty six made none.

For Tarnmoor, there is no such gap. When Tarnmoor Engineering hives its actuators business down into the new Tarnmoor Actuators on the first of February in Group Year Six, Tarnmoor Engineering is already a member of the Tarnmoor group, and it has used the actuator assets in its trade for years. When it sells Tarnmoor Actuators eleven months later, paragraph fifteen A counts that use, so the holding test is met. Chapter twenty, on buying and selling companies, works through that sale in full, including the degrouping gain that rides on the exempt proceeds, which chapter seventeen explained.

The planning lesson from M Group is simple. If the seller is a single company, form the subsidiary early, or wait.

Reorganisations, when nothing happens.

If the exemption does not apply, the next question is whether anything has happened at all.

Section one two six of the Gains Act defines a reorganisation as a reorganisation or reduction of a company's share capital. It includes an allotment of shares to existing holders in proportion to their holdings, whether they pay for them or not. That covers bonus issues and rights issues. It also includes a change in the rights attached to shares where there is more than one class. Section one two seven then gives the key rule. The original shares and the new holding are treated as a single asset, acquired when the original shares were acquired. There is no disposal. The cost of the old shares becomes the cost of the new holding.

Section one two eight deals with money moving either way. If the shareholder pays for new shares, as in a rights issue, the payment is added to the cost. If the shareholder receives cash as part of a reorganisation, that is a part disposal of the holding, computed in the usual way. Where the new holding includes more than one class, sections one two nine and one three oh apportion the cost between the classes by their market values. If any class is quoted within three months, the values on the first day of dealing are used.

Section one three two extends the same treatment to conversions of securities, for example convertible preference shares turned into ordinary shares, or an exchange forced by a compulsory acquisition. Section one three three deals with a small cash premium paid on a conversion. The premium is deducted from cost rather than treated as a disposal.

Now the one that catches people, stock dividends. A stock dividend is a share issued instead of a cash dividend, usually because the shareholder chose shares. For an individual, the law treats the share as income equal to the cash forgone, and section one four two gives the share a base cost equal to that amount. But those income rules apply to individuals, trustees and personal representatives. A company receiving a stock dividend is outside them. H M R C's view, in its company taxation manual, is that a stock dividend received by a company is generally not income through any route, and that the company acquires no additional base cost. In other words, for a corporate shareholder, a stock dividend behaves like a bonus issue. The new shares join the old under section one two seven, at no extra cost.

Here is a labelled example, not part of our story. Suppose Coldwater had offered a scrip alternative to a cash dividend, and Tarnmoor plc had taken one new share for every fifty it held. Its holding would have grown by two per cent in number, with no change in its twelve per cent proportion and no change in its pool cost. For the exemption, nothing would change, because the holding was always the same proportion of Coldwater.

The examiner's favourite reorganisation questions combine these rules with the matching rules and the exemption. A share-for-share exchange followed by a later sale, for instance, needs you to know the base cost of the new shares. If the exemption applied to the exchange, the new shares cost their market value at the exchange. If it did not, and section one two seven applied, they cost what the old shares cost. The examiners reported in twenty twenty five that this base cost after the exchange was the weak spot in an otherwise well answered question.

Paper for paper, and the Helmside exchange.

Takeovers paid for in the buyer's shares would grind to a halt if every target shareholder were taxed on a gain they had not turned into cash. Section one three five of the Gains Act prevents that. It applies when one company, call it the acquirer, issues shares or debentures to the holders of shares or debentures in another company, the target, in exchange for them, in one of three cases. In case one, the acquirer holds, or will hold as a result, more than twenty five per cent of the target's ordinary share capital. In case two, the acquirer makes a general offer, initially conditional on giving it control. In case three, the acquirer holds, or will hold, the greater part of the voting power. Where it applies, sections one two seven to one three one apply as if the two companies were one. The shareholder's old shares become the new shares, with the old cost and acquisition date. Section one three six does the same for a scheme of reconstruction, which chapter nineteen defines.

Now Tarnmoor. Helmside Energy is the hydrogen joint venture formed on the first of January in Group Year One. Tarnmoor plc held forty five per cent, Greyfell Utilities, an invented listed utility, forty per cent, and an invented infrastructure fund, Northlight, fifteen per cent. Heads of terms with Greyfell were signed on the first of December in Group Year Four. On the first of March in Group Year Five, Tarnmoor plc issues two million new Tarnmoor shares, at their market price of eight pounds each, sixteen million pounds in all, to Greyfell in exchange for Greyfell's forty per cent. Tarnmoor plc now holds eighty five per cent, and Helmside joins the seventy five per cent group, with the consequences for group relief that chapter fifteen traced.

Look first at Greyfell. On its face, this is a textbook case one share exchange, because Tarnmoor plc will hold more than twenty five per cent of Helmside. So section one three five looks as if it should apply, giving Greyfell no disposal. But apply the order of questions. Greyfell is a company. It has held forty per cent of Helmside, far more than ten per cent of the shares, profits and assets, since Helmside was formed. Helmside trades. So the substantial shareholding exemption applies, and paragraph four puts the wall in front of section one three five. Greyfell has a disposal. Its gain, sixteen million pounds less its cost of eight million pounds, eight million pounds, is exempt. And its new Tarnmoor shares are acquired at their value, sixteen million pounds, not at the old cost.

That is better for Greyfell than deferral. With deferral, the eight million pound gain would have waited inside its new Tarnmoor shares, a holding far too small to be substantial, and been taxed when it sold them. The exemption cleans the gain away at the moment of exchange.

Now look at Tarnmoor plc. It disposes of nothing. Issuing its own new shares is not a disposal. But it acquires forty per cent of Helmside, and it needs a base cost. What did it give? It gave shares, not money. The House of Lords considered this in Stanton versus Drayton Commercial Investment Company, decided in nineteen eighty two. In an arm's length bargain, the consideration a company gives by issuing its own shares is the value the parties agreed for those shares. H M R C's capital gains manual applies that decision to share acquisitions. So Tarnmoor plc's cost for Greyfell's forty per cent is sixteen million pounds. Added to its original nine million pounds for its own forty five per cent, the Helmside pool now holds eighty five per cent at a cost of twenty five million pounds.

Two more taxes touch the deal. Greyfell's transfer of its Helmside shares is a sale of shares, so stamp duty is due at half of one per cent of the sixteen million pound consideration, eighty thousand pounds, payable by Tarnmoor plc. The relief for share-for-share acquisitions needs the acquirer to take the whole of the target's share capital, and Northlight keeps its fifteen per cent, so no relief is available. Chapter twenty one, on stamp taxes for groups, takes that further. And corporation tax on Greyfell's side is the exemption we have just seen.

Purpose, and the new section one three seven.

Section one three five has always had a gatekeeper. Section one three seven, as it stood until November twenty twenty five, denied paper-for-paper treatment unless the exchange was effected for bona fide commercial reasons and did not form part of a scheme or arrangements of which the main purpose, or one of the main purposes, was avoiding capital gains tax or corporation tax. Holders of five per cent or less of the target were outside it altogether.

Return now to Euromoney. On the facts the courts recorded, Euromoney sold a business called Capital Data to a buyer, Diamond Topco, for consideration that was originally to be ordinary shares and cash. At Euromoney's suggestion, the cash was replaced by redeemable preference shares in the buyer. The exchange of shares for shares would fall within section one three five, with no disposal. Some months later, once they had been held for a year, the preference shares were redeemed for cash, and that redemption was expected to fall within the shareholding exemption. The result, if it worked, was that tax on the gain attributable to the cash element would never be paid at all.

H M R C argued that the preference share arrangement was itself a scheme with a main purpose of avoiding tax, and that the whole exchange was therefore tainted. The First-tier Tribunal found that avoiding tax was a purpose, but in the context of the whole commercial deal, not a main purpose. The Upper Tribunal agreed. So did the Court of Appeal, in Delinian Limited versus H M R C, decided on the third of November, twenty twenty three. The court held that the question is whether the exchange forms part of a scheme or arrangements with a main avoidance purpose. You look at the arrangements the exchange forms part of, as a whole, not at a selected piece of them.

Finance Act twenty twenty six, section thirty seven, rewrote section one three seven for exchanges and reconstructions involving an issue of shares or debentures on or after the twenty sixth of November, twenty twenty five. Three changes matter. First, the rule now targets arrangements relating to the exchange, and asks whether the main purpose or one of the main purposes of those arrangements is to avoid or reduce capital gains tax or corporation tax. Second, instead of switching the relief off altogether, the counteraction is by just and reasonable adjustments. These can disapply section one three five or one three six only in so far as is needed to cancel the advantage. Third, the old five per cent exception is gone. Minority shareholders in a listed takeover are now inside the rule like everyone else.

Commentators, including the Chartered Institute's own magazine, have read the new wording as Parliament's answer to Delinian, though the government's explanatory note did not name the case. Under the new rule, H M R C would no longer need to taint the whole exchange. It could aim at the preference share arrangement and adjust only the tax on the cash element. There is no case law on the new wording yet.

One more point of H M R C's view is worth carrying into the exam. Its manual says that arrangements which lead to tax never being paid, rather than merely paid later, are tax avoidance for these rules. Deferral is what section one three five is for. Permanent escape is what section one three seven is for.

For Tarnmoor, the new rule is relevant to any shareholder who relies on section one three five, and Greyfell does not. Its gain is exempt under the shareholding exemption, which comes first. To see where the new rule would bite, suppose, as an invented variation that did not happen, that Northlight had also swapped its fifteen per cent for Tarnmoor shares. Northlight is a partnership, so each partner would be treated as disposing of its share of the holding. Partners that are not companies cannot use the shareholding exemption, so they would need section one three five. Under the old law, a partner with five per cent or less of Helmside would have been outside section one three seven entirely. Now every partner would be inside it, and each would need to be confident that no main purpose of the arrangements was avoidance.

Clearance before the issue.

Section one three eight offers certainty. If, before the shares are issued, H M R C notify that they are satisfied that the exchange will be effected without arrangements to which section one three seven applies, section one three seven does not apply. Either company can apply. H M R C have thirty days to reply or to ask for further particulars, and thirty days to reply after receiving them. If they refuse, or fail to reply, the applicant can require the matter to go to the tribunal within thirty days. A clearance is void if the application did not fully and accurately disclose all the facts and considerations material to the decision.

Clearance also carried the transitional protection for the new rule. Where a clearance application was made before the twenty sixth of November, twenty twenty five, clearance was given, and the shares were issued before the twenty sixth of January, twenty twenty six, or within sixty days of the clearance if later, the old rule still applies.

Three practical points. First, the clearance must come before the issue. A clearance timetable has to be built into the deal timetable, which is why a share exchange cannot sensibly complete before the clearance arrives. Second, the clearance is narrow. It says only that section one three seven will not apply. It does not confirm that section one three five applies, that the case one test is met, or whether loan notes are qualifying corporate bonds. Third, one application can cover several clearances at once. H M R C's clearance team accepts a single letter covering share exchanges, reconstructions, demergers and transactions in securities.

Without a clearance, the shareholder simply files on the basis that section one three five applies. If H M R C disagree, they make their just and reasonable adjustments by assessment, or by amending a self assessment, within the usual enquiry and discovery limits. In that dispute the shareholder's best evidence is what Delinian's was, the commercial record made at the time, and its worst is an email like the one that opened this chapter.

Tarnmoor did not apply for the Helmside exchange. Greyfell relied on the exemption, not on section one three five, and Northlight was not part of the deal. Tom Hesketh's board paper said so in one paragraph, and recorded why, so that the decision not to seek clearance was itself on the file.

Earn-outs, and a right that outlives the sale.

Buyers and sellers often disagree about what a company is worth. An earn-out bridges the gap. The buyer pays a fixed price now and promises more later, if the business performs. The tax question is what the seller has disposed of, and when.

The answer comes from Marren versus Ingles, decided in nineteen eighty. An individual sold shares in a private company for an immediate payment, plus a further sum that depended on a future event, the company's later stock market quotation and the price on its first day of dealing. The amount could not be known at the time of the sale. The courts held that the right to the future sum was itself an asset, a chose in action, which means a right that can be enforced only by legal action, such as a right to be paid. Its value at the date of sale formed part of the consideration for the shares. When money was later received under it, that was a disposal of the right, a capital sum derived from an asset. It was not simply the payment of a debt.

Distinguish the ascertainable case. If the extra consideration is a fixed sum payable on a condition, it is ascertainable, even though it may never be paid. Section forty eight of the Gains Act brings it into the original disposal in full, with no discount for waiting or for risk, and adjusts later if it is not paid. For a company there is a twist. Where the deferred consideration takes the form of a creditor relationship, such as loan notes, it is taken out of section forty eight and brought in at fair value, and the loan relationship rules take over. Chapter sixteen covered section forty eight.

For an unascertainable right, the consequences are the ones the November twenty twenty three examiners wanted. The original computation is closed at the date of sale, using the value of the right. It is never reopened. Each later receipt is a separate disposal of the right, or of part of it. That disposal gets none of the reliefs that applied to the original shares. And the election that lets an individual carry a loss on the right back against the original gain is not available to companies.

There is one rescue for earn-outs paid in paper. If the earn-out right can be satisfied only by an issue of the buyer's shares or debentures, and the original exchange would otherwise fall within section one three five, section one three eight A treats the right as a security of the buyer. The exchange is paper for paper, and the later issue of shares is a conversion, not a disposal. The seller can elect out. A company must do so within two years of the end of the accounting period in which the right was conferred, and the election cannot be revoked.

The Tarnmoor earn-out.

On the thirty first of December in Group Year Six, Tarnmoor Engineering sells Tarnmoor Actuators to Brennock Industries, an invented American buyer, for forty eight million pounds in cash, plus a cash earn-out of up to six million pounds, measured on the business's results over the next two years. At completion, the right is valued at three million pounds. The share sale is within the shareholding exemption, with the help of paragraph fifteen A. So the forty eight million pounds, the three million pound value of the earn-out right and the degrouping gain that rides on the proceeds are all exempt, as chapter twenty explains.

The question left open when that sale was planned is what happens to the later receipts. The right is not a share, and it is not one of the share-related assets, such as options, that paragraph two of Schedule seven A C covers. So the generally accepted view, which practitioners apply and which this book follows, is that the shareholding exemption stops at the value of the right at completion. Receipts above that value are chargeable gains. H M R C's capital gains manual does not, so far as this book has found, address the combination directly, so treat the point as the accepted view rather than settled law.

Here is how it plays out in our invented case. Tarnmoor Engineering's base cost in the right is its completion value, three million pounds. In Group Year Seven, Brennock pays the first instalment, two million pounds. The right to the second instalment is then valued at two million pounds. So the first receipt is a part disposal, and the cost is split in proportion, half and half. One and a half million pounds of cost is used, and the gain is half a million pounds, with corporation tax of one hundred and twenty five thousand pounds. In Group Year Eight, the final instalment of two and a half million pounds arrives. Against the remaining cost of one and a half million pounds, the gain is one million pounds, and the tax two hundred and fifty thousand pounds. In total, four and a half million pounds of receipts, against a right valued at three million, gave gains of one and a half million pounds and tax of three hundred and seventy five thousand pounds.

Had the business disappointed, and the receipts totalled less than three million pounds, the shortfall would be a capital loss on the right. On the same reasoning, because a gain on the right would be chargeable, the loss would be allowable. But Tarnmoor Engineering could use it only against its own chargeable gains, or a group company's through a reallocation election, and never against income. The valuation at completion is therefore not a formality. A high value increases the exempt proceeds and reduces later taxable gains. H M R C's valuation specialists can be asked to agree both the completion value and the value of what remains after each instalment.

Going further, and how the examiner tests it.

For a large group, the rules in this chapter turn into a short list of standing tasks.

Keep every share pool current, including pools whose disposals have all been exempt. The exemption can lapse, as Coldwater's sell-by date shows, and the base cost will matter on that day.

Keep a register of holdings with their exemption status. For each holding, record the percentage of shares, profits and assets across the fifty one per cent group, the date the ten per cent threshold was first met and last met, and the six-year expiry date.

Keep evidence that each company you might sell is trading, from management accounts for subsidiaries and published accounts for minority stakes.

Before any hive-down, check that the seller is already in a group, or that there will be twelve months of group existence before the sale. M Group is the warning.

Before any deal paid in shares, decide early whether any seller will rely on section one three five. If so, build the clearance into the timetable, and assume the five per cent exception has gone.

When designing an earn-out, compare a fixed contingent sum, which falls into the original exempt disposal, with a formula that creates a separate taxable right, and agree the completion value with the valuation specialists.

And never plan to fail the exemption just to keep a loss without reading section sixteen A first.

On the exam side, in May twenty twenty five a twenty mark question tested share disposals by an investment company, with the exemption across group members, pooling and matching, and the base cost after a share exchange. In November twenty twenty three a fifteen mark question tested a joint venture sold with an earn-out, the share exchange conditions and stamp duty, and a computation question included an Irish subsidiary sold at a loss within the exemption. Both twenty twenty four sittings brought the exemption into sales of subsidiaries.

The style is technical prose and computations, with half a mark to a mark per point. The reported traps are consistent. Letting indexation create or increase a loss. Computing a loss the exemption makes irrelevant. Reopening the original computation when an earn-out is paid. Getting the base cost wrong after an exchange. The reading edition sets out the layouts.

What to take away.

When a company parts with shares, ask five questions in order. Which shares were sold, under the matching rules. Whether the asset is really a debt, and so a loan relationship. Whether the substantial shareholding exemption applies. Whether a reorganisation or exchange means there is no disposal. And only then, the ordinary computation.

Companies match same day, then the previous ten days, then the section one oh four pool, with no thirty-day rule. Pool indexation is frozen at December twenty seventeen and can never create or increase a loss.

For a company, any loan relationship asset is a qualifying corporate bond, so debt is taxed as income under the loan relationship rules, and gilts and bonds are outside the gains code.

The shareholding exemption needs at least ten per cent of the shares, profits and assets, held across the fifty one per cent group for twelve months in the six years before the sale. The company sold must trade from the start of that period to the sale, and immediately afterwards only if the buyer is connected or the hive-down rule is used. The seller need not trade. The exemption is automatic. It exempts gains and denies losses, and paragraph four puts it in front of every no-disposal rule. Tarnmoor plc's sale of four per cent of Coldwater gave an exempt gain of two point one six million pounds, and its remaining eight per cent stays exempt until about September of Group Year Nine.

Paragraph fifteen A counts the group's use of a trade's assets before a hive-down, but a single company is not a group, as M Group Holdings showed.

A share exchange within section one three five is no disposal, unless the exemption applies first. In the Helmside exchange, Greyfell's eight million pound gain was exempt, Tarnmoor plc's cost was the sixteen million pound value of the shares it issued, giving a Helmside pool of twenty five million pounds, and stamp duty was eighty thousand pounds.

The new section one three seven applies to issues from the twenty sixth of November, twenty twenty five. It tests the main purposes of the arrangements relating to the exchange, counteracts by just and reasonable adjustments, and has no five per cent exception. Clearance must come before the issue.

A company receiving a stock dividend gets bonus issue treatment, with no extra cost, on H M R C's view.

An unascertainable earn-out is a separate asset under Marren versus Ingles. Its completion value is part of the sale proceeds, and later receipts are disposals of the right, outside the exemption on the accepted view. Tarnmoor Engineering's receipts of four and a half million pounds against a three million pound right gave gains of one and a half million pounds.

So the answer to the chapter's question is that the law does not ask whether shares were swapped or sold. It asks, in a fixed order, what kind of holding this is and why the deal was shaped as it was.

Chapter twenty: Buying and selling companies.

On the thirty first of August, twenty twenty three, the Upper Tribunal decided a case that turned on one missing month. The company was M Group Holdings Limited. It had run a trade itself, as a single company with no subsidiaries.

On the twenty ninth of June, twenty fifteen, it formed a subsidiary, Medinet Clinical Services Limited. On the thirtieth of September it moved its trade and assets into that subsidiary. On the twenty seventh of May, twenty sixteen, it sold the subsidiary to an outside buyer. That was just under eleven months after the subsidiary was born. The reported gain was about fifty three million pounds.

The company claimed the substantial shareholding exemption, which needs a holding of at least twelve months. It relied on a special rule for hive-downs, which lets a seller count time when the business's assets were used in the trade of another member of its group. The First-tier Tribunal said no. The Upper Tribunal, Mr Justice Michael Green and Judge Phyllis Ramshaw, agreed. Before the subsidiary existed there was no group, because a group requires more than one company. The missing month could not be borrowed.

Some years into our invented story, Tarnmoor Engineering does something that looks almost identical. Tarnmoor, its companies, its people and its numbers are invented for teaching, as they are throughout this book. On the first of February in Group Year Six, Tarnmoor Engineering moves its actuators business into a new subsidiary. On the thirty first of December, eleven months later, it sells that subsidiary to an American buyer. Same gap. Different answer. By the end of this chapter you will see why.

This is the second last chapter of Part Three. It owns no single row of the syllabus. Instead it applies a dozen core rows at once, the way the examiner does. They include the shareholding exemption, companies leaving groups, change in company ownership, value shifting, depreciatory transactions, earn-outs, returns and instalments, the senior accounting officer and transfer pricing. Stamp taxes, at awareness level, run alongside. It builds on The Living Law, chapters twenty three and twenty four, on company gains and on groups.

Most of the gains law is in the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act nineteen ninety two, which I will call the Gains Act. Intangible assets and trading profits sit in the Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, the two thousand and nine Act. Losses and distributions sit in the Corporation Tax Act twenty ten, the twenty ten Act. We apply the law for the financial year twenty twenty six.

Two ways to buy a business.

A business can change hands in two ways. The buyer can buy the company that owns the business. Or the buyer can buy the business's assets out of the company. The commercial result can look the same. The tax result rarely is.

Think of it as buying a house. Buy the house with everything in it, and you get the previous owner's furniture, the unpaid bills in the drawer and the quarrel with the neighbours. Buy only the furniture you choose, and you leave the drawer and the neighbours behind. A share purchase is the first. An asset purchase is the second.

Start with the buyer. On a share purchase, nothing inside the company changes for tax. Its assets keep their old base costs, their old tax written down values and their old capital allowance pools. The buyer gets a cost for the shares and nothing else. The Living Law, chapter twenty three, explained why that cost will probably never matter. A later sale of a trading subsidiary is likely to fall within the shareholding exemption, so the base cost is never used.

On an asset purchase, the buyer pays for each asset separately, and each gets a fresh tax history at the price paid. Plant gets capital allowances on its cost. Intangible assets such as patents and customer relationships come into the buyer's intangibles regime at cost, relieved as the accounts amortise them or, by election, at four per cent a year. Purchased goodwill gets relief at six point five per cent a year only where it comes with qualifying intellectual property, and only up to six times that spending. Chapter eleven explained those limits. Land gets a new base cost for gains.

That fresh start is called a step-up. It is worth real money, because it turns the price into future deductions. A share buyer forgoes it.

The asset buyer also leaves the history behind. Any tax the old company failed to pay stays with the old company. Any open enquiry, any doubtful claim, any loss the change in ownership rules will cancel, all of it stays with the seller. The share buyer takes all of it, along with the company.

Two more differences decide many deals. The first is stamp taxes. Shares cost the buyer stamp duty at half a per cent. Land bought directly costs stamp duty land tax at up to five per cent on commercial property. Chapter twenty one owns those rules. The second is value added tax. A transfer of a business as a going concern is usually outside the tax, and a company joining a group may join its value added tax group. That is a signpost only, because the paper does not examine value added tax.

The misconception to clear away first is this. People say a share sale is always better for the seller. Often it is. But only if the shareholding exemption applies, and only if the seller does not give the advantage back in the price.

The seller's side of the table.

Now sit on the other side. A company selling a trading subsidiary usually wants to sell shares. If the conditions of the shareholding exemption are met, the gain is simply not a chargeable gain. There is nothing to claim and nothing to pay. Chapter eighteen owns the conditions. In outline, the seller must have held at least ten per cent for a continuous twelve months within the six years before the sale. And the company sold must be a trading company or the holding company of a trading group.

An asset sale is very different for the selling company. A gain on land is chargeable. Plant sold for more than its tax written down value produces a balancing charge. Plant that had full expensing produces a special balancing charge on the proceeds, as chapter eight showed. Home-grown intangibles are the sharpest point. Chapter eleven showed that a company selling patents or know-how it created is taxed on almost the whole price as income. The shareholding exemption protects a sale of shares, never a sale of the assets themselves.

And the cash is then stuck in the selling company. If the group wants it in the parent, it must pay a dividend up the chain. For a corporate seller that dividend is usually exempt, so the second layer costs little. For an individual owner, a second layer of tax on extraction is the main reason to resist an asset sale. That is why owner-managed businesses are so often sold as shares.

So why would a seller ever accept an asset deal? Because the buyer pays for what it gets. A buyer who loses a step-up worth, say, a million pounds of future tax will want some of that back in the price. A buyer who fears hidden liabilities will want indemnities, a retention from the price, or a lower price. The structure is a trade. The seller gains tax and certainty. The buyer gains relief and protection. Price settles the difference, and it is often the largest number in the negotiation.

That is why tax advisers say the question is never just share or asset. The question is what each side's tax position is worth, and who pays for it. The examiner sees it the same way. The Chartered Institute of Taxation's own description of the paper asks candidates to plan for corporate transformations. Its syllabus for twenty twenty eight names share versus asset sale as a topic in its own right.

What comes with the shares.

The share buyer inherits the company's whole tax history. Five parts of it matter most at this level, and each one changes what the buyer should pay.

First, losses. Chapter fourteen explained that a loss-making company's losses do not travel safely through a change of ownership. More than half of the ordinary share capital changing hands is a change in ownership. A major change in the nature or conduct of the trade, in any five-year period beginning up to three years before the change, cancels the carried-forward trading losses. Even with no change, Chapter two C of Part fourteen stops the new group using the target's pre-acquisition losses by group relief for five years. A buyer who pays for losses is paying for something the law may take away.

Second, base costs and pools. The company keeps its own. If its factory has a low base cost, the latent gain comes too, and will be taxed if the factory is ever sold.

Third, intangible assets. When a group buys a company, it buys shares, and shares are outside the intangibles regime. The goodwill and customer relationships inside the company never become the buyer's own intangible assets for tax. In the group's consolidated accounts, though, they appear at fair value. That gap is a deferred tax liability. In our invented case, Tarnmoor's accounts recognised Calder's customer relationships at four million pounds, with a deferred tax liability of one million pounds, as chapter six showed. The tax return sees none of it.

Fourth, secondary liabilities. A company in a group can be made to pay tax that belongs to someone else. Under section one ninety of the Gains Act, H M R C can collect unpaid tax on a group company's gain from other members of the fifty one per cent group in some cases. Under Chapter six of Part fourteen, tax a company left unpaid before a change in ownership can be collected from the people who controlled it before. A stamp duty land tax relief claimed by the target can be clawed back from the target when it leaves its old group, as Tarnmoor will discover. These are the drawers full of other people's bills.

Fifth, the target's status. Nothing about it resets on completion. Its instalment history, its research and development claims, its transfer pricing position and its returns all continue. Due diligence exists to find out what they are.

Due diligence.

Due diligence is the buyer's investigation of the target before it signs. The tax part has a simple purpose. It finds every past or present tax exposure the buyer would inherit, values it, and decides how the contract should deal with it. It also finds the tax assets, so the buyer knows what it is really buying.

A tax due diligence report for a larger target works through a familiar list. Returns, payments and penalties come first, including whether the company has paid by instalments when it should have. Open enquiries and disputes follow, with time limits for discovery. Losses come next, by type and age, with the change in ownership analysis. Then capital allowances, including whether fixtures claims are supported by elections and structures and buildings allowance statements. Then intangible assets and their tax values. Then transfer pricing, the interest restriction, withholding tax on payments abroad, research and development claims, the senior accounting officer regime, uncertain tax treatments, and any tax avoidance schemes the company used or was offered. Employment taxes, value added tax and stamp taxes complete it.

The buyer's team does not just list findings. It sorts them. Some are dealt with in the price. Some by an indemnity. Some by a condition that must be fixed before completion. Some are simply noted.

Tarnmoor bought Brackenwell Sensors on the first of July in Group Year Two. The diligence found four things worth telling. Brackenwell's return for its last year as an independent company was unfiled, and it carried a payable research and development credit claim of nine hundred and forty three thousand, nine hundred and fifty pounds. Its two earlier returns had both been late. Its eight point six million pounds of losses would not survive Tarnmoor's own integration plan, which would make Brackenwell a captive supplier to the group. And it paid royalties to a university in Vallaria, our invented country, deducting tax at the treaty rate of five per cent.

Each finding needed a different answer. The losses went into the price at nil, as chapter fourteen explained. The late returns went into the filing plan, because a third late return in a row would carry a higher fixed penalty, as chapter three showed. The royalty withholding was checked against the treaty and the paperwork for the reduced rate. The credit went into the contract, as the next section explains.

One more finding was a missed opportunity. Research and development status is tested across linked enterprises. On H M R C's view, a company taken over by a large group is large for the whole accounting period in which the takeover happens. Ending Brackenwell's accounting period at completion could have kept its first six months under the scheme for small, research-intensive companies. Nobody thought of it in time. Chapter ten tells that story. The lesson is that research status belongs on the diligence list before signing, not after.

Warranties, indemnities and the tax deed.

The contract to buy a company is the share purchase agreement. Its tax provisions come in two forms, and the difference between them is worth understanding.

A warranty is a statement of fact by the seller about the company. For example, that all returns have been filed on time, or that no enquiry is open. If a warranty proves untrue, the buyer can sue for damages. But damages for breach of warranty compensate for the loss in the value of the shares, and the buyer must prove that loss. The seller can also qualify warranties by disclosure. A matter fairly disclosed before signing cannot found a claim. Warranties therefore work mainly as a tool to make the seller disclose.

An indemnity is a promise to pay. The tax indemnity is often set out in a separate tax deed or tax covenant. It says that the seller will pay the buyer, pound for pound, any tax of the company arising from events before completion. No loss in share value needs to be shown. Disclosure does not cut it down. That is why buyers want an indemnity for tax, and why sellers negotiate its limits. Those are a time limit, a cap, exclusions for tax already provided for in the accounts, and conduct rights over any dispute with H M R C.

Tax deeds also deal with the payments themselves. A typical deed says that any payment under it is treated as a reduction in the price. The tax system broadly follows. Under section forty nine of the Gains Act, a seller who pays out under a warranty can have its gain recomputed as if the price had been lower. H M R C's published concession, extra statutory concession D thirty three, applies the same treatment to indemnity payments in practice. For the buyer, the payment then reduces the cost of the shares instead of being taxed as a receipt. Deeds also contain a gross-up, so that if the buyer is taxed on a payment anyway, the seller pays more to cover it.

Now return to Brackenwell's credit. The money would arrive after completion, in a company Tarnmoor now owned. Who should have it? In our invented case, the price of twenty two point two million pounds assumed that the credit would be paid in full. The tax deed then made the sellers liable to repay Tarnmoor if H M R C reduced the credit after an enquiry. The sellers were paid for the credit once. The buyer bore no risk that it might shrink.

The same logic runs through every deal in this chapter. Tax that belongs to the seller's years is the seller's. The contract makes sure the buyer can recover it, whoever H M R C sends the bill to.

The costs of buying.

A larger acquisition generates a pile of invoices. The examiner likes them, because each takes a different route. The May twenty twenty three paper gave ten marks to nothing else.

Chapter thirteen worked through the leading case. The case was Centrica Overseas Holdings versus H M R C, decided by the Supreme Court in twenty twenty four. Advisers' fees for selling a subsidiary were capital, so the investment company could not deduct them as expenses of management. H M R C's view is that appraising possible investments is revenue, but costs become capital at the earliest when the company decides to acquire a particular target. In our invented case, Tarnmoor plc deducted three hundred thousand pounds of strategic review costs incurred before its board decided to bid for Calder. The nine hundred thousand pounds of fees after that decision were capital.

Finance costs go elsewhere. The arrangement fee on the bank facility that funded Calder was a loan relationship debit, relieved as the accounts spread it, as chapter twelve explained. The examiners noted that some candidates missed exactly that point in May twenty twenty three.

Stamp duty is part of the cost of the shares. Tarnmoor paid one hundred and sixty thousand pounds on Calder's thirty two million pounds, and one hundred and eleven thousand pounds on Brackenwell's twenty two point two million pounds. Brackenwell also produced a smaller trap. On the same day, Tarnmoor bought Brackenwell's convertible notes from the venture investors for one point eight million pounds. Transfers of loan capital are normally free of stamp duty. But the exemption in section seventy nine of the Finance Act nineteen eighty six does not apply to loan capital carrying a right of conversion into shares. So the transfer of the notes cost another nine thousand pounds.

Payments to people come last. Compensation for loss of office paid by the target to a departing director is deductible only if it is paid wholly and exclusively for the target's trade. H M R C's guidance says that test is unlikely to be met where the payment is connected with a sale of the business or a change in the controlling shareholding. The paperwork, such as the share purchase agreement and the negotiation emails, often shows the real purpose.

A retention bonus is different. A target that pays key staff to stay after completion is paying for their future services. That is an ordinary employment cost of its trade. The usual rule for remuneration applies. If it is not paid within nine months of the end of the period of account, it is deducted only when paid. If the acquiring holding company pays it instead, it is paying for services to another company, and it may get no deduction at all. Who pays matters as much as what is paid.

The day a company joins.

When a company joins a large group, almost every compliance setting changes. The November twenty twenty four paper turned that into a twenty mark question. An American group had bought a small British company, and candidates had to explain its new obligations. The marking guide spread the marks across instalments, the country-by-country report, transfer pricing, the interest restriction and stamp duty. Use the same checklist on every acquisition.

Begin with associated companies, because two different clocks start. For the small profits rate and marginal relief, a company counts as associated if it is associated at any time in the accounting period. So the target's limits are divided at once, for the whole period in which it joins. For quarterly instalment payments, H M R C's manual says associates are counted on the day before the accounting period begins. So the instalment thresholds change only from the next period that starts after the company joins. Chapter three set out both timings.

Next, group relief. The target can surrender to, and claim from, its new group only from the day it joins, and only for the overlapping part of each accounting period. Its old group's relief stops even earlier, once arrangements for the sale exist. Chapter fifteen owns those rules. The group's single five million pound deductions allowance for carried-forward losses also needs the newcomer to sign the group's nomination. And the group's one annual investment allowance of one million pounds is now shared with it.

Then governance. A company that joins a group with turnover above two hundred million pounds falls within the senior accounting officer regime. But it counts for a financial year only if it was a member of the group at the end of its previous financial year. The group's published tax strategy must cover it. The uncertain tax treatment rules start to apply to its returns.

Then the borders. Transfer pricing has an exemption for small and medium-sized enterprises, and size is measured across linked enterprises. Join a large group and the exemption is gone. The target's dealings with group companies abroad must now be at arm's length and documented, in the group's master file and the company's local file, and the target appears in the group's country-by-country report. For periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six, dealings between two companies both within U K corporation tax are generally exempt. That limits the practical change for a target whose group dealings are all domestic. Chapter twenty seven owns those rules. The interest restriction now tests the target as part of the worldwide group, with one reporting company filing for all. And the global minimum tax rules reach it as a member of a group with revenue above seven hundred and fifty million euros.

Finally, research and development. A small company joining a large group loses the scheme for research-intensive small companies and moves to the expenditure credit. That was Brackenwell's story.

The marking guide's lesson is that every item earns marks, and the easy ones are easy to miss. The examiners reported that most candidates found transfer pricing, but some wrote at length about it and ignored easier points, and some raised hybrid mismatches with nothing in the question to trigger them.

Calder and Brackenwell join Tarnmoor.

Apply the checklist to our invented group's two purchases.

Tarnmoor plc bought Calder Valve Engineering from the Oldroyd family, our invented former owners, on the first of April in Group Year One. The price was thirty two million pounds in cash. Stamp duty was one hundred and sixty thousand pounds. The Oldroyds are individuals, so their side of the deal is capital gains tax, which this paper does not examine.

Calder's accounting period simply began that day, because its year ended on the thirty first of March. A change of owner never ends an accounting period by itself.

For marginal relief, Calder had eight associates at once, so its limits were divided by nine. That made no difference, because Calder's profits were far above them. For instalments, the day before its first group period was the thirty first of March, when the Oldroyds owned no other companies. So Calder's divisor was one. With profits of three million pounds it was large, not very large. It paid four instalments of one hundred and eighty seven thousand, five hundred pounds, in months seven, ten, thirteen and sixteen. From its next accounting period, beginning on the first of April in Group Year Two, its associates were counted on the day before. There were eight of them, so the very large threshold fell to twenty million divided by nine, about two point two two million pounds. Calder became very large one period after it joined, and paid six instalments in a single calendar year. That is the cash trap in every acquisition. The buyer's treasury needs the dates before the first one falls due.

Calder's other settings moved too. Its first senior accounting officer year was the one beginning on the first of April in Group Year Two, because it had not been a member at the end of an earlier year. It joined the group's deductions allowance nomination on the day it joined. Its year to the thirty first of March overlapped two group years, which complicated group relief and the shared annual investment allowance. And its dealings with Tarnmoor's overseas companies, which began in later years, brought transfer pricing into its life.

Brackenwell joined on the first of July in Group Year Two. Tarnmoor paid twenty two point two million pounds for its shares and one point eight million pounds for its three million pounds of convertible notes. Chapter twelve explained why buying a connected company's debt at a discount is dangerous. A deemed release credit of one point two million pounds was avoided only because Tarnmoor released the notes within sixty days under the corporate rescue exception.

Brackenwell lost three things by joining. Its losses were cancelled by the major change in its trade, as chapter fourteen showed. Its small company research relief was replaced by the expenditure credit, as chapter ten showed. And its exemption from transfer pricing went, though its new dealings were with Tarnmoor companies in the U K, inside the domestic exemption. It also brought one thing in. A capital loss of four hundred thousand pounds, realised on a failed spin-out before it joined, is a pre-entry loss. Chapter seventeen explains the narrow uses still open to it.

Selling a subsidiary.

Now turn round. A group selling a subsidiary has its own checklist, and the examiner has used it twice in recent sittings. The May twenty twenty four paper combined a sale with the degrouping charge, the shareholding exemption, the change in ownership rules and enquiries. The November twenty twenty four paper combined a sale with pre-sale transactions, depreciatory transactions, value shifting and pre-sale dividends. Work through it in this order.

First, does the shareholding exemption cover the share sale? Chapter eighteen owns the conditions. If it applies, gains are exempt and losses are not allowable.

Second, does the company leave with assets it received from another group member in the last six years? If so, section one seven nine of the Gains Act, the degrouping charge, treats it as having sold and bought back the asset at market value immediately after it received it. Chapter seventeen owns the rule. The point for a sale is where the gain lands. Since twenty eleven, where the company leaves because a group member sells shares, the degrouping gain is added to the seller's sale proceeds for the shares. If the share sale is exempt, so is the degrouping gain. And the leaving company keeps the asset at its market value at the date of the transfer. The intangibles regime reaches a similar result by another road. Its degrouping charge is simply switched off where the share sale qualifies for the exemption, under section seven eight two A of the two thousand and nine Act. But then the asset keeps its old tax value. Nothing is rebased.

Third, does stamp duty land tax group relief claw back? If land moved into the company within three years and the company now leaves the group, the relief is withdrawn, charged on the market value at the original transfer. The bill falls on the company that leaves. Chapter twenty one owns the rule.

Fourth, what is happening to value before the sale? A pre-sale dividend can take cash out of the target and reduce the price. The value shifting rule for companies, section thirty one of the Gains Act, adds back value stripped from shares under arrangements with a main purpose of avoiding corporation tax on a gain. But it does not apply where the arrangements consist only of an exempt distribution. An ordinary dividend paid from the target's profits is outside it. The depreciatory transaction rule in section one seven six works differently. It has no motive test, and it only reduces a loss. The November twenty twenty four examiners reported that candidates confused the two, and missed the exempt distribution carve-out.

Fifth, when does the company leave? For gains and degrouping, on the sale. For group relief, earlier, when arrangements for the sale exist. H M R C's guidance treats a plain sale of a subsidiary as an arrangement. Its guidance on consortium arrangements dates them from the point the parties agree in principle, even subject to contract. Chapter fifteen looks at the timing in detail.

Sixth, how is the price paid? Cash now is simple. A right to an uncertain amount later, an earn-out, is a separate asset, under the House of Lords' decision in Marren versus Ingles in nineteen eighty. Its value at completion is part of the sale proceeds.

The hive-down.

The hive-down is the group's standard way of selling a business that is not already in its own company. The group moves the trade and its assets into a new subsidiary, and then sells the subsidiary's shares.

Each part of the move is designed to be tax-neutral. Under Chapter one of Part twenty two of the twenty ten Act, a transfer of a trade between companies under seventy five per cent common ownership is not treated as a cessation. Capital allowances carry on at tax written down value, and any trading losses can pass, subject to a restriction where the transferor's liabilities stay behind. Under section one seven one of the Gains Act, land and other capital assets pass at no gain, no loss. Under section seven seven five of the two thousand and nine Act, intangible assets pass at their tax value. Stamp duty land tax group relief covers the land, if no arrangements exist at that time for the new company to leave the group. Chapter nineteen owns the transfer of trade rules.

Why do it? Because the buyer gets a clean company. Its only history is the history since the hive-down. Every older liability stays with the seller. The seller, in return, gets a share sale, which can fall within the exemption.

The difficulty is time. The exemption needs twelve months of holding, and a new subsidiary has none. Paragraph fifteen A of Schedule seven A C to the Gains Act fills the gap. Suppose the subsidiary uses assets that another member of the group used in its trade. Then the seller is treated as having held the shares during any time in the previous twelve months when that member used them. One condition follows. Because the holding period relies on that rule, the company sold must still be trading immediately after the sale.

That is where M Group failed. Before its subsidiary was formed, M Group Holdings was a single company. Its assets were used in a trade, but not by a member of a group, because there was no group. A business run by a single company cannot hive down and sell within twelve months. A business run inside an existing group can.

The hive-down does not dissolve every charge. The degrouping charge still arises, though it is usually exempt. Stamp duty land tax relief claws back if the sale comes within three years. Value added tax needs the going concern rules to apply to each transfer. And if the new company has losses, the change in ownership rules apply to them when it is sold.

Tarnmoor Actuators is sold.

Now the story. On the first of February in Group Year Six, Tarnmoor Engineering hived its actuators business down into a new subsidiary, Tarnmoor Actuators. In our invented case, the board's reason was to run actuators as a separate business with its own management. No buyer was in view at that time. An invented American company, Brennock Industries, approached the group that summer.

Tarnmoor Actuators received three assets that matter here. A factory, which had cost Tarnmoor Engineering five million pounds and was worth seven point five million pounds at the transfer. A set of patents, with a tax written down value of one point two million pounds and a market value of four million pounds. And plant, which moved at its tax written down value.

On the thirty first of December, eleven months later, Tarnmoor Engineering sold the shares to Brennock. The price was forty eight million pounds in cash, plus an earn-out of up to six million pounds based on the company's results over the next two years. The earn-out right was valued at three million pounds at completion.

First, the exemption. Tarnmoor Engineering had held the shares for only eleven months. But the actuators assets had been used in its own trade, as a member of the Tarnmoor group, for the whole of the previous twelve months. So paragraph fifteen A gives it the twelve months it needs. Tarnmoor Actuators was trading before and after the sale. The exemption applies.

Second, the factory. It came from another group member within six years, so the degrouping charge applies. The gain is seven point five million pounds less five million pounds, two point five million pounds. It is added to Tarnmoor Engineering's proceeds for the shares. Those become forty eight million pounds of cash, three million pounds for the earn-out right and two point five million pounds of degrouping gain. That is fifty three point five million pounds in all. All of it is exempt. Taxed on its own, the degrouping gain would have cost six hundred and twenty five thousand pounds. And Tarnmoor Actuators keeps the factory at a base cost of seven point five million pounds.

Third, the patents. The intangibles degrouping charge is switched off, because the share sale qualifies for the exemption. Without that rule, a credit of two point eight million pounds would have arisen in Tarnmoor Actuators, costing seven hundred thousand pounds. The patents keep their tax value of one point two million pounds.

Fourth, stamp duty land tax. The factory moved with group relief less than three years before the sale. The relief is clawed back, on the market value at the transfer, seven point five million pounds. The bill is three hundred and sixty four thousand, five hundred pounds, and the law puts it on Tarnmoor Actuators, which by then belongs to Brennock. Brennock's lawyers insisted on an indemnity. Tarnmoor Engineering pays.

Fifth, the pre-sale dividend. In December, Tarnmoor Actuators paid Tarnmoor Engineering a dividend of two million pounds out of its profits since the hive-down. The dividend was exempt for Tarnmoor Engineering, and because the arrangement consisted only of an exempt distribution, the value shifting rule did not apply. It took cash out before the sale. It also meant the buyer paid for a company that had already paid out that cash, so there was less consideration on which to pay stamp duty.

Sixth, the buyer's stamp duty. Brennock must pay stamp duty at half a per cent on the share transfer, within thirty days, and an overseas buyer is not excused. On the cash price that is two hundred and forty thousand pounds. But stamp duty also catches contingent consideration. On the contingency principle, which H M R C's stamp taxes manual traces to a House of Lords decision of nineteen oh six, a stated maximum is charged in full. So the earn-out's cap of six million pounds adds thirty thousand pounds, whether or not it is ever paid. Brennock's bill is two hundred and seventy thousand pounds in all.

Seventh, leaving. Tarnmoor Actuators had no losses, so the group relief arrangements rules took nothing away. Its accounting period ended on the thirty first of December in any case. In Brennock's hands it starts the next one with a new set of associated companies, and Brennock's advisers now run the joining checklist from the other side.

The earn-out itself is the last loose thread. Each later payment will be a disposal of the earn-out right, not of shares. Chapter eighteen looks at how those receipts are taxed.

Shares or assets, priced.

Suppose, for this example only, that Tarnmoor Engineering had sold the actuators assets to Brennock directly, at the same values. Two items alone show the difference.

The factory gain of two point five million pounds would have been chargeable, at a cost of six hundred and twenty five thousand pounds. The patents would have produced a taxable credit of two point eight million pounds, costing seven hundred thousand pounds. That is one point three two five million pounds of corporation tax before any balancing charges on plant. On the share sale it was nil. Every pound of that saving came from the shareholding exemption.

What did Brennock give up? On the factory, almost nothing. The degrouping rule rebased it to seven point five million pounds anyway. On the patents, everything. Bought as assets, they would have had a tax cost of four million pounds, relieved over time. Bought inside the company, they carry one point two million pounds. That lost step-up is worth up to seven hundred thousand pounds of future tax, before discounting for time. A well-advised buyer asks for some of it back in the price.

The plant tells the same story on a smaller scale. Bought as assets, it would have entered Brennock's pools at its price, though as second-hand plant it would get no full expensing. Inside the company it carries on at its old tax written down value, with any balancing charges deferred to a later sale.

Stamp taxes cut the other way. A direct purchase would have cost Brennock stamp duty land tax of three hundred and sixty four thousand, five hundred pounds on the factory. The share route cost a clawback of the same amount, borne by Tarnmoor Engineering under the indemnity, plus Brennock's stamp duty of two hundred and seventy thousand pounds. A hive-down followed by a sale within three years made the stamp taxes dearer, not cheaper.

The tax function's job is to run this comparison before heads of terms are signed, not after. Once the structure is agreed in principle, the price has been struck on it, and the arrangements that switch off reliefs may already exist.

So the misconception from the start of the chapter has its answer. The share sale was better for Tarnmoor. But only because the exemption applied. And the price still had to absorb the buyer's lost relief on the patents, though not on the factory.

How the examiner tests it.

This topic rarely arrives as one question with one label. It arrives as a scenario. A group buys or sells a company, and the requirement asks for the tax consequences.

Four recent questions show the shapes. In May twenty twenty three, ten marks went on acquisition costs. They covered due diligence, legal fees, a loan arrangement fee, compensation for loss of office and retention bonuses. In May twenty twenty four, fifteen marks went on the sale of a subsidiary after an intra-group transfer, with the degrouping charge and the exemption, then losses after the change in ownership, then enquiries. The examiners said it was well answered, but some candidates wrote about loss relief before the sale, which was not asked. In November twenty twenty four, fifteen marks went on pre-sale transactions, and twenty marks on a company joining an American group.

The style is technical explanation with short computations. Half a mark to one mark per point. When the requirement says tax consequences, the examiners expect every tax in the syllabus, including stamp duty and stamp duty land tax. When it names a tax, nothing else scores.

The grades tell you what to expect. The shareholding exemption, companies leaving groups, change in company ownership, value shifting, depreciatory transactions, Marren versus Ingles, the senior accounting officer and transfer pricing are all core. Stamp duty and stamp duty land tax are awareness only, but they are the add-on marks candidates most often leave behind.

A good answer to a deal question follows the deal. Say what happens on the day the company is bought or sold, then what changes afterwards, tax by tax. Give the rule, apply it to the figures, and give the number. Where the requirement asks for a recommendation, give one. Where the facts leave a point open, such as whether a change in a trade is major, state the facts that drive your conclusion. The marks are for the reasoning as much as the answer.

The traps are consistent. Using market value instead of the transferor's cost on a no gain, no loss transfer. Measuring the degrouping gain at the date of leaving instead of the date of the transfer. Computing a loss on a sale within the exemption. Confusing stamp duty with stamp duty land tax. Counting associates on the wrong date. Writing at length on transfer pricing and missing easy marks elsewhere. And forgetting the stamp taxes altogether.

What to take away.

A business can be bought as shares or as assets. A share buyer gets the company's whole history and no step-up. An asset buyer gets a fresh tax cost for each asset and leaves the history behind. A share seller usually gets the substantial shareholding exemption. An asset seller pays tax on gains, balancing charges and intangibles credits. Price settles the difference.

Due diligence finds the history. Warranties make the seller disclose. A tax indemnity makes the seller pay, pound for pound, for tax of the years it owned the company. Payments under either are usually treated as reductions in the price.

Acquisition costs follow their own routes. Costs after the decision to buy a target are capital. Arrangement fees are loan relationship debits. Stamp duty is part of the cost of the shares, and convertible notes are not exempt loan capital. Compensation for loss of office tied to a change of control is unlikely to be deductible.

When a company joins a large group, count associates twice. For marginal relief, at any time in the period. For instalments, on the day before the period begins. Calder was large in its first group period and very large one period later. Then work through group relief, the deductions allowance, the annual investment allowance, the senior accounting officer, transfer pricing and its records, the interest restriction, the global minimum tax and research and development status.

When a group sells a subsidiary, check the exemption, the degrouping charge, stamp duty land tax clawback, value shifting and depreciatory transactions, the leaving date and the form of the price. A degrouping gain on a share sale is added to the seller's proceeds and is usually exempt. The intangibles charge is switched off instead, with no rebasing.

A hive-down gives the buyer a clean company. Paragraph fifteen A lends the seller the trade's history inside the group, but only inside a group, as M Group learnt.

In our invented case, Tarnmoor Engineering sold Tarnmoor Actuators for forty eight million pounds and an earn-out valued at three million pounds. The two point five million pound degrouping gain joined fifty three point five million pounds of exempt proceeds. The patents kept their tax value of one point two million pounds. The stamp duty land tax clawback of three hundred and sixty four thousand, five hundred pounds was paid under an indemnity. And Brennock paid stamp duty of two hundred and seventy thousand pounds, because the earn-out's cap counted.

Eleven months was enough for Tarnmoor, because the group had been there all along.

Many of the taxes in this chapter were stamp taxes, quietly attached to bigger questions. Chapter twenty one, on stamp taxes for groups, gives them a chapter of their own.

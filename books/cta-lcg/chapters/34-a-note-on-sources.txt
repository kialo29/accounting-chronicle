A note on sources.

Every rule in this book came from somewhere. This note says where. It describes the places corporate tax law lives and how to read them, it says plainly what this book could not open while it was being written, and it suggests how to keep up once the exam is behind you. The law described throughout is the law for the financial year twenty twenty six, and the facts in this note are as they stood in October twenty twenty six.

The statute book.

The law itself lives on legislation dot gov dot uk, the free official site run by the National Archives. Every Act this book relies on is there. The Corporation Tax Acts of two thousand and nine and twenty ten. The Taxation, International and Other Provisions, Act twenty ten, which this book has called the International Act. The Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act nineteen ninety two, the Capital Allowances Act two thousand and one, and the Taxes Management Act nineteen seventy. The company return rules in Schedule eighteen to the Finance Act nineteen ninety eight. The Pillar Two legislation in the Finance Number Two Act twenty twenty three. And the Finance Act twenty twenty six itself, which received Royal Assent on the eighteenth of March, twenty twenty six.

The site gives you each Act in two forms. The revised text shows the law as later Acts have amended it. The enacted text shows exactly what Parliament passed on the day. You need both. The revised text tells you what a section says now. The enacted text tells you what an amending Act did, and its commencement provisions tell you from when. Those provisions often sit at the end of a Schedule, far from the section they govern.

Read the box headed Changes to legislation before you rely on any revised page. It tells you the date up to which the text is up to date with changes known to be in force, and it lists any effects the editors have not yet applied. The site itself warns that revised legislation may not be fully up to date. When the box lists outstanding changes, the words on your screen may not be the law.

The site also keeps point in time versions, so you can read a section as it stood on a chosen date. For most legislation the record starts on the first of February, nineteen ninety one. For a tax adviser this is one of the most useful features on the site, because tax law applies by period. A company with a thirty first of March year end and a company with a thirty first of December year end can be under different versions of the same section on the same day, as chapter two showed with the new rules on permanent establishments.

Statutory instruments live on the same site, with the same features. The instalment payment regulations, the Disregard Regulations, the change of accounting practice regulations, the country-by-country reporting regulations and the transfer pricing records regulations all appear in this book. So do treaties, which reach a company's return through Orders in Council under section two of the International Act.

Judgments.

Court and tribunal judgments are published free by the National Archives through its Find Case Law service, which launched in April twenty twenty two. You can search it by case name, neutral citation, judge, court or date. The neutral citation is the quickest key. BlackRock, for example, is twenty twenty four, England and Wales Court of Appeal, Civil Division, number three hundred and thirty, and in the reading edition you will see it written in its standard short form.

Coverage of older decisions is uneven, so for an older case you may need the court's or tribunal's own pages, or the older free archive, the British and Irish Legal Information Institute. Two habits matter more than the source. Read the judgment, not a summary, because the paragraph that decides your point is rarely the one a newsletter quotes. And check what happened next, because a tribunal decision can be reversed, and an appeal may still be pending, as Syngenta's was when this book was finished.

H M R C's manuals and guidance.

H M R C, His Majesty's Revenue and Customs, publishes its internal manuals on the government's website, gov dot uk. This book has drawn on many of them. The International Manual, for treaties, double tax relief, permanent establishments, controlled foreign companies and transfer pricing. The Corporate Finance Manual, for loan relationships, derivatives and the interest restriction. The Company Taxation Manual, for groups, losses, instalments and distributions. The Corporate Intangibles Research and Development Manual. The Capital Gains Manual. The Senior Accounting Officers Guidance. And the Tax Compliance Risk Management manual, which explains how H M R C's large business teams rate a group's tax risk.

Each manual has an updates page, and many pages now carry banners when the law has moved faster than the guidance. Around the manuals sit policy papers, consultation documents and their outcomes, and tax information and impact notes, which say what a measure is for and whom it affects.

Keep their status in mind. A manual is H M R C's view, not law. A tribunal is not bound by it, and where it conflicts with the statute, the statute wins. Where this book has relied on a manual, it has said H M R C's view is, or H M R C's guidance says. But as chapter two explained, departing from H M R C's known position can, for a large business, create a duty to notify under the uncertain tax treatment rules. So the manuals are not law, and they still matter.

The O E C D's texts.

The fourth source is international. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, the O E C D, publishes the Model Tax Convention and its Commentary, the Transfer Pricing Guidelines, the twenty ten report on attributing profits to permanent establishments, the base erosion and profit shifting reports of twenty fifteen, and the model rules and guidance behind Pillar Two.

For most of the corporation tax code these texts are persuasive, not binding. In Fowler versus H M R C, decided by the Supreme Court in twenty twenty, the court said the Commentaries carry persuasive force as the cogency of their reasoning deserves. But for chargeable periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six, the Finance Act twenty twenty six writes them into United Kingdom law in three places. Transfer pricing must be read consistently with Article nine of the Model and the Transfer Pricing Guidelines of twenty twenty two. Attribution of profits to a permanent establishment must be read consistently with Article seven and the related texts. And the definition of a permanent establishment is tied to Article five of the Model and its Commentary. The version of the Model the statute names is the one approved by the O E C D Council on the eighteenth of November, twenty twenty five. For transfer pricing and attribution, the references follow those texts as they are amended from time to time.

In the exam, the position is simpler. You are given the twenty seventeen articles of the Model as an on-screen document, and a supplied treaty extract beats it.

What this book could not open.

A source note should be honest about its gaps. Here are this book's.

First, the O E C D's own texts. The O E C D's website could not be opened while the book was written. So the book has not read the Transfer Pricing Guidelines, the twenty twenty five Model and Commentary, or the attribution report in their full original form. Where it describes them, it relies on the statute that refers to them, on H M R C's descriptions in its manuals, and on published summaries. It says so where the point matters. A reader who needs the exact words of a Guideline or a Commentary paragraph should go to the O E C D's own publication. The Commentary changes approved in November twenty twenty five, for example, include new paragraphs on when a home office can be a permanent establishment. Those are known here only from summaries.

Second, the examination documents not yet published. As this book was finished, the Chartered Institute of Taxation had published no syllabus grid, no prospectus and no tax tables for twenty twenty seven. Every grade in this book's exam lens boxes comes from the twenty twenty six grid. The comparison between the twenty twenty five and twenty twenty six grids was made against a draft of the twenty twenty five grid, because the final version could not be found. And the transitional rules for students moving to the new qualification in twenty twenty eight had not been published either.

Third, and most important for how much weight to put on any one sentence, the method of checking. For this edition, much of the verification relied on search results that quote primary sources, rather than on reading the full text of each source from start to finish. A search result can show the words of a section, a manual page or a judgment, but it shows an extract, sometimes a truncated one. Where an extract was clear and came from the primary source, the book states the rule as law or as H M R C's view. Where only H M R C's description could be found, and not the statutory text, the book says H M R C's guidance says. Where a point rested on secondary reports, such as figures that commentators gave for the size of a case, the book says so. And where a conclusion is the book's own reasoning from the sources, not a point confirmed by them, the book labels it as this book's reading.

Some examples will help you calibrate. The day on which associated companies are counted for instalments rests on H M R C's manual, because the amended text of the instalment payment regulations was not found. The rule on payments for a surrendered expenditure credit rests on H M R C's description of the new provision, not on its full statutory text. And the three-month window for filing a revised interest restriction return was seen in H M R C's guidance, not in the statutory text itself. None of those points is likely to be wrong. But each is a point to check against the full text, in your own legislation product, before you rely on it for a client.

Finally, everything about Tarnmoor, its people, its numbers and its countries, is invented. Real cases, statutes and dates are real. The invented case never stands in for a real one.

Keeping up after the exam.

Tax law moves on an annual cycle, and once you know the cycle you can keep up without reading everything.

The Budget comes in the autumn. The next is due on the twenty eighth of October, twenty twenty six, and as this note was finished it had not yet happened. Budget day brings the policy papers and the tax information and impact notes for each measure, and the Finance Bill follows. In the summer, usually in July, the government publishes draft clauses for the next Finance Bill, with a technical consultation. This year's draft clauses were published on the thirteenth of July, twenty twenty six. The Bill then passes through Parliament and becomes the Finance Act, often in the spring. Watch each measure's commencement words. Periods beginning on or after a date is not the same as payments made on or after it.

For the law, use legislation dot gov dot uk, and the gov dot uk collection for each Finance Bill. For judgments, use Find Case Law. For H M R C's thinking, use the manuals' update pages and H M R C's consultations.

For commentary, the professional bodies are a good start. The Chartered Institute of Taxation publishes Tax Adviser magazine monthly, with a technical newsdesk section, and sends members a weekly technical email. It also publishes its own submissions and briefings on each Finance Bill. The Tax Faculty of the Institute of Chartered Accountants in England and Wales publishes a weekly newsletter, TAXwire, and a monthly publication, TAXline. The accountancy and law firms publish their own briefings on Budget day and Legislation Day. Use them as maps, then go back to the text.

For the international layer, watch the O E C D's announcements on Pillar Two and on the Model and Guidelines, because the Finance Act twenty twenty six now imports later versions of the Guidelines into transfer pricing automatically, unless the Treasury uses its power to switch an update off. And for the large business relationship with H M R C, read the guidance on gov dot uk about H M R C's large business directorate and its risk reviews.

For the exam itself, check the Institute's pages for the twenty twenty seven grid and tax tables before you sit, and its key dates page for the timetable.

And whatever the source, keep the habits this book has tried to teach. Find the primary text. Check the date it applies from. Say whether a point is law, H M R C's view, disputed, or only proposed. And when a summary arrives from someone else, or from a piece of software, read it the way the new qualification will ask you to, critically, against the source.

That is the end of the book. The law will keep living. You now know where to find it.

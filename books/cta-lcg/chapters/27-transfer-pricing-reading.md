# Chapter 27: Transfer pricing and advance pricing agreements

In GY5, in a meeting room at Calder's works, two members of Tarnmoor's tax team spread a set of engineering drawings across a table. Opposite them sat Dan Hartley. He had left Calder two years earlier, when his division closed, and he had agreed to come back for an afternoon. *Our invented group, Tarnmoor, and everyone in it, Dan included, are invented for teaching. Dan's story began in* The Living Law *(TKS), and it continues here.*

The questions they put to him sounded like engineering questions. Who decided how these valves were made? Where did the know-how live, in the drawings or in people's heads? And when Calder sold its process valve business to Tarnmoor Vallaria SA (TVS), the group's subsidiary in the invented country of Vallaria, what actually went across?

They were really tax questions, and very old ones. When one company in a group sells to another, **what would a stranger have paid?** Calder had sold the business's customer contracts and manufacturing know-how for £6.0m. HMRC had opened an enquiry and was arguing for £9.5m. The answer would turn on facts like the ones Dan could give.

This is the sixth chapter of Part Four. **Transfer pricing and advance pricing agreements are core (grade 1)** on the 2026 LCG grid, and the examiners have tested them at five sittings since May 2023. *The Living Law* signposted transfer pricing but did not teach it, so this chapter starts from first principles and goes to Advanced Technical depth. The prologue's *BlackRock* case gave a first glimpse. For **FY2026** the rules have just been reshaped by **FA 2026 (s 47 and Sch 6)**, and this chapter teaches the reshaped law.

**Statutes in this chapter:** Taxation (International and Other Provisions) Act 2010 (TIOPA 2010, "the International Act"), Part 4 (ss 146–217) and Part 5 (ss 218–230); FA 2026 s 47, Sch 6 and s 48; CTA 2009 s 845; CTA 2010 s 1124; FA 2007 Sch 24; FA 1998 Sch 18 para 23; the Transfer Pricing Records Regulations 2023 (SI 2023/818); the Country-by-Country Reporting Regulations 2016 (SI 2016/237).

## A family sale priced for strangers

Start with the problem. A group is one business. It plans as one, borrows as one and reports to its shareholders as one. But the tax system taxes each company separately, and each country taxes the companies within its reach. So the price at which one group company sells to another decides where the group's profit lands.

Picture a family selling a car to a cousin. The price might be generous, or token, or nothing at all, because the money stays in the family. Nobody minds, unless a third party has a share in what each relative earns. In a multinational group that third party is every tax authority. Underprice a sale from a UK company to a sister in a lower tax country, and profit leaves the UK without anything real having moved.

The answer the world settled on is the **arm's length principle**: each transaction between related parties is priced as if the parties were independent, dealing in comparable circumstances, each able to walk away. **Article 9(1) of the OECD Model** states it for treaties; **Art 9(2)** lets the other state make a **corresponding adjustment** so that the same profit is not taxed twice. The OECD **Transfer Pricing Guidelines** (TPG) explain how to apply it.

The UK rule is **TIOPA 2010 Part 4**. It does not ask why the group chose its price. It asks what strangers would have agreed, and taxes the UK company on that basis if the real price leaves it better off for tax.

**Misconception: "transfer pricing only matters when a tax haven is involved."** It is easy to see why people believe it: the famous scandals involve brass-plate companies on islands. But Part 4 applies between any related parties, in any countries, whenever the UK side gets a potential tax advantage from the actual terms. A sale from a UK company (25%) to a sister taxed at 20% is enough. So is a sale to a sister taxed at 30%, if the price shrinks the UK profit. The rule protects the UK's share; it does not care where the other profit goes.

The figures show how much is at stake. Advisers summarising HMRC's *Transfer pricing and Diverted Profits Tax statistics 2024 to 2025* (published March 2026) report TP yield of about **£3,387m** in 2024-25 (up from £1,786m), with TP enquiries taking on average about **41 months** to settle [secondary: KPMG, EY, Saffery summaries; the gov.uk tables were not opened]. Much of that came from groups whose counterparties were in ordinary tax systems, not havens.

**The policy debate (debate L3).** The arm's length principle treats an integrated group as a set of strangers. Critics say that is a fiction, because a group exists precisely to do things strangers cannot. Defenders say it is the only test two tax authorities can both apply, and that the alternatives (dividing profit by formula) would need a global agreement nobody has. The law, for now, stands with the defenders. Chapter 30 shows how the global minimum tax adds a floor without replacing the principle.

## From abroad, to home, and back again

For years UK transfer pricing (latterly ICTA 1988 Sch 28AA) applied only where one party was outside the UK. **FA 2004 (ss 30–37)** extended it, with thin capitalisation, to UK-to-UK transactions for chargeable periods beginning on or after **1 April 2004** (INTM413250; chapter 2). The usual explanation is EU law: in *Lankhorst-Hohorst* (2002) the Court of Justice held that national thin capitalisation rules could breach freedom of establishment, and applying the rules at home as well as abroad removed the discrimination. **That is the common account, not something the statute says.**

The UK's own earlier rules were then litigated. In the *Test Claimants in the Thin Cap Group Litigation* (Case C-524/04), the Court of Justice gave judgment on **13 March 2007**: the UK thin capitalisation rules restricted freedom of establishment where they targeted lending within groups. The Court of Appeal's decision in the litigation followed in 2011 (*Test Claimants in the Thin Cap Group Litigation v HMRC* [2011] EWCA Civ 127).

So for two decades a loan from one UK company to another had to be priced at arm's length although both paid UK tax. Usually that achieved nothing: if the borrower lost interest, the lender claimed a compensating adjustment (s 174) and the two cancelled out. The **Corporate Tax Roadmap** (October 2024, para 2.48) said that "the potential removal of UK-to-UK transfer pricing ... would reduce the UK compliance burden".

**FA 2026 did it**, with **TIOPA 2010 s 164A**, the UK-to-UK exemption, for chargeable periods commencing on or after **1 January 2026** (FA 2026 Sch 6 para 32(1)). The same Schedule reshaped the rest of Part 4:

| FA 2026 Sch 6 | Change | From |
|---|---|---|
| para 2 | New s 148A: TP notice deeming participation | 1 January 2026 |
| paras 13–15 | New s 161 (acting together in financing), s 162A (common management), s 162B (anti-avoidance); s 162 omitted | 1 January 2026 |
| para 3 | s 151(3)–(4): non-money consideration for intangibles assumed to be money | 1 January 2026 |
| para 4; new ss 153A–153B; s 154 | Guarantees and implicit support rewritten (old ss 152–153 omitted) | staggered (see "Lending to relatives") |

| para 20 | ss 208–211 (Commissioners' sanction) omitted | 18 March 2026 |
| paras 25, 28 | CTA 2009 s 845(4ZA)–(4ZG), s 846: cross-border intangibles transfers subject to TP priced at arm's length, not market value | transfers on or after 1 January 2026 |
| para 29 | s 173A: exchange gains and losses on qualifying financial instruments outside the adjustment | chargeable periods commencing on or after 1 January 2026 |

The same Act replaced DPT with **unassessed transfer pricing profits** (UTPP) inside corporation tax (FA 2026 s 46, Sch 5): chapter 30.

## The basic rule

Slow down here: the basic rule is where marks are won. **TIOPA 2010 s 147** has five building blocks.

| Block | Rule | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Provision | **Provision** made or imposed between two **affected persons** by a **transaction or series** of transactions. Provision = the terms (price, rate, amount, duration, risk allocation). A series includes arrangements, understandings and mutual practices; not every party need be party to every step | s 147(1); s 150 |
| 2. Participation | The **participation condition** is met (next section) | ss 147(1)(b), 148 |
| 3. Arm's length | The actual provision differs from the **arm's length provision**, the terms independent enterprises would have made. The arm's length provision can be **no provision at all** | s 147(1)(d); s 151(2) |
| 4. Advantage | The actual provision confers a **potential advantage in relation to UK taxation**: Effect A, smaller profits; Effect B, larger losses. In deciding advantage, **ignore the CIR (Part 10)** and the s 1000(1)E distribution rule | ss 147(2), 155 |
| 5. Adjustment | The advantaged person's profits and losses are computed as if the arm's length provision had been made; if both are advantaged, both | s 147(3), (5) |

**A one-way street.** Part 4 never reduces UK profits. If a UK company overcharges a foreign sister, the UK simply collects more tax; relief is for the other state. The UK disadvantaged person's relief, where it is within UK tax, comes only by **claim** (s 174; see "Making both sides whole").

**Boundaries in a computation.**
- **Not capital allowances or chargeable gains** (ss 213–214): those regimes have their own market value and connected person rules. Calder's plant sold to TVS for £1.4m was dealt with under the CAA (chapter 8).
- **Exchange gains and losses** on qualifying financial instruments (matched, currency tax offset, Disregard Regulations, fair-value or reference-currency instruments) are outside s 147(3)/(5) (new **s 173A**, FA 2026 Sch 6 para 29; CTA 2009 ss 447, 449–451 and 694 repealed).
- Part 4 applies for **income tax and corporation tax** (s 146).

**Self-assessment.** A company must compute its profits on the arm's length basis **in its return**. HMRC does not have to find the problem first. That is why documentation exists, and why a wrong price can carry a penalty.

**Back to the prologue.** In *BlackRock HoldCo 5, LLC v HMRC* [2024] EWCA Civ 330 (11 April 2024) about $4bn of intra-group loans funded LLC5's purchase of preference shares. HMRC argued that no independent lender would have lent that much on those terms (s 151(2)). The FTT found that an independent lender would have lent, provided it received covenants that would have been given; the UT reversed; the Court of Appeal restored the FTT's finding. **On transfer pricing the taxpayer won.** It lost on the unallowable purpose rule (CTA 2009 s 441; chapter 12).

Commentators have read one passage of Falk LJ's judgment (para 63 is the one cited) as a hint that HMRC argued from the wrong side of the table. Transfer pricing asks what independent **enterprises** would have done, and the borrower is an enterprise too: would an independent borrower in LLC5's position have wanted the debt at all? That argument was not run and the case did not decide it. It is a live question, and a good point in an answer: **test both sides of the deal.**

The prologue's lesson stands. Transfer pricing is a **valuer's** question (what would strangers have agreed?). It never asks why the group did it; that is s 441's job. A loan can pass one test and fail the other.

## Who participates

The **participation condition** (s 148) has two versions.

| Version | Test | Timing |
|---|---|---|
| **Condition B** (ordinary provision) | One party directly or indirectly participates in the management, control or capital of the other, or the same person(s) participate in both | At the time of the provision (s 148(3)) |
| **Condition A** (financing arrangements) | Same test | At the time **or within 6 months after** the provision (s 148(2), (4)) |

**Direct participation** means **control** in the sense of CTA 2010 **s 1124** (power to secure that the company's affairs are conducted in accordance with your wishes, through shares, voting power or constitutional powers) (s 217). **Indirect participation** (ss 157–160, 163) reaches further: potential participants (rights attributed), and each of two persons holding at least **40%** of a joint venture. The six-month window catches a lender who advances money just before taking control of the borrower: the shape of **M23 Q3** (a loan from a US company that might take 60% control).

**FA 2026 additions (from 1 January 2026):**

| Rule | Effect |
|---|---|
| **s 148A** TP notice | Where participation fails only because of the technical rules in ss 157–161 but the parties are participating "having regard to all of the circumstances" (the Art 9 sense), HMRC may give a notice: condition treated as met for that and later periods; limited appeal ground (s 170) |
| **s 161** (substituted) acting together | In financing cases, persons with qualifying (parallel equity) interests are treated as indirectly participating: a private equity club cannot escape by splitting the shares |
| **s 162A** common management | A and B managed by the same person(s) with a mechanism aligning shareholders' economic interests: each treated as controlling the other (stapled structures) |
| **s 162B** anti-avoidance | Arrangements with a main purpose of failing the participation condition are disregarded |

HMRC's guidance (INTM414020) says "the existing conditions for participation at s 148 remain unchanged": the new rules sit around their edges.

**One group, many definitions (chapter 1).** TP participation rests on control and common control, direct or indirect, not on the 75% chains that group relief and gains groups need. A company can be outside a group relief group and a gains group yet inside Part 4.

**In our invented case** participation is never in doubt: TPLC owns every relevant company outright. The real questions are whether an exemption applies, and what the arm's length price is.

## Who is let off

Part 4 has three exemptions.

**1. Dormant companies** (s 165): dormant since 31 March 2004.

**2. The SME exemption** (s 166): s 147(3) and (5) do not apply to a **small or medium-sized enterprise** for the chargeable period. The definition (s 172) is the Annex to **Commission Recommendation 2003/361/EC**, with UK modifications (INTM412080):

| | Staff | Financial limits | Aggregation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Small** | < 50 | turnover **or** balance sheet ≤ **€10m** | linked enterprises in full; partner enterprises (25%–50%) proportionately |
| **Medium** | < 250 | turnover ≤ **€50m** **or** balance sheet ≤ **€43m** | as above |
| UK modifications | | data for the actual chargeable period, annualised; no two-consecutive-years rule; no new-enterprise estimate; liquidators' rights ignored | |

The thresholds are in the CIOT tax tables. **FA 2026 left the exemption unchanged** (the government "decided that small and medium-sized enterprises will continue to benefit from the existing exemption": consultation outcome, 26 November 2025).

**Do not confuse with R&D.** The R&D SME ceilings are doubled (500 staff; €100m / €86m: chapter 10). TP uses the unmodified Recommendation: **fewer than 250 staff**.

**Exceptions:**

| Exception | Rule | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Election | An SME may elect out, irrevocably | s 167(2) |
| Non-qualifying territory | Exemption never applies where the other party (or a party to the series) is resident in a non-qualifying territory (broadly, no treaty with a non-discrimination article) | ss 167(3), 173 |
| Small: TP notice | Only where the provision feeds **patent box** relevant IP profits | s 167A |
| Medium: TP notice | HMRC may give a notice requiring an arm's length computation **only after an enquiry notice**; appeal only on the ground that the company is not medium-sized; return may be amended within **90 days** | ss 168–171 |

> **Worked example 27.2: the size test (invented for illustration)**
>
> | Test (group-wide, with linked enterprises) | Figure | Medium limit | Met? |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Staff | 220 | < 250 | Yes |
> | Turnover | €60m | ≤ €50m | No |
> | Balance sheet | €40m | ≤ €43m | Yes |
>
> Staff test met, and **either** financial test is enough: **medium-sized**, so exempt, unless HMRC gives a TP notice after an enquiry or a counterparty is in a non-qualifying territory.

**The joining-a-group twist.** **N24 Q5** (20 marks) had a US multinational buy a UK SME. The marking guide gave **6.5 marks** for seeing that TP now applied (the target's linked enterprises include the whole group) and for the Master File and Local File contents. **In our invented case**, Brackenwell Sensors (45 staff) was small on its own; from 1 July GY2 its linked enterprises included the whole Tarnmoor group (about 6,000 people) and the exemption fell away. Calder (about 300 staff) never had it.

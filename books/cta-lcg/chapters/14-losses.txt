Chapter fourteen: Losses in the larger company.

On the first of July in Group Year Two, Tarnmoor plc completed its purchase of Brackenwell Sensors. Tarnmoor, its companies, its people and its numbers are invented for teaching, as they are throughout this book. Brackenwell was a young developer of industrial sensors, founded by Dr Asha Varma, and it had never made a profit. In the data room sat a schedule headed tax losses available. The total at the bottom was eight point six million pounds.

At the main rate of twenty five per cent, eight point six million pounds of losses looks like two point one five million pounds of tax that will never have to be paid. The sellers' advisers were happy for the buyer to see it that way. Tarnmoor's group head of tax, Tom Hesketh, wrote two words in the margin of his copy. Value at nil.

This chapter explains why he was right, and what a large group can and cannot do with a loss. It is the last chapter of Part Two. Loss relief, the change in company ownership rules and companies in liquidation or administration are all core topics on the paper. The rule against loss refreshing is non-core. The chapter builds on The Living Law, chapter twenty two, which taught the single-company rules. Here we take them to Advanced Technical depth and into the group.

Most of the law is in the Corporation Tax Act twenty ten, which I will call the twenty ten Act. A few rules sit in the Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, the two thousand and nine Act, and one in the Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act nineteen ninety two, the Gains Act. We apply the law for the financial year twenty twenty six. Finance Act twenty twenty six made no change to the loss rules, the loss restriction or the change in ownership rules, so the framework you learn here is stable.

The twenty seventeen bargain.

Every loss rule in this chapter makes more sense once you see the deal that shaped it. Before April twenty seventeen, a company's carried-forward trading loss was a narrow thing. It could only be set against future profits of the same trade. It could not be used against interest income or gains. It could not be passed to another group company. Group relief was for current-year losses only.

In return for that narrowness, the old rules had no general cap outside banking. A company with a hundred million pounds of old trading losses could wipe out a hundred million pounds of later trading profits in a single year.

The reform that took effect on the first of April, twenty seventeen, legislated in the Finance Number Two Act twenty seventeen, turned both dials at once. Losses arising in accounting periods beginning on or after that date can be carried forward against total profits, not just the same trade, and can be surrendered to group companies in later years. That is the relaxation. The price is the loss restriction. Above a five million pound allowance, carried-forward losses can shelter only half of a company's profits. And the change in ownership rules, which stop people buying losses, were widened at the same time. The window for a major change in a trade grew from three years to five, and for investment businesses to eight.

The logic of the restriction is easy to see. Without it, a large company returning to profit after heavy losses could pay no corporation tax for years. With it, a profitable large company pays some tax every year, while companies whose profits rarely exceed five million pounds are largely untouched.

Think of carried-forward losses as credit sitting in a bank account. After twenty seventeen you can spend that credit in more shops. But the bank now limits how much a large customer can withdraw in any one year. And if the account changes hands and the business behind it changes, the bank freezes the account. Hold on to that picture. The rest of this chapter is the fine print on each of those three features.

For the exam, the reform matters in a practical way. Almost every loss you will meet in a question arose after April twenty seventeen, so it is a flexible loss that can be carried forward against total profits. But it is also a restricted loss, and a fragile one when ownership changes. The examiner tests all three properties together.

Relief now, in the current year and the year before.

The first question for any trading loss is whether it can be used now. Section thirty seven of the twenty ten Act lets a company set a trading loss against its total profits of the same accounting period. Total profits means everything, including trading profits, interest, rents and chargeable gains. If the loss exceeds them, the company can carry the balance back against total profits of accounting periods falling within the previous twelve months.

Three features catch candidates. First, the carry-back comes only after the current-year claim. You cannot skip the loss year and go straight back. Second, the relief is all or nothing within each period. You cannot choose to use only part of the loss to leave profits in charge. Third, where an earlier accounting period falls only partly within the twelve months, its profits are time-apportioned and only the part inside the window can be relieved.

Section thirty seven relief is not touched by the loss restriction. A current-year claim and a twelve-month carry-back are unlimited in amount. That makes them the most valuable reliefs a large company has, and it is why a tax function looks at them first.

There was, for a short time, a temporary three-year carry-back during the pandemic years. It has expired. The examiners reported after the November twenty twenty three sitting that many candidates still thought the temporary rule applied, and lost marks for it. For the financial year twenty twenty six the trading carry-back is twelve months, except in the terminal year, which we come to later.

Other kinds of loss have their own rules, and the differences are a favourite trap. A non-trading loan relationship deficit, which chapter twelve explained, can be set against total profits of the same period. But it can be carried back for twelve months only against non-trading loan relationship profits of the earlier period, not against trading profits. Excess management expenses of a company with investment business, the subject of chapter thirteen, cannot be carried back at all. And a capital loss can never be set against income. It sits only against chargeable gains, now or later.

One more current-year route matters most of all in a group. The loss-making company can surrender its current-year loss as group relief to a profitable company in the same seventy five per cent group, for the overlapping period. Chapter fifteen owns the mechanics. Here, the point is the comparison. Current-year group relief, like a section thirty seven claim, is not restricted by the fifty per cent rule. A pound of loss used now, somewhere in the group, is almost always worth more than a pound carried forward.

Relief later, by carrying forward.

What is not used now is carried forward. For losses of periods beginning on or after the first of April, twenty seventeen, section forty five A of the twenty ten Act lets the company claim to set the carried-forward loss against its total profits of a later period. The claim can be for any amount, so the company can leave some loss in reserve. And unused carried-forward losses can be surrendered to group companies under Part five A, group relief for carried-forward losses, which chapter fifteen explains.

The flexible route has exclusions. Where the trade has become small or negligible, or relief under section thirty seven was not available for the loss in the first place, the loss falls under section forty five B instead. It is then set automatically against later profits of the same trade only. Losses from periods before April twenty seventeen follow the old rule in section forty five, also same trade only, also automatic. The Living Law, chapter twenty two, told the Leekes case about what happens to those losses when trades are combined. You only need the outline here, because nearly every loss you meet will be a post twenty seventeen loss.

The other carried-forward amounts follow the same pattern with small differences. A non-trading loan relationship deficit can be carried forward against total profits, by a claim made within two years of the end of the later period. Surplus management expenses are carried forward as management expenses of the next period, again by claim within two years, and they lose the priority they had in the year they arose. Capital losses carry forward against chargeable gains only.

Now meet the first story use of the carry-forward rules. Helmside Energy, our invented hydrogen joint venture, was formed on the first of January of Group Year One. Tarnmoor plc holds forty five per cent, an invented listed utility called Greyfell holds forty, and an invented infrastructure fund partnership holds fifteen. Helmside made losses in its first three years. Most of each loss went to the two corporate members as consortium relief, which chapter fifteen explains. The fifteen per cent share matching the fund's stake stayed in Helmside, because the fund is a partnership, not a company, and cannot claim consortium relief.

So Helmside carried forward six hundred thousand pounds from Group Year One, four hundred and fifty thousand from Group Year Two and one hundred and fifty thousand from Group Year Three. That is one point two million pounds in all. In Group Year Four Helmside made its first profit, two point five million pounds. It claims to deduct the whole one point two million pounds, leaving taxable total profits of one point three million pounds. Chapter fifteen picks up the story from there, with Tarnmoor surrendering relief down to Helmside against what remains.

Was the claim restricted? No. And the reason takes us to the next rule.

The fifty per cent rule.

Part seven Z A of the twenty ten Act is the loss restriction. It applies only to carried-forward amounts. These are carried-forward trading losses of every kind, carried-forward non-trading loan relationship deficits, carried-forward management expenses, carried-forward property losses, losses claimed as group relief for carried-forward losses, and, since the first of April, twenty twenty, carried-forward capital losses.

The mechanism has a simple heart. Each company, or each group, has a deductions allowance of five million pounds for a twelve month period, reduced proportionately for a shorter one. Carried-forward losses can relieve profits up to the allowance in full. Above the allowance, they can relieve only half of the profits. The statute calls the ceiling the relevant maximum.

H M R C's manual builds the calculation in steps. Start with the company's total profits, with a few adjustments. Deduct the current-year reliefs, such as current-year losses and current-year group relief. What is left is called qualifying profits. Deduct the deductions allowance to get relevant profits. The relevant maximum is the deductions allowance plus fifty per cent of relevant profits. The company keeps separate tallies for trading profits, other income and, from April twenty twenty, chargeable gains, and decides how to divide its allowance between them.

Here is a worked example, invented for the purpose and not part of the Tarnmoor story. Suppose a company has qualifying profits of fourteen million pounds, carried-forward trading losses of twenty million pounds, and the full five million pound allowance. The first five million pounds of profit can be fully relieved. Above that are nine million pounds of profit, and half of that, four point five million pounds, can be relieved. So the company deducts nine point five million pounds of loss. Its taxable total profits are four point five million pounds, its tax at twenty five per cent is one point one two five million pounds, and ten point five million pounds of loss carries forward. The full working is in the reading edition.

Now Helmside makes sense. Its profit of two point five million pounds was below its five million pound allowance, so the whole of its one point two million pounds of losses could be used. Helmside was not a seventy five per cent subsidiary of anyone in Group Year Four. It was owned by a consortium. So it was not part of a group for this purpose, and it had its own five million pound allowance.

Three things are outside the restriction, and the examiner likes all three. Current-year relief, including section thirty seven carry-back and current-year group relief, is unrestricted. Terminal relief for carried-forward losses on a cessation is unrestricted, as we will see shortly. And the restriction defers losses, it does not destroy them. What cannot be used this year simply carries forward.

A common misconception says the fifty per cent rule means a large company can only ever use half its losses. It does not. It means a large company cannot use carried-forward losses to wipe out more than half of its profits above five million pounds in any one year. Given enough years of profit, every pound of loss can still be used.

One allowance for the whole group.

If every company in a group had its own five million pound allowance, groups would simply multiply companies. So the law gives a group one allowance to share. The group deductions allowance is five million pounds a year for the whole group, however many companies it has.

There is a procedure, and the procedure is the trap. The group must nominate one company to administer the allowance. The nomination must be signed on behalf of each company in the group that is within the charge to corporation tax. H M R C's guidance says the nomination does not have to be sent to H M R C, but the group must have one that meets the statutory requirements and be able to produce it. The nomination continues in effect until a new one replaces it, it is revoked in writing, or the nominated company leaves the group.

The nominated company then files a group allowance allocation statement for each of its accounting periods, saying how much of the five million pounds goes to each member. No member can be given more than five million pounds. A member that is allocated nothing has a deductions allowance of nil. And if no statement is filed in time, no company in the group has any allowance at all. H M R C's guidance gives the deadline as the first anniversary of the filing date for the nominated company's return, with later dates where an enquiry or amendment reopens the position.

In our invented case, every U K company in the Tarnmoor group signed a nomination of Tarnmoor plc with effect from the start of Group Year One. As a matter of the group's own practice, Tom Hesketh has each new member countersign it when it joins, so that Calder Valve and Brackenwell are on the document. Each year Tarnmoor plc files the allocation statement, and in Group Years One to Four it allocates the whole five million pounds to Tarnmoor Engineering, the group's main trading company.

Notice the timing. The allocation statement is filed long after the year end, when the group knows which companies actually need the allowance. That is a planning opportunity. Allocate with hindsight, to the company whose carried-forward losses meet profits above five million pounds, and the group can squeeze the most from one shared allowance.

The examiner tested exactly this procedure in May twenty twenty six. A two-company group had carried-forward losses below five million pounds, so there was no restriction in amount. The point being tested was that the allowance still has to be nominated and allocated, because without it the member's allowance is nil. The lesson generalises. Whenever a question has carried-forward losses in a group, say who is nominated and that an allocation statement is needed, even if the numbers are small.

Deficits and expenses in a group.

In a listed group the largest losses are often not trading losses at all. They are the holding company's interest and running costs. Tarnmoor plc, the parent of our invented group, shows how the non-trading rules work in practice.

In Group Year One, Tarnmoor plc had non-trading loan relationship debits of eight point eight million pounds. That was seven point two million pounds of interest to the group treasury company and one point six million pounds of bank interest and fees on the facility that paid for Calder. The corporate interest restriction, in chapter twenty eight, disallowed two point two one million pounds, and the group allocated that disallowance to Tarnmoor plc. That left a non-trading deficit of six point five nine million pounds. Separately, Tarnmoor plc had management expenses of eight million pounds against recharge income of two point one million pounds, leaving excess management expenses of five point nine million pounds.

What could it do with them? Consider the options in the order the examiner expects, as a twenty-mark question in November twenty twenty five asked candidates to do. The deficit could be set against Tarnmoor plc's own total profits of the same year, but there were none left. It could be carried back twelve months, but only against non-trading loan relationship profits, and there were none. It could be carried forward against total profits, by claim. Or it could be surrendered as current-year group relief.

The excess management expenses had fewer routes. They could not be carried back. They could be carried forward as management expenses of the next year, by claim. Or they could be surrendered as current-year group relief, but only so far as they exceeded Tarnmoor plc's own gross profits, which chapter fifteen explains. The deficit, by contrast, can be surrendered in full, whatever the surrendering company's own profits.

One caution about the order of events. The interest restriction comes first. The two point two one million pounds it disallowed is not part of the deficit at all. It carries forward under the interest restriction rules instead, and can be reactivated in a later year, as some of Tarnmoor's was in Group Year Three.

Tarnmoor plc surrendered both amounts to Tarnmoor Engineering, twelve point four nine million pounds in all. The reasoning is the chapter's theme in miniature. Current-year group relief is unrestricted and certain. Carried forward, the same amounts would have met the fifty per cent rule, the claim deadlines, and the risk that a later change in ownership of the group would strand them. A tax function that lets a holding company's deficits pile up has usually missed a group relief claim.

Endings and terminal relief.

When a trade ends, two special reliefs open up, and both are more generous than the ordinary rules.

The first is terminal loss relief under section thirty nine of the twenty ten Act. A loss made in the final twelve months of trading can be carried back against total profits of the three years before, not just twelve months. The logic is plain. A business that has stopped has no future profits for the loss to meet, so the law looks backwards further.

The second is terminal relief for carried-forward losses, in section forty five F. When a company ceases a trade, losses carried forward into the final period and not relieved there can be set against profits of the final period and of accounting periods falling within the three years ending with it. For a post twenty seventeen loss, those are total profits. Three features matter. The relief is unrestricted by the fifty per cent rule, so it can release losses that the restriction held back. It cannot be set against profits of the period in which the loss was made, or any earlier period. And it cannot reach back into any period beginning before the first of April, twenty seventeen. The claim must be made within two years.

There is a limit worth knowing. When a trade is transferred to another company under common ownership, using the rules in Part twenty two of the twenty ten Act that chapter nineteen explains, the transferring company cannot make a terminal claim. The losses pass to the successor instead, and so does the history.

Both reliefs are claims, and both reward planning. A group closing a loss-making company's trade should map the three-year look-back before it fixes the cessation date, because the profits inside that window decide how much relief there is.

Here is a point the Tarnmoor story makes for us. In Group Year Three, Calder Valve closed its process valves division, the closure that cost Dan Hartley his job, as The Living Law told it from his side. Closing a division is not the same thing as ceasing a trade. Whether a company carries on one trade or several is a question of fact. In our invented case, Calder carries on a single engineering trade and continues to trade after the closure. So the closure costs are deductible in the ordinary way, but there is no terminal loss and no terminal relief. A candidate who reaches for section thirty nine whenever something closes will lose marks.

Why buying losses was a business.

Now we come to the frozen bank account, and to Tom Hesketh's two words in the margin.

Imagine a company that has run out of business but kept its tax losses. Its shares are worth almost nothing, except to a buyer with profits. The buyer acquires the shell, feeds profitable activity into it, and the old losses shelter the new profits. The seller gets a price for a corpse. The buyer gets a tax saving. The Exchequer pays for both. That trade in tax shells is what the change in ownership rules exist to stop, and they have existed in some form for decades. Today they live in Part fourteen of the twenty ten Act.

Part fourteen is a family of chapters, each switched on by the same trigger, a change in ownership of the company. Chapter two deals with trading losses. Chapter two A widens the net for losses arising after April twenty seventeen. Chapters two B and two D deal with gains on assets moved into the company after the change. Chapter two C stops pre-acquisition losses being surrendered to the new group. Chapter two E deals with transfers of trade. Chapter three, which chapter thirteen of this book explains, deals with investment companies. Chapter five A deals with shell companies. Chapter six lets H M R C recover unpaid tax from the old owners.

What is a change in ownership? Section seven nineteen gives three conditions. Condition A is met if a single person acquires more than half of the ordinary share capital. Condition B is met if two or more persons each acquire at least five per cent and together acquire more than half. Condition C is met if two or more persons together acquire more than half, ignoring holdings below five per cent unless they top up an existing holding. Indirect ownership counts, so selling a parent company changes the ownership of all its subsidiaries. Other interests, such as voting power, can be brought in where the share capital test would give an odd result.

Two disregards stop the rules biting on pure reorganisations. Inserting a new company between a parent and its subsidiaries is not a change. And since Finance Act twenty fourteen, inserting a new holding company at the very top of a group is disregarded too, provided the shareholdings in the new company mirror those in the old one. For the chapters added in twenty seventeen there is also a wider, group-based trigger. In H M R C's summary, an acquisition that brings the company into a group relationship with another company can itself be a change in ownership for those chapters.

Here is the misconception that this whole part of the chapter exists to correct. When you buy a loss-making company, you do not buy its losses. You buy a company that has losses, which is a different thing. Whether those losses survive depends on what the company does next.

A major change in the nature or conduct of a trade.

Chapter two of Part fourteen is the oldest and still the most examined rule. It applies on a change in ownership of a company in either of two situations. Condition A is that, within any period of five years beginning no more than three years before the change, there is a major change in the nature or conduct of a trade carried on by the company. Condition B is that the change in ownership happens at a time when the trade has become small or negligible, before any significant revival.

Read the window slowly, because it is the part candidates get wrong. It is not five years after the change. It is any five-year period, starting as early as three years before the change. So a major change that the sellers made two years before the sale, to dress the company up, can count. So can a change the buyer makes four years after it.

What is a major change? The statute says it includes a major change in the type of property dealt in, or the services or facilities provided, and a major change in customers, outlets or markets. It also says a major change can be the result of a gradual process. H M R C's Statement of Practice ten of nineteen ninety one sets out H M R C's view of how the test works, and H M R C's manual reports the cases.

Two of those cases help you calibrate. H M R C's manual draws on Purchase versus Tesco Stores for the idea that major means more than significant but less than fundamental. And it reports the case it spells Williams versus Peeters Picture Frames. After a takeover, the company stopped selling direct and sold instead through a group distributor, which sold on to the company's original customers. Looked at in isolation, the Special Commissioners thought that could be a major change in the conduct of the trade. But on the special facts, as the manual reports it, the change was held not to be major. The end customers were the same people. The route to them had changed.

If Chapter two applies, the consequences are severe. Losses made before the change cannot be carried forward against profits arising after it. That covers every carry-forward route, under section forty five, section forty five A and section forty five B. In the other direction, a loss made after the change cannot be carried back to a period before it. Where the change falls in the middle of an accounting period, the period is split into two notional periods at the change, and profits and losses are apportioned between them on a time basis, or on another basis if time would be unjust.

And because the window runs forward, a change that happens years later can disallow losses that have already been used. Chapter eight of Part fourteen extends H M R C's time limits for assessments to let it reach back. The tax function's job does not end at completion.

Brackenwell's eight point six million pounds.

Return now to Brackenwell. Its schedule of losses had three layers. Five point three million pounds came from its years before Tarnmoor's first group year. Seven hundred thousand pounds came from its year that matched Group Year One, after it had surrendered most of that year's loss for a research and development payable credit, as chapter ten explained. And in Group Year Two, Brackenwell's own year to thirty first December, it made a loss of five point two million pounds after its research and development expenditure credit. The change in ownership on the first of July split that year into two notional periods. On a time basis, by months, two point six million pounds of the loss fell before the change and two point six million pounds after it.

So the pre-change losses were five point three million, plus seven hundred thousand, plus two point six million. Eight point six million pounds.

Tarnmoor bought Brackenwell for a reason, and the reason was the problem. The integration plan, agreed before completion, made Brackenwell the group's captive sensor developer and supplier. It would design and make sensors for Calder's and Tarnmoor Engineering's valves, and it would run down its sales to the outside industrial customers it had served for years. Tom Hesketh's team looked at that plan against Chapter two. Brackenwell's customers would change from many unconnected buyers to two group companies. Its markets would change from general industrial sensing to components for the group's own products. Unlike the Peeters case, the end customers would not be the same people. On the group's analysis, that was a major change in the nature or conduct of the trade, and it would happen well inside the five-year window.

The consequence was that none of the eight point six million pounds could be carried forward against Brackenwell's profits after the first of July in Group Year Two. The post-change loss of two point six million pounds for the second half of Group Year Two was a different matter. It arose after the change, so Chapter two did not touch it. It was surrendered as current-year group relief to Tarnmoor Engineering, as chapter fifteen sets out.

Even without the major change, the losses would have been worth little to the group. Chapter two C of Part fourteen stops a company's pre-acquisition losses being surrendered under Part five A to its new group for five years. H M R C's guidance measures the five years from the end of the accounting period in which the change happens. For Brackenwell that period ended on thirty first December of Group Year Two, so the bar runs to the end of Group Year Seven. The losses could only ever have been used against Brackenwell's own profits, and only within the fifty per cent rule. The reading edition works through that hypothetical. With one million pounds of the group allowance and four million pounds of profit, Brackenwell could have used only two point five million pounds of loss in a year.

So the price reflected nothing for the losses, as chapter twenty explains. The group's accounts recognised no deferred tax asset for them, as chapter six explains. Dr Varma, in our invented story, said at the integration meeting that it felt as if the company's history had been written off. In a sense it had. The law treats a company that changes hands and changes course as a new taxpayer for the purpose of its old losses.

The wider net after twenty seventeen.

The twenty seventeen reform made losses more useful, so it also made them more attractive to buy. Parliament responded by adding chapters to Part fourteen. You need their shape, and the priority rule that sorts them.

Chapter two A applies to changes in ownership on or after the first of April, twenty seventeen. It looks for a major change in the business of the company, which is wider than a change in a trade. In H M R C's summary, it includes a major change in scale, the beginning or ending of a trade or business, and a change in the nature of investments, as well as changes in property dealt in, services, customers, outlets and markets. Transfers of a trade or property between companies that were related before the change are ignored. Where it applies, it restricts the use of pre-change carried-forward losses of various kinds against post-change profits, with windows of five or eight years depending on the relief. And here is the priority rule, which H M R C's manual states. Chapter two A gives way where Chapter two or Chapter three can apply instead. Brackenwell's case is therefore a Chapter two case. Its trade changed, and Chapter two does the work.

Chapters two B and two D deal with a familiar trick. After buying a loss company, a group moves an asset with a large built-in gain into it, without tax, under the no gain, no loss rule for groups, and then sells the asset from inside the loss company. If a gain arises within five years of the change, the pre-change losses cannot shelter it, and nor can group relief for carried-forward losses. Chapter seventeen explains the parallel rule for capital losses that a company brings into a group.

Chapter two C, as we have seen, fences pre-acquisition losses off from Part five A surrender for five years. It applies even if the business does not change at all. Chapter two E restricts relief where a trade carrying post twenty seventeen losses is transferred after a change in ownership.

Chapter five A deals with shell companies, companies with no trade, no investment business and no property business. After a change in ownership of a shell, its non-trading loan relationship debits, deficits and certain intangibles losses are restricted. And Chapter six deals with a different abuse. Sellers sometimes stripped a company of its assets, sold it on, and left its corporation tax unpaid. Where tax for a pre-change period is still unpaid more than six months after it was assessed, H M R C can assess a linked person, someone who controlled the company before the change, or a company they control. The assessment must be made within three years of the final determination of the tax.

One more Tarnmoor point shows why timing matters. On the first of March in Group Year Five, Tarnmoor plc bought Greyfell's forty per cent of Helmside and took its holding to eighty five per cent, as chapter eighteen explains. Whether an increase from forty five to eighty five per cent counts as a change in ownership is a question we do not need to answer. Helmside used its last carried-forward loss in Group Year Four. By the time Tarnmoor took control, there was nothing left for Part fourteen to restrict.

Loss refreshing and the purpose rules.

Mechanical rules invite mechanical schemes. So the law has two purpose-based rules aimed at the use of losses in groups.

Part fourteen A, introduced by Finance Act twenty thirteen, deals with the transfer of deductions. It applies after a qualifying change, defined by reference to the allowance-buying rules in the Capital Allowances Act two thousand and one. If, at the date of the change, it was highly likely that certain deductions would be used in a current-year loss claim or group relief, and a main purpose of the change was to achieve that, the deductions cannot be used that way. It also blocks deductions against profits generated by profit transfer arrangements made with that purpose.

Part fourteen B, introduced by Finance Act twenty fifteen, is the one the grid lists as non-core. H M R C's manual describes its target as corporate loss refreshing. The idea of a refresh is to turn old, restricted losses into new, flexible deductions. A group company makes a deductible payment to the loss company. The payer gets a fresh deduction now, usable anywhere in the group. The loss company receives matching income, and its old carried-forward losses absorb it. Nothing has changed economically, except that the losses have moved from the slow lane to the fast one.

Part fourteen B stops the old losses being deducted from the profits of such arrangements. Five conditions must all be met. Condition A is that the profits arise to the company because of the arrangements, and that without the rule the company could set carried-forward losses against them. Condition B is that the company or a connected company gets a deduction it would not otherwise have had. Condition C is that a main purpose of the arrangements is a corporation tax advantage, or an advantage in relation to the controlled foreign company charge. Condition D is that the tax value of the arrangements is reasonably expected to exceed their non-tax value. Condition E is a priority rule for banks.

In Group Year Three of our invented case, a promoter came to Tarnmoor with a scheme it called a loss refresh. Tarnmoor Engineering would pay Brackenwell a large upfront fee for future development work, deductible at once. Brackenwell would book the income and set its old losses against it. To get round the major change, Brackenwell would restart a small external sales channel, so that the promoter could argue the trade had not really changed.

Tom Hesketh's advice to the board turned on two locks, either of which was enough. If the group's analysis was right, the major change had already happened, and nothing done later could undo it. The losses were dead under Chapter two. If the promoter was right and the losses were still alive, Part fourteen B would stop them being set against the scheme's profits. A fee paid only to refresh losses has tax value far above its non-tax value. The board declined. Chapter four explains the disclosure rules and the general anti-abuse rule, which made the scheme even less attractive.

When a company is wound up.

Losses often end in liquidation, and the rules for companies in liquidation or administration are core. Nothing about them has been examined in a standalone question recently, so they are easy marks for the prepared.

Start with accounting periods. When a company goes into liquidation, an accounting period ends immediately before the winding up starts. For a voluntary liquidation, winding up starts when the resolution is passed. For a compulsory one, it starts when the petition is presented, if an order follows. After that, accounting periods run for twelve months at a time until the winding up is complete. Administration works differently. An accounting period ends immediately before the day the company enters administration, and another ends when it leaves.

Rates come next. The liquidator often needs to settle the tax for the final year before Parliament has fixed the rate. The twenty ten Act tells the liquidator to use the rate fixed for that year, or if none, the rate proposed by Budget resolution, or if none, the rate for the year before. The liquidator may also assume a date for the end of the winding up, and if that assumption proves wrong, a new accounting period begins. Repayment interest of two thousand pounds or less in the final period is not taxable.

Now the point the examiner tested in November twenty twenty four, and which most candidates missed. When winding up begins, the company loses beneficial ownership of its assets. The leading authority, Ayerst versus C and K Construction, a nineteen seventies case, is cited by H M R C for that proposition. The assets include shares in subsidiaries. Group relief depends on beneficial ownership of the shares in a seventy five per cent subsidiary. So when an intermediate holding company goes into liquidation, in H M R C's view its subsidiaries stop being part of its group for group relief, and they can no longer be grouped through it with the rest of the group. H M R C's guidance also treats the appointment of an insolvency practitioner as taking control away from the owners and breaking the group relationship. Chapter fifteen explains the arrangements rules that sit behind that view.

For gains, the law takes the opposite view on purpose. Section one seventy, subsection eleven, of the Gains Act says a company does not leave a gains group merely because it, or another group company, is being wound up. H M R C's manual explains why. Without it, winding up an intermediate holding company would drop its subsidiaries out of the gains group, with degrouping charges on the way out.

Here is a labelled example, not part of the Tarnmoor story. Suppose a group's intermediate holding company owns two trading subsidiaries, and the group decides to liquidate the holding company to simplify the structure. From the moment its winding up starts, the two subsidiaries can no longer receive or surrender current-year group relief with the rest of the group through that holding company. But no gains degrouping charge arises just because of the liquidation. The better plan, if losses need to move, is to hive the subsidiaries up to the parent before the liquidation begins.

Administration is different in one respect that matters to groups. Entering administration is not generally thought to strip a company of beneficial ownership, although the facts matter, and the arrangements rules still need checking.

Choosing the claim in a group.

The examiner often asks you to minimise tax, or to minimise long-term costs. In a group, that means choosing among current-year relief, carry-back, group relief, and carry-forward, and choosing where in the group each pound of loss should land.

Start with value. In a small group, a pound of loss saves most tax where it keeps a company's profits below the upper limit, which is why the best answers in May twenty twenty six used group relief to keep a company in the small profits band. In a large group the arithmetic is simpler. The marginal relief limits are divided among each company and its associated companies. Counting that way, the divisor for every Tarnmoor company was nine in Group Year One and ten from Group Year Two, so the upper limit for each company was about twenty seven thousand pounds and then twenty five thousand pounds. Every profitable Tarnmoor company pays at twenty five per cent. A pound of loss is worth twenty five pence wherever it lands.

So the choice turns on four other things. The first is certainty. Current-year relief and carry-back are unrestricted, and losses carried forward face the fifty per cent rule, the change in ownership rules and the risk of a cessation that strands them. Use losses early. The second is cash. For very large companies paying quarterly instalments, expected current-year group relief reduces the instalments due during the year itself, while a carry-back produces cash only after the loss year has ended. The third is the group allowance. Allocate it, with hindsight, where carried-forward losses meet large profits. The fourth is interaction with other regimes. The corporate interest restriction, in chapter twenty eight, can disallow interest and so change which companies have profits to relieve. Deferred tax, in chapter six, depends on whether future profits will absorb carried-forward losses.

For acquisitions, the planning rule is the one Tom Hesketh wrote in the margin. Value a target's losses at nil unless the trade will continue unchanged, and even then discount them for Chapter two C and the fifty per cent rule. Draft the sale agreement so that the price does not depend on losses the buyer's own plans will extinguish. And record the integration plan honestly, because the major change analysis will be read against it.

The tax function's annual tasks follow. Keep the group deductions allowance nomination current, especially as companies join and leave. Diarise the allocation statement deadline. Track the five-year windows for each acquired company. And before any group company is liquidated, check its effect on group relief and on the gains group.

Losses appear in nearly every computation question the examiner sets, so this is where the steady marks are. The reading edition gives the detail of each sitting. Here are the patterns.

In May twenty twenty four, part of a fifteen-mark question on selling a subsidiary asked about losses after a change in ownership. The examiners said it was well answered and that candidates discussed the major change rules, but some wrote about pre-sale loss relief, which was not asked for. Answer the question set.

In November twenty twenty three, a twenty-mark computation ended with loss relief. Many candidates applied the expired three-year carry-back. In November twenty twenty five, a twenty-mark question asked for the options to relieve a new subsidiary's trading losses and non-trading loan relationship deficits. In May twenty twenty six, a twenty-mark group question turned on the five million pound allowance and the nomination. And in November twenty twenty four, a twenty-mark group and consortium relief question included a holding company in liquidation, and the examiners said its impact was missed.

The style is technical prose with computations, at half a mark to a mark per point. Lay out the loss relief in the statutory order, show each claim separately, state the time limit for each claim, and say what carries forward.

What to take away.

Start with the twenty seventeen bargain. Losses of periods beginning on or after the first of April, twenty seventeen, are flexible. They can be carried forward against total profits and surrendered to the group. In exchange, carried-forward losses are restricted, and the change in ownership rules are stricter.

For current-year relief, section thirty seven sets a trading loss against total profits of the same period, then the previous twelve months. It is all or nothing, current year first, and unrestricted. The temporary three-year carry-back has gone. A non-trading loan relationship deficit can be carried back only against non-trading loan relationship profits. Management expenses cannot be carried back. Capital losses never touch income.

For carry-forward, section forty five A gives relief against total profits by claim, for any amount. Section forty five B and the old section forty five keep losses to the same trade, automatically.

The loss restriction gives a deductions allowance of five million pounds a year. Above it, carried-forward losses can relieve only fifty per cent of profits. In our worked example, fourteen million pounds of qualifying profits allowed nine point five million pounds of relief. Current-year relief, carry-back and terminal relief are outside the restriction. In a group there is one five million pound allowance. It needs a nomination signed for every member, and an allocation statement filed by the first anniversary of the nominated company's filing date. Without it, every member's allowance is nil.

On cessation, section thirty nine carries a final-year loss back three years. Section forty five F relieves carried-forward losses against profits of the final three years, unrestricted, but not before April twenty seventeen and not against the loss-making period itself. Closing a division is not ceasing a trade.

A change in ownership means more than half of the ordinary share capital changing hands, under section seven nineteen. Under Chapter two of Part fourteen, a major change in the nature or conduct of the trade within any five-year period beginning up to three years before the change stops pre-change losses being carried forward. Chapter two A widens the net but gives way to Chapters two and three. Chapter two C bars Part five A surrender of pre-acquisition losses for five years. Brackenwell's eight point six million pounds were lost to the major change. Helmside's one point two million pounds were used before Tarnmoor took control.

Part fourteen B stops loss refreshing where the tax value of the arrangements exceeds their non-tax value.

In liquidation, an accounting period ends immediately before winding up starts, and then they run for twelve months at a time. Beneficial ownership is lost, so in H M R C's view group relief through the company ends, but the gains group survives under section one seventy, subsection eleven.

And the question this chapter opened with has its answer. When you buy a loss-making company, you do not buy its losses. You buy a chance to use them, and the law takes that chance away if the business changes.

Losses are only half of the group story. Chapter fifteen, on group relief, consortia and joint ventures, shows how a loss in one company becomes relief in another, and finishes Helmside's story.

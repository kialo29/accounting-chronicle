# Chapter twenty four: Treaties and double tax relief

On **14 April 1926** Winston Churchill, then Chancellor of the Exchequer, signed a short agreement with the government of the Irish Free State; Ernest Blythe (Earnán de Blaghd) signed for the Irish side. Until a few years before, the two countries had been one tax system. Now they were two, and the same people and the same profits were being taxed twice, once in London and once in Dublin. The agreement sought the reciprocal exemption of persons resident in one country but not both, and relief from double taxation for persons resident in both. It would have effect only while legislation confirming it was in force in both countries. One historian of Irish tax describes it as the first international double taxation agreement the United Kingdom ever made.

A century later the UK has one of the largest treaty networks in the world, and a large group's tax function reads treaties every week. Which country may tax a royalty? How much may the paying country withhold? Is a construction project abroad a taxable presence there? And when two countries both tax the same profit, how much of the foreign tax will the UK give back as a credit?

This chapter answers those questions. It sits in Part Four, after residence (chapter 22) and inbound permanent establishments and withholding (chapter 23). Both of its syllabus rows are **core (grade 1)**: "Double tax treaties – application of OECD model and supplied extracts from treaties" and "Double tax relief". *The Living Law* (TKS), chapter 26, taught the basics of credit relief, the source-by-source limit (s 42), the allocation of deductions (s 52), underlying tax and expense relief. Here we read the treaties themselves, then take the credit rules to Advanced Technical depth, with the anti-avoidance and the cases. Law: **FY2026** (Finance Act 2026). FA 2026 made no change to TIOPA 2010 Part 2.

**Statutes in this chapter.** Taxation (International and Other Provisions) Act 2010 (**TIOPA 2010**, "the International Act"): Part 2 (double taxation relief), ss 2–134. Corporation Tax Act 2010 (**CTA 2010**): Part 8A (Patent Box). Corporation Tax Act 2009 (**CTA 2009**): Part 2 Ch 3A (branch exemption) and Part 9A (dividend exemption), signposted only.

## Why two countries tax the same profit

Every country taxes on two footings. It taxes its own **residents** on what they earn, wherever they earn it; and it taxes anyone, resident or not, on what they earn inside its borders (**source**). Each footing is reasonable alone. Put together across a border, they tax the same pound twice.

- **Juridical double taxation:** the same income taxed in the hands of the **same person** by two states. Treaties deal mainly with this.
- **Economic double taxation:** the same profit taxed in the hands of **different persons** (a subsidiary on its profit, the parent on the dividend). It returns below with underlying tax, and in chapter 27 with transfer pricing corresponding adjustments.

The fix was worked out between the wars. In **October 1928** representatives of **27 countries** met under the League of Nations and produced model tax treaties. The line runs from those models to the **OECD Model Tax Convention**, which most UK treaties follow closely.

**Analogy: a peace agreement.** Each side gives up some territory in return for certainty. The source state agrees to tax business profits only through a permanent establishment (PE), and to cap its withholding on dividends, interest and royalties; the residence state agrees to relieve what the source state may still take, by exemption or credit.

**The precise rule.** A treaty **allocates and limits** taxing rights; it **never creates a charge**. If UK law does not tax something, no treaty can make it taxable. Method, always: (1) does domestic law charge the income? (2) does a treaty remove or limit the charge, or require relief?

**In our invented case.** Tarnmoor plc and its group are invented; so are Vallaria and Marrovia (only Ireland, its 12.5% trading rate and the UK–Ireland treaty with the MLI are real).

| Invented country | CT rate | UK treaty | Withholding under the treaty | Other terms |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vallaria | 20% | Yes: modern, OECD pattern | Interest 10%; dividends 15%; royalties 5% | Construction project a PE only if it lasts **more than 12 months**; older dependent agent wording; no MLI Art 12; Art 9 and Art 25 as in the OECD Model; **preamble and principal purpose test** as in MLI Arts 6–7 (story assumption fixed in this chapter) |

## How a treaty reaches a tax return

**TIOPA 2010 s 2:** arrangements made with another territory have effect if specified in an **Order in Council** declaring that they were made with a view to affording relief from double taxation and that it is expedient that they have effect; s 5(2) requires the draft Order to be approved by resolution of the House of Commons. Arrangements have effect in accordance with **s 6** (headed "The effect given by section 2 to double taxation arrangements"). Chapter 2 described that route.

**Interpretation (recap of chapter 2).** *Fowler v HMRC* [2020] UKSC 22: a domestic deeming rule (the diver deemed to trade) did not change which treaty article applied; treaty words take their ordinary international meaning; Lord Briggs said the OECD Commentaries carry persuasive force "as the cogency of their reasoning deserves".

**The Multilateral Instrument (MLI).** In force for the UK from **1 October 2018**. It edits covered treaties, only where both states choose the same options; HMRC publishes **synthesised texts**. The UK–Ireland synthesised text applies MLI Arts 3 (transparent entities), 4 (dual resident entities), 6 (preamble), 7 (principal purpose test), 13 (anti-fragmentation of PE exemptions), 15 (closely related person), 16 (MAP), 17 (corresponding adjustments) and Part VI arbitration; **not Art 12** (commissionaire and dependent agent PEs). Read: treaty → MLI → synthesised text.

**Three practical points.**
1. **Treaty relief is not automatic.** It is claimed, at source from the paying country or from HMRC (HMRC: "The benefit of a double taxation agreement cannot be assumed" in the context of UK payments, INTM342510).
2. A treaty binds only its two parties. The UK–Vallaria treaty limits Vallarian withholding on a royalty paid to a UK company; it says nothing about a payment to Marrovia.
3. **In the exam** the OECD Model is an on-screen PDF of the **2017 articles** (UK statute now refers to the version approved on 18 November 2025 for PE and TP purposes: chapter 2). A **supplied treaty extract is the treaty for that question**. After N23 the examiners reported that some candidates "ignored the supplied treaty article and used the OECD Model text instead".

| 3 | General definitions; undefined terms take their domestic meaning unless the context otherwise requires | — |

**Method for any treaty question:** classify the income → find the governing article → which state may tax, and how much → apply the relief article.

> **Worked example 24.1 (invented story): is Calder's refinery contract a Vallarian PE?**
>
> Calder Valve Engineering Ltd (CVE), acquired by Tarnmoor plc on 1 April GY1 (chapter 1), installs process valves at a Vallarian refinery from **August GY2 to September GY3: 14 months**.
>
> | Step | Analysis |
> |---|---|
> | Domestic law | Calder is UK resident: UK CT on its worldwide profits, including the Vallarian contract (no branch exemption election: chapter 25) |
> | Classify | Business profits from a construction and installation project |

## Who really owns the income

The withholding caps in Arts 10–12 apply only if the recipient is the **beneficial owner**. The concept stops a resident of a third country routing income through a treaty-favoured company.

**The leading English case: *Indofood International Finance Ltd v JP Morgan Chase Bank NA* [2006] EWCA Civ 158.** It was not a tax appeal but a commercial dispute between the issuer of loan notes and the bank, turning on the meaning of a treaty. An Indonesian group raised money through a **Mauritian** issuer, which on-lent the proceeds to the Indonesian parent; the parent guaranteed the notes. Mauritius was chosen for the Indonesia–Mauritius treaty, which reduced Indonesian withholding on interest from **20% to 10%**. The question was whether a newly created **Dutch** company could be interposed to claim the same 10% rate under the Indonesia–Netherlands treaty. The Court of Appeal held that it could not: the Dutch company would be bound to pay on what it received, with no real benefit from the interest; its position would be like that of an administrator of the income. "Beneficial owner" in a treaty has an **international fiscal meaning**, not a narrow domestic legal one.

The court needed no purpose test: it asked who, in substance, enjoyed the income. That is why *Indofood* is described as the forerunner of the principal purpose test.

**In our invented case.** From **GY4** Calder licenses its Ashlar patents to Tarnmoor Vallaria SA (TVS) for **6% of TVS's Ashlar valve sales: £1.2m a year**. Calder developed the invention, owns the patents, keeps the royalty and bears the patent costs: it is the beneficial owner. TVS withholds at the treaty rate of **5% (£60,000 a year)**. The group gives the paying company whatever evidence of UK residence it asks for (for example an HMRC certificate of residence); Vallaria's own procedure is ignored.

## The principal purpose test

Beneficial ownership catches conduits; it does not catch a genuine owner placed where it is mainly to win a treaty benefit. The OECD's BEPS work produced a broader answer, carried into existing treaties by the MLI:

- **MLI Art 6 (preamble):** the treaty is intended to eliminate double taxation without creating opportunities for non-taxation or reduced taxation through evasion or avoidance (including treaty shopping).
- **MLI Art 7 (principal purpose test, PPT):** a benefit is denied if "it is reasonable to conclude, having regard to all relevant facts and circumstances, that obtaining that benefit was one of the principal purposes of any arrangement or transaction" (UK–Ireland synthesised text), unless granting it would accord with the object and purpose of the relevant treaty provisions.

**Why it is sharp.** "One of the principal purposes", not "the main purpose"; tested **per arrangement or transaction**; and "reasonable to conclude" makes the documents decisive. A board paper saying the structure was chosen for the 5% rate is evidence against you.

> **Worked example 24.2 (labelled hypothetical, not a story fact): the PPT**
>
> Suppose the Ashlar patents were moved to Tarnmoor Capital Ltd (TCM) in Marrovia (9%). Marrovia has no treaty with Vallaria in our story, so royalties from TVS would bear full Vallarian domestic withholding. Suppose a planner adds a holding company in a third country with a good Vallarian treaty, to receive the royalty and pass most of it on.
>
> - **Beneficial ownership:** a pass-through company is *Indofood*'s conduit: likely not the beneficial owner.
> - **PPT:** even if the company were given real functions, if obtaining the treaty rate was one of the principal purposes and the treaty's object does not support granting it, the benefit is denied.
>
> The group never proposed this; it illustrates the test only.

Purpose tests run through this book: the GAAR (chapter 4), unallowable purpose (chapter 12), and now treaties.

## Non-discrimination and the mutual agreement procedure

**Art 24 (non-discrimination)** (INTM153260): nationals not taxed more burdensomely than the other state's nationals in the same circumstances; a **PE** not taxed less favourably than a domestic enterprise carrying on the same activities; **foreign-owned enterprises** not subjected to more burdensome taxation than similar domestic enterprises.

***HMRC v FCE Bank plc* [2012] EWCA Civ 1290** (Court of Appeal, appeal dismissed 17 October 2012; earlier [2011] UKUT 420 (TCC)). Under the **pre-2000** group relief rules, a loss could be surrendered only if the group link ran through a **UK-resident** parent. FCE Bank and its UK sister company had a common parent in the **United States**. The court held that Art 24(5) of the **1975 UK–US treaty** prevented the refusal of group relief on that ground. The modern rules let groups be traced through non-resident companies (chapter 15). Exam method: where a supplied treaty has a non-discrimination article, ask whether the UK rule treats a foreign-owned company worse than a domestic one in the same circumstances.

**Art 25 (mutual agreement procedure, MAP)** (INTM153270): where actions of one or both states result, or will result, in taxation not in accordance with the treaty, the taxpayer may ask its own competent authority to intervene, even while domestic remedies remain. In the UK the competent authority is HMRC.

| Point | Rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Time to present a case | OECD Model / MLI Art 16: **3 years** from the first notification of the action; UK domestic fallback **6 years after the end of the chargeable period**, or longer if the treaty allows | MLI (UK–Ireland synthesised text); TIOPA 2010 s 125(3) (HMRC guidance, INTM423040) |
| Giving effect | A solution or mutual agreement is given effect **notwithstanding anything in any enactment** | TIOPA 2010 s 124(2) |
| Form of relief | Discharge, repayment, credit, amended assessment or otherwise | s 124(3) |
| Consequential claims | Out-of-time consequential claims (loss carry-back, group relief) within **12 months** of notification of the agreement | s 124(4) (INTM423070) |
| Failure to agree | INTM153270 still says the treaty has no provision for this: **out of date** where MLI Part VI **arbitration** applies (it does for UK–Ireland) | Law sheet 3; UK–Ireland synthesised text |

**In our invented case.** In **GY6** HMRC and Calder settle the TP dispute on Calder's GY3 sale of its process-valve business to TVS: price re-set at £8.0m, adjustment **£2.0m**. Vallaria had computed TVS's profits on the original price. Under the invented treaty's Arts 9 and 25 the group seeks a **corresponding adjustment**, obtained in **GY7** (chapter 27).

> **Going further: treaty claims as a governance process.** Keep a treaty register: for each cross-border flow, the treaty (synthesised text), the article, the rate claimed, the evidence supplied (residence certificates), the beneficial ownership analysis and the PPT rationale, with the board papers that support it. Diarise MAP time limits from the date any foreign assessment or TP adjustment is first notified, not from when it is paid.

## From treaty to credit

Treaties tell the residence state to relieve double taxation; **TIOPA 2010 Part 2** says how.

| | Credit relief | Expense relief |
|---|---|---|
| How | Foreign tax reduces UK tax on the same income (s 18) | Foreign tax deducted in computing the income (s 112), after an election that credit is not to be given (s 27) |
| Value | £1 of credit saves £1 | £1 of deduction saves at most 25p (main rate) |
| When better | Almost always | Where credit would be useless: e.g. losses, so no UK tax to absorb a credit; the deduction increases the loss |

| | Treaty relief | Unilateral relief |
|---|---|---|
| Source | The treaty's relief article, given effect by s 2 and s 18 | TIOPA 2010 ss 8–17 (s 9 Rule 1 credit; underlying tax rules) |
| When | A treaty applies | No treaty (e.g. **Marrovia**), for foreign tax corresponding to UK income tax or CT |

**Rules that apply to every credit.**
- **Gross up:** income is computed without deduction for the foreign tax (s 31). A royalty of 100 with 10 withheld is taxed at 100, credit 10.
- **Same income:** credit only for foreign tax on the income the UK is taxing. ***Anson v HMRC* [2015] UKSC 44** (1 July 2015; Lord Reed): a UK resident member of a **Delaware LLC** was taxed in the US on his share of profits as they arose; HMRC argued the UK was taxing different income (distributions from a separate entity). The Supreme Court held that he was entitled to the profits as they arose, so the two countries taxed the same income and credit was due. HMRC's Revenue and Customs Brief 15 (2015) treated the decision as fact-specific.
- **Minimisation (s 33):** credit is limited to the foreign tax that would have been payable had the company taken all reasonable steps to minimise it, including claiming treaty reliefs. If TVS withheld above 5% because Calder failed to claim the treaty rate, the excess would not be creditable.
- **Time limits (s 19; INTM162560):** claim within **4 years after the end of the AP** in which the income is taxed or, if later, **1 year after the end of the AP in which the foreign tax is paid**. Where a UK or foreign adjustment makes a credit insufficient or excessive, HMRC's guidance gives **6 years from the adjustment** (s 79; INTM162570).
- **Branch exemption bar (s 18(3A)):** foreign tax on profits exempted by a CTA 2009 s 18A election is not creditable (chapter 25).

## The credit limit, source by source

**TIOPA 2010 s 42:** the credit cannot exceed the CT attributable to the income or gain, computed as **R × IG**:
- **R** = the rate of CT at which the company is charged for the AP, before any credit (25% for a main-rate company in FY2026);
- **IG** = the income or gain, **after deductions allocated to it** under ss 52–56.

**Analogy:** a refund capped at what the UK would have charged on the same slice. The UK will not use UK tax on other income to absorb a foreign rate above its own.

**Source by source.** Each item of foreign income is tested on its own. Onshore pooling of foreign tax on dividends (with "eligible unrelieved foreign tax") existed from 2001 but was **repealed for dividends paid on or after 1 July 2009** (INTM164210), when Part 9A exempted most foreign dividends. Never pool across sources in a current-period answer. The **mixer cap** is different: it is s 58 Step 3, for underlying tax only (below).

**Trade income: s 44.** The credit cannot exceed the CT attributable to the income from **the particular transaction, arrangement or asset**, after a **just and reasonable** apportionment of the trade's deductions, which can include expenses of **connected companies** reasonably attributable to the income (anti-avoidance in ss 45–49). A royalty cannot be presented as cost-free because the research was paid for elsewhere in the group.

**The N25 wrinkle.** In N25 Q1 (overseas royalties under withholding, no treaty), the examiners reported: "Nearly all candidates failed to realise that the royalties can be treated as one source and the foreign tax credits aggregated." **This book could not confirm the statutory reasoning** behind that answer (s 44's "particular transaction, arrangement or asset" points the other way unless the royalties arise under a single arrangement). Treat it as the examiners' approach on those facts: where a question's royalties look like one stream of the same income, consider treating them as one source, state the assumption, and show the aggregated credit.

### The misconception: "foreign tax is always fully credited"

People believe it because treaties promise to "eliminate double taxation", and in the commonest case (a foreign rate below 25%) the credit does cover the foreign tax. But the credit stops at UK tax on that income, source by source; foreign tax above the limit is **lost**, unless it is tax on the profits of a **foreign PE**:

**Unrelieved foreign tax on PE profits (TIOPA 2010 ss 72–78).** HMRC's manual (INTM163040) describes the rule: the excess can be **carried forward without time limit**, or **back to APs beginning in the 3 years before**, but only against UK tax on profits of the **same PE**, and by claim, **within 4 years** after the end of the AP in which the excess arises. Current-period relief comes first; on a carry-back the most recent period is used first. A ceasing PE followed by a new one in the same territory is treated as a separate PE (s 76). Where a foreign PE loss is used abroad against other persons' profits, foreign tax on later PE profits is reduced for credit purposes by the tax saved (ss 71A–71B, FA 2018).

## Calder's refinery

> **Worked example 24.3 (invented story): credit on the Vallarian PE's profits**
>
> Calder's periods: AP to **31 March GY3** (contains August GY2–March GY3, 8 months of the project); then a **9-month AP 1 April–31 December GY3** (April–September GY3, the remaining 6 months).
>
> | £ | AP to 31 Mar GY3 | 9-month AP to 31 Dec GY3 |
> |---|---|---|
> | PE result (UK principles) | (300,000) | 900,000 |
> | Vallarian tax at 20% (Vallarian loss relief ignored: story simplification) | — | 180,000 |
> | UK CT on the PE result at 25% (R × IG) | loss reduces UK profits | 225,000 |
> | Credit: lower of foreign tax and limit | — | **180,000** |
> | UK top-up on the PE profit | — | **45,000** |
>
> Effective rate on the PE profit: 20% + 5% = 25%. Where the foreign rate is below the UK rate, credit relief always lands on the UK rate.
>
> **Lessons.** (1) **IG is measured by UK rules**: the profit the UK is actually taxing. (2) **Order of profits and losses matters**: the UK relieved the early £0.3m loss at 25% and later collected only a top-up. That asymmetry drives the branch exemption decision in chapter 25, where Calder decides **not** to elect.

> **Worked example 24.3B (labelled variation, not a story fact): excess foreign tax on a PE**
>
> Suppose Vallaria had measured the PE profit at **£1,200,000** (refusing a head office charge the UK allows).
>
> | £ | |
> |---|---|
> | Vallarian tax 20% × 1,200,000 | 240,000 |
> | Credit limit (25% × 900,000) | 225,000 |
> | Credit | 225,000 |
> | Unrelieved foreign tax | **15,000** |
>
> Carry forward or back (ss 72–78)? Only against UK tax on the **same PE**. The earlier AP gave a PE loss (no UK tax on PE profits to absorb a carry-back), and the PE ceased in September GY3 (nothing to carry forward to). The £15,000 is **lost**.

>

>
> | £ | With Patent Box | Without Patent Box |
> |---|---|---|
> | Royalty (gross: s 31) | 1,200,000 | 1,200,000 |
> | Attributable costs | (300,000) | (300,000) |
> | Royalty profit (IG) | 900,000 | 900,000 |
> | Patent Box deduction: 900,000 × 15/25 | (540,000) | — |
> | Taxable | 360,000 | 900,000 |

>

>
> | £ | With Patent Box | Without Patent Box |

## Allocating deductions

**TIOPA 2010 s 52(2):** an amount deductible from profits of more than one description may be allocated "in such amounts, and to such of its profits for the period, as it thinks fit". Deductions from total profits (group relief, qualifying charitable donations, losses set against total profits) can be steered. Loan relationship and intangibles amounts have their own sections: earlier periods' non-trading deficits (s 53), non-trading debits (s 54), current-year deficits (s 55), intangibles debits (s 56), with ss 50–51 modifying how UK tax on loan relationship and intangibles profits is computed (headings verified; detailed mechanics not covered here).

**Order:** UK profits first (no foreign tax); then the foreign source with the **lowest foreign rate** (most room between foreign tax and UK tax on it); keep the highest-taxed sources as large as possible.

> **Worked example 24.5 (labelled hypothetical, not a story fact): s 52 allocation**
>
> For this example only, suppose Calder had **not** elected into the Patent Box, and in one AP had: UK trading profits **£500,000**; the TVS royalty profit **£900,000** (withholding **£60,000**); a royalty from an unconnected **Marrovian** licensee of **£500,000** gross (withholding 30% = **£150,000**; profit after its own costs **£400,000**); and **£1,000,000** of group relief and qualifying charitable donations to deduct.
>
> Total profits £1,800,000 − deductions £1,000,000 = TTP **£800,000**; CT at 25% **£200,000**.
>
> | £ | Bad allocation | Pro rata (5/9 of each) | Good allocation |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Deductions set against UK profits | 500,000 | 277,778 | 500,000 |
> | Deductions set against TVS royalty | 100,000 | 500,000 | 500,000 |
> | Deductions set against Marrovian royalty | 400,000 | 222,222 | — |
> | TVS royalty IG | 800,000 | 400,000 | 400,000 |
> | TVS credit: lower of 60,000 and 25% × IG | 60,000 | 60,000 | 60,000 |
> | Marrovian royalty IG | — | 177,778 | 400,000 |
> | Marrovian credit: lower of 150,000 and 25% × IG | — | 44,444 | **100,000** |
> | **Total credit** | 60,000 | 104,444 | **160,000** |
> | **CT payable** (200,000 − credit) | 140,000 | 95,556 | **40,000** |
>
> The good allocation is optimal (checked by search over all allocations in £10,000 steps): the TVS royalty has £660,000 of "free room" (it can fall to £240,000 before 25% × IG drops below £60,000), so it absorbs deductions at no cost. Even so, **£50,000** of Marrovian tax (150,000 − 100,000) is lost: 30% exceeds 25%, and it is not PE tax, so it cannot be carried.

> - **Traps:** s 42 is **not** the mixer; gross up; no pooling (repealed 1 July 2009); **minimisation**; allocate deductions to UK profits first, then the lowest-taxed source; excess credit lost except PE tax (ss 72–78); exempt dividends carry no credit; the Patent Box deduction lowers the limit.

## Dividends and underlying tax

Most foreign dividends received by UK companies are **exempt** (CTA 2009 Part 9A: chapter 25). Underlying tax matters only when a dividend is **taxable**: it falls outside every exempt class or is caught by the anti-avoidance rules; it is deductible for the payer abroad (then **not exempt** and **no underlying tax credit**, s 931D(c), s 57(3): only withholding is creditable); or the recipient elects to tax it (s 931R).

| Rule | Detail | Reference |
|---|---|---|
| Entitlement | Recipient controls, directly or indirectly, at least **10%** of the voting power in the payer | s 14 (unilateral Rule 6); treaty terms similar |
| Amount | Foreign tax on the relevant profits, attributable to the dividend (Steps 1–2) | s 58 |
| **Mixer cap** | Step 3: (D + PA) × M%, where D is the dividend, PA the underlying tax and M the UK rate | s 58; INTM164220 (formula from HMRC, as legislation.gov.uk shows it as an image) |
| Gross up | Dividend grossed up by the **full** underlying tax, **including** any amount disallowed by Step 3 | s 31(2)–(3) |
| Rate boosting | Underlying tax at a rate above the UK rate, included only because of an avoidance scheme, restricted to the relievable rate | ss 67–68 |
| History | Mixer cap from 2001; onshore pooling and EUFT repealed for dividends paid on or after **1 July 2009** | INTM164210 |

> **Worked example 24.6 (labelled hypothetical, not a story fact): a taxed dividend**
>
> Suppose TVS paid TPLC a dividend of **£1,000,000** and TPLC elected under s 931R to have it taxed. TVS's profits were taxed at 20% with no differences.
>
> | £ | |
> |---|---|
> | Dividend | 1,000,000 |
> | Underlying tax: 1,000,000 × 20/80 | 250,000 |
> | Gross income (s 31) | 1,250,000 |
> | UK CT at 25% | 312,500 |
> | Mixer cap on underlying tax: (1,000,000 + 250,000) × 25% | 312,500 → underlying tax allowed 250,000 |
> | Vallarian withholding 15% × 1,000,000 | 150,000 |
> | Total foreign tax (withholding + underlying) | 400,000 |
> | Credit: lower of 400,000 and s 42 limit 312,500 | **312,500** |
> | UK CT payable | **nil** |
> | Foreign tax wasted | **87,500** |
>
> Electing to tax saves no UK tax here. Chapter 25 discusses when the election might still be made.

## Double tax relief anti-avoidance

***Bayfine UK v HMRC* [2011] EWCA Civ 304** (Arden, Pitchford and Tomlinson LJJ, reversing Peter Smith J). A US corporation owned two UK subsidiaries, which entered into forward contracts producing a large profit in one and a matching loss in the other. The transactions had no real commercial purpose beyond exploiting the different treatment of the profit in the two countries. The US treated the profitable subsidiary as a **disregarded entity** and taxed the profit in the parent's hands. In the UK the loss-making company surrendered most of its loss as group relief, and the profitable company claimed DTR of **£35,888,482** under Art 23 of the UK–US treaty for the US tax its parent had paid on the same profit. The Court of Appeal read the treaty purposively: "the primary purposes of the Treaty are ... to eliminate double taxation and ... to prevent the avoidance of taxation". Granting the credit would have let the US tax wipe out UK tax on the profit while the matching loss was relieved in the UK as well. Nor did the UK have to give unilateral relief for tax that had failed to qualify under the treaty.

**The statutory rules: TIOPA 2010 ss 81–88.**

| Condition (s 82) | Content |
|---|---|
| A | Credit for foreign tax would otherwise be allowable |
| B | There is a scheme or arrangement whose main purpose, or one of whose main purposes, is to cause foreign tax to be taken into account |
| C | The scheme is within s 83: one of the types in ss 84–88 (attribution of foreign tax; the effect of paying the foreign tax; deemed foreign tax; claims or elections; reducing a person's tax liability; tax-deductible payments). Underlying-tax schemes are tested as if the overseas company were UK resident |
| D | The total amount of credit claimed (including by connected persons) is more than minimal |

**Effect (s 81, as substituted by FA 2018 s 31):** the effects of the scheme are counteracted by "such adjustments as are necessary", by assessment, modification, amendment or disallowance of a claim. Since FA 2018 the counteraction is **self-executing**: the company must make it in its own return; previously HMRC had to give a counteraction notice (the notice machinery is in ss 89–95; their current status was not checked).

## The tax function, and how the examiner tests it

> **Going further: the treaty and credit cycle (Tom Hesketh's team, in our invented case)**
>
> | When | Task |
> |---|---|
> | Before money moves | Identify the treaty (synthesised text); claim the reduced rate at source; supply residence certificates; document beneficial ownership and commercial purpose (PPT) |
> | When foreign tax is paid | Collect receipts and assessments (credit is for tax actually payable, minimised: s 33) |
> | In the return | Compute credit source by source (s 42, s 44); allocate deductions (s 52); show the Patent Box effect on royalty credits; consider expense relief in loss periods (ss 27, 112) |
> | Deadlines | 4 years after the AP, or 1 year after the AP in which the foreign tax is paid (s 19); 6 years from a later adjustment (s 79); PE excess-tax claims within 4 years (HMRC); MAP within the treaty's period (often 3 years) or 6 years domestically (s 125) |
> | After a TP adjustment | MAP and corresponding adjustment (Arts 9, 25); consequential claims within 12 months of the agreement (s 124(4)) |
>
> **Planning.** Excess foreign tax is a pure cost: reduce source tax by treaty claims; earn income through better-treaty jurisdictions only for genuine commercial reasons (PPT); prefer expense relief in loss years; check that a domestic relief (Patent Box) does not just turn UK tax into wasted foreign credit; never build a structure whose only story is the treaty rate.

**How the examiner tests it.** (1) **Treaty extracts**: apply the supplied article's words (residence, PE, withholding). (2) **DTR inside a computation**: N25 Q1 (unilateral relief on royalties), M25 Q4 (royalties at different rates), M26 Q5 (branch). (3) **Discussion**: explain the limit, allocation, or credit versus exemption.

> **Going further: where DTR meets the other regimes**
>
> | Regime | Interaction | Chapter |
> |---|---|---|
> | Withholding by UK payers | The mirror image: the UK as source state, applying treaty rates (s 911 reasonable belief for royalties; Treaty Passport for interest) | 23 |
> | Branch exemption | Exempt PE profits carry no credit (s 18(3A)); the election is all-or-nothing and irrevocable | 25 |
> | Dividend exemption | Exempt dividends carry no credit; withholding on them is a pure cost | 25 |
> | CFCs | Foreign tax on a CFC's profits reduces the CFC charge as **creditable tax** (a different mechanism from Part 2 credit) | 26 |
> | Transfer pricing | Art 9 corresponding adjustments and MAP remove economic double taxation after an adjustment | 27 |
> | Patent Box | The deduction lowers UK tax on the royalty and so the s 42 limit (CIRD260160) | 11, this chapter |
>
> **Judgement.** A credit computation is only as good as its evidence. Foreign tax receipts, assessments and the treaty claim are working papers an enquiry will ask for, and the credit is for foreign tax actually payable after minimisation.

> **Exam lens: the chapter as a whole**
> - **Grades:** treaties **1**; DTR **1**; Patent Box (the interaction) **3**; branch exemption and Part 9A belong to chapter 25 (**1**).
> - **Style:** "Calculate, with explanations"; DTR computations sit inside CT computations, often as the last steps before CT payable and instalments.
> - **What earns marks:** gross up; R × IG per source; credit = lower; unrelieved amount stated and its fate (lost, or PE carry); a sentence on minimisation; correct allocation of deductions with the reason.
> - **Common waste:** discussing dividend credits for an exempt dividend; quoting the OECD Model when an extract is given; raising DPT (now UTPP, awareness) in a DTR question.

## What to take away

- A treaty **allocates and limits** taxing rights; it **never creates a charge**. It reaches UK law through TIOPA 2010 s 2 and an Order in Council; since **1 October 2018** many treaties are edited by the **MLI**: read the synthesised text.
- **Method:** classify → article → who may tax and how much → relief article. In the exam, use the **supplied extract**.
- Business profits are taxed at source only through a **PE**. Under the invented UK–Vallaria treaty, a construction project counts after **12 months**; Calder's **14-month** refinery contract was a PE.
- Withholding caps depend on **beneficial ownership** (*Indofood*, 2006); the **PPT** denies a benefit where obtaining it was **one of the principal purposes** of an arrangement.
- **Non-discrimination** protects foreign-owned companies (*FCE Bank*, 2012); **MAP** resolves taxation not in accordance with a treaty; **arbitration** backs it where the MLI applies.
- DTR is usually a **credit**, by treaty or **unilaterally**; income is **grossed up**; the foreign tax must be on the **same income** (*Anson*, 2015) and **minimised** (s 33); claims within **4 years** (or 1 year after the AP of payment).
- **Credit ≤ R × IG, source by source** (s 42); for trade income, per **transaction, arrangement or asset** (s 44). Excess is **lost**, except PE tax (forward indefinitely, or back 3 years, same PE).
- **Calder:** refinery PE profit **£900,000**, Vallarian tax **£180,000** fully credited, UK top-up **£45,000**; TVS royalty **£1.2m**, withholding **£60,000** fully credited although the Patent Box cuts UK CT on it to **£90,000**.
- **Allocate deductions** to UK profits first, then the lowest-taxed source (worth **£100,000** in the labelled example).
- **Underlying tax** only for taxable dividends; 10% holding; gross up by the full underlying tax; **mixer cap (D + PA) × M%**; pooling ended **1 July 2009**.
- **Anti-avoidance:** *Bayfine* (2011) read the treaty purposively; **ss 81–88** counteract schemes automatically since FA 2018.

A credit is one way to relieve foreign profits. Chapter 25, on going abroad through a branch or a subsidiary, looks at the other way, exemption, and asks when a group should choose it.

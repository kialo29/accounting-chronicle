Part Three: The Group.

Chapter fifteen: Group relief, consortia and joint ventures.

On the twenty seventh of October, twenty twenty one, a Budget day, a set of rules written to satisfy European law quietly stopped working. For more than a decade, a United Kingdom company had been able, in narrow circumstances, to claim relief for the losses of a subsidiary resident elsewhere in the European Economic Area. Those rules were the long shadow of the Marks and Spencer litigation, which The Living Law, chapter twenty four, told. The Finance Act twenty twenty two repealed them, with effect from that Budget day. In substance, H M R C's manual now says that relief for the overseas losses of group companies resident in the European Union, for periods after the twenty sixth of October, twenty twenty one, is no longer available.

So the border is back where it began. Losses move between companies inside the United Kingdom tax net, and almost nowhere else. And even inside it, they do not flow freely. They move one company at a time, by claim and by consent, measured period by period, and they stop dead when the ownership tests fail or when someone has agreed to sell.

This chapter asks a simple question. A group plans, borrows and reports as one business. Why does the law make its losses travel company by company, and what decides how far each one can go?

This is the first chapter of Part Three, on the group. Group relief, including consortium relief, is a core topic for this paper. So are joint ventures, companies as partners, and the group relief rules for non-resident and dual resident companies. The chapter builds on The Living Law, chapter twenty four, which taught the seventy five per cent test and the basics. Here we take group and consortium relief to Advanced Technical depth.

Most of the law is in the Corporation Tax Act twenty ten, which I will call the twenty ten Act, mainly in Part five. Some rules sit in the Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, the two thousand and nine Act. Claims are governed by the company return rules in Schedule eighteen to the Finance Act nineteen ninety eight. We apply the law for the financial year twenty twenty six.

Why relief, and not one tax return.

The Living Law, chapter twenty four, made the basic point. The United Kingdom taxes each company separately. There is no consolidated return. A parent with fifty subsidiaries files fifty one returns, and each company pays its own tax.

Some countries let a group file as one taxpayer. The United Kingdom chose a different bridge. Group relief lets one company surrender a current-year loss to another company in the same group, which claims it against its own profits. The effect looks like consolidation, but only for the amounts surrendered, only for the periods that overlap, and only between companies that pass the tests.

Picture a building with a separate water tank for every flat. Group relief is a set of pipes between the tanks. Each pipe has a valve, a gauge and a rule about which tanks it may join. The tanks are never merged.

That design has three consequences that run through this chapter. First, relief is always a claim, never automatic. Someone must make it, with consent, on time. Second, the amount is always the lower of two things. What the surrendering company can give, and what the claimant can take. Third, the group must exist, in the statutory sense, throughout the period being measured. If it stops existing, even on paper, the pipe closes.

The choice is a live debate. One side says the group is the real economic unit, so tax should follow the consolidated result, and the modern rules on interest and the global minimum tax already look at the worldwide group. The other side says separate companies are separate legal persons, with their own creditors, minorities and regulators, and that a claim-based bridge keeps the Exchequer's protections company by company. The United Kingdom has kept the second model for losses while borrowing the first for some anti-avoidance rules. This book returns to that tension at the end.

Here is the misconception to drop now. A group does not pay tax as one. It pays tax as many companies, some of whom have agreed to pass relief to others. That distinction decides every exam answer in this area.

Our invented group, Tarnmoor, shows the scale. Tarnmoor plc, the listed parent, sits above a set of trading, property and finance companies. Its companies, people and numbers are invented for teaching, as they are throughout this book. In Group Year Two, Tarnmoor Engineering, the main trading company, claimed seventeen point oh nine five million pounds of group and consortium relief from three different sources. Each source had its own limit. This chapter takes them one at a time.

Who is in the group.

For group relief, two companies are in the same group if one is the seventy five per cent subsidiary of the other, or both are seventy five per cent subsidiaries of a third company. The holding can be direct or indirect, traced through the chain.

Ordinary share capital is only the first test. Section one hundred and fifty one of the twenty ten Act adds two more. The parent must be beneficially entitled to at least seventy five per cent of the profits available for distribution to equity holders, and to at least seventy five per cent of the assets available to them on a winding up. Equity holders are defined in Chapter six of Part five. In outline, they are ordinary shareholders and lenders whose loans are not normal commercial loans. The point of the extra tests is to stop a parent holding seventy five per cent of the shares on paper while the real economic interest sits with someone else, through preference shares or special loans. The examiners tested exactly this in November twenty twenty four, with preference shares in the facts.

Indirect holdings are multiplied through the chain. If a parent owns ninety per cent of a company, which owns eighty per cent of another, the parent's indirect share of the second company is seventy two per cent. That is below seventy five, so the parent and the bottom company are not grouped, although each is grouped with the company in the middle. Questions love that sum, because candidates add instead of multiplying, or stop at the first link.

The word beneficially matters too. Chapter fourteen showed that a company in liquidation loses beneficial ownership of its shares, and with it, in H M R C's view, the group relationship through it.

The group can be traced through companies that are not resident here. Tarnmoor's Vallarian and Marrovian subsidiaries, both in invented countries, can sit in a chain without breaking it. But relief only passes between companies that are U K related. A U K related company is one resident in the United Kingdom, or a non-resident company carrying on a trade here through a permanent establishment.

The tracing rule has a story behind it. In H M R C versus F C E Bank, decided by the Court of Appeal in twenty twelve, two U K companies had a common parent in the United States. Under the law as it then stood, that parent's non-residence stopped them being grouped. The Court of Appeal held that the non-discrimination article of the United Kingdom and United States treaty required relief to be allowed. Treaties, it turned out, could reach into group relief. Chapter twenty four returns to that article.

In our invented case, every Tarnmoor company is wholly owned, so the share, profit and asset tests are easy. Tarnmoor Ireland, though incorporated in Ireland, is U K resident while its board meets in Leeds, so it can surrender and claim like any other U K company until it migrates in Group Year Four. Tarnmoor Vallaria and Tarnmoor Capital are outside the charge, so their losses stay abroad. The dormant pumps company is in the group but has nothing to give. And Helmside Energy, the hydrogen joint venture, is not in the group at all. Tarnmoor owns forty five per cent. Helmside is a consortium company, and it has its own rules, which come later.

What can be surrendered.

Section ninety nine of the twenty ten Act lists what a company can surrender for an accounting period. There are seven items, and they fall into two families.

The first family can be surrendered in full, whatever the surrendering company's own profits. These are trading losses, excess capital allowances, and non-trading loan relationship deficits. A company with trading losses does not have to use them against its own interest income first. It can surrender the whole loss and pay tax on its other profits. That freedom is deliberate and useful.

The second family can only be surrendered so far as it exceeds a threshold. These are excess qualifying charitable donations, losses of a U K property business, management expenses, and non-trading losses on intangible fixed assets. Section one hundred and five sets the threshold. The law calls it the profit-related threshold, and these four items can be surrendered only to the extent that, together, they exceed it.

Two more features of the surrendering side catch candidates. The surrendering company can choose how much to surrender, and to whom. It can split one loss between several claimants, as long as the total does not exceed what it has. And excess charitable donations are a special case. A trading company cannot carry them forward at all, so if they are not surrendered in the year, they are lost.

For most companies the threshold is simply the company's gross profits of the period. That is its profits before deducting those reliefs and any amounts from other periods. The logic is fairness to the Exchequer. A company should use its own management expenses against its own income before passing the surplus to someone else.

Since twenty thirteen, the threshold has a second component, and Tarnmoor is the perfect example. The threshold also includes any controlled foreign company profits apportioned to the company. Tarnmoor plc is apportioned eight hundred and twenty five thousand pounds a year of its Marrovian finance company's profits, as chapter thirteen explained.

So run the numbers for Group Year One. Tarnmoor plc had management expenses of eight million pounds. Its only taxable income was a recharge to its Vallarian subsidiary of two point one million pounds. The surplus over gross profits was five point nine million pounds. But the threshold is two point one million plus eight hundred and twenty five thousand, which is two point nine two five million pounds. So only five point oh seven five million pounds could be surrendered. Eight hundred and twenty five thousand pounds was carried forward.

The parent's non-trading loan relationship deficit, six point five nine million pounds after the corporate interest restriction, belongs to the first family. It went across in full. Notice the order of events. The corporate interest restriction comes first. The two point two one million pounds of interest it disallowed is not part of the deficit at all. It is carried forward under the interest restriction rules, and it can never be surrendered as group relief. In total, Tarnmoor plc surrendered eleven point six six five million pounds to Tarnmoor Engineering in Group Year One, and thirteen point one four five million pounds in Group Year Two.

The carried-forward eight hundred and twenty five thousand pounds a year is the sting. On the book's reading of the rules for carried-forward losses, the parent cannot pass it to the group later while its own recharge income could absorb it, and using it against that income only displaces current expenses that the threshold then traps. So in our invented case those expenses are stranded. By the end of Group Year Four they total three point three million pounds, with no deferred tax asset recognised.

How much the claimant can take.

The surrendering company's side is only half the sum. The claimant's side is the other half.

Section one hundred and thirty eight of the twenty ten Act limits group relief to the lower of two figures. One is the unused part of the surrenderable amount for the overlapping period. The other is the unused part of the claimant's available total profits for that period.

Available total profits are the claimant's total profits after its own reliefs. The idea is that the claimant uses its own losses first. Relief from another company comes last, after the claimant's own current-year losses, its carried-forward losses, and its qualifying charitable donations. That is why group relief sits at the very bottom of the computation, just above taxable total profits.

Take Tarnmoor Engineering in Group Year Two. Its trading profits, after capital allowances, were twenty six million pounds. It had no other income and no losses of its own. Against that it claimed group relief from three places. From the parent, five point four seven five million pounds of management expenses and seven point six seven million pounds of deficit. From Brackenwell Sensors, the loss-making company the group bought halfway through the year, two point six million pounds. And from Helmside, through the consortium rules, one point three five million pounds.

The total was seventeen point oh nine five million pounds. Taxable total profits fell to eight point nine oh five million pounds, and corporation tax at twenty five per cent was two million, two hundred and twenty six thousand, two hundred and fifty pounds.

Two practical points follow. First, group relief changes cash during the year. Tarnmoor Engineering is a very large company, paying instalments in months three, six, nine and twelve of its year. Its instalments must reflect its expected liability after the group relief it expects to claim. Getting the group relief forecast wrong means interest. Chapter three covered that.

Second, do not confuse group relief with other things that reduce tax. In the same year Brackenwell also surrendered six hundred thousand pounds of research and development expenditure credit to Tarnmoor Engineering. That credit discharges part of the liability once claimed. It is not group relief, and it does not reduce the instalments, which are based on the full liability. After the credit, the engineering company's net tax for the year was one million, six hundred and twenty six thousand, two hundred and fifty pounds. Keep the two regimes in separate boxes.

When the years do not match.

Group companies often have different year ends, and companies join and leave part of the way through a year. The law deals with both through one idea, the overlapping period.

The overlapping period is the time common to the surrendering company's accounting period and the claimant's accounting period. Section one hundred and forty two of the twenty ten Act then removes from it any part during which the group condition is not met. Relief is measured only for what is left. Both companies' figures are apportioned to that period, by time unless that would be unjust or unreasonable, which is H M R C's approach in its manual.

Then the lower of the two figures applies, as before. Where a claimant takes relief from several companies for overlapping periods that cover the same months, the statute stops the same slice of profit being relieved twice. Tarnmoor Engineering shows how that check works. For the six months from the first of July in Group Year Two, its available total profits were thirteen million pounds, half of twenty six million. The parent's and Helmside's claims covered the whole year, so half of each counts against those six months, about seven point two five million pounds. That left about five point seven five million pounds of room, comfortably more than Brackenwell's two point six million.

Brackenwell shows the joining rule. Tarnmoor signed a conditional contract to buy it on the first of May in Group Year Two, and completed on the first of July. On the usual analysis, the group relationship began at completion, when Tarnmoor became the beneficial owner of the shares, not when the conditional contract was signed. Signing a contract whose conditions are still outstanding does not, by itself, pass beneficial ownership. Brackenwell kept its thirty first of December year end. Its loss for the year, after its expenditure credit, was five point two million pounds. The overlapping period with Tarnmoor Engineering's year was only the six months from the first of July. So two point six million pounds was available to surrender. The other two point six million pounds belonged to Brackenwell's life before the group, and chapter fourteen explained why it was lost.

Now a labelled hypothetical, to show non-matching year ends. For this example, suppose Calder Valve, whose year then ran to the thirty first of March, had claimed Brackenwell's loss instead. Calder's year from the first of April in Group Year Two overlaps Brackenwell's year for nine months, but the group condition is met for only six of them. Suppose Calder's available total profits for its year were four million pounds. Six twelfths of that is two million pounds. Brackenwell could offer two point six million pounds for those six months. Relief is the lower figure, two million pounds.

The lesson is mechanical but vital. Always draw the time line. Mark each company's accounting period, the date the group condition starts and stops, and the overlap. Most errors in group relief questions are errors of dates, and the examiners said so in plain terms after November twenty twenty four. Wrong joining and leaving dates were the first item in their list.

Arrangements, and the day a group ends early.

If groups ended only at completion, a seller could agree to sell a loss-making subsidiary, then keep its losses flowing to the rest of the group right up to the last day. The buyer's group might be lining up to use them too. The arrangements rules stop that.

Section one hundred and fifty four of the twenty ten Act treats two companies as not being in the same group if arrangements exist with certain effects. The first effect is that one company could leave the group and join the group of a third company. The second is that some person could obtain control of one company but not the other. There are others. Arrangements means arrangements of any kind, written or not, and they need no tax purpose. A simple, commercial sale of a subsidiary is the standard example in H M R C's manual.

When do arrangements begin? H M R C's view is that it is a question of fact, usually the point at which the parties agree in principle that the deal will proceed, even if that agreement is still subject to contract. Its manual gives a worked example in which terms agreed subject to contract on one date, and an unconditional contract signed a month later, produce arrangements from the earlier date. Arrangements can start earlier still, for example where one side has been given an option.

The group relationship is broken only while the arrangements exist, but that is enough. The overlapping period shrinks to the time before the arrangements began.

The Living Law told the case that shows how wide the second effect reaches. In Farnborough Airport Properties Company versus H M R C, decided by the Court of Appeal on the eighth of February, twenty nineteen, a receiver was appointed over the whole property of a loss-making group company under an existing debenture. The court held that the appointment was an arrangement under which the shareholders lost control. Group relief for losses of about ten point six million pounds was denied.

Not every arrangement counts. Sections one hundred and fifty five A and one hundred and fifty five B, for arrangements made in accounting periods beginning on or after the first of March, twenty twelve, exclude certain ordinary joint venture provisions. These are share transfer clauses or suspended voting rights triggered by events such as a member leaving, becoming insolvent, changing control or defaulting, or a deadlock between members. Without that safe harbour, almost every joint venture agreement would break relief from the start.

Notice the asymmetry. Arrangements can end a group relationship early, but they never start one early. A company joins a group only when the ownership tests are actually met. So an acquisition delays relief on the way in and can cut it off on the way out. The examiners tested both directions in November twenty twenty four.

For the tax function, the lesson is to diarise the deal timetable, not just the completion date. When Tarnmoor later sells its actuators company, in chapter twenty, the date that matters for group relief is the day the deal is agreed in principle.

Claims, consents and payments.

Group relief is made by claim, in the claimant's company tax return, under the company return rules in Schedule eighteen to the Finance Act nineteen ninety eight.

The surrendering company must give a notice of consent, in writing, to H M R C, at or before the time of the claim, and a copy goes with the claim. A notice of consent cannot be amended. If the figures change, the surrendering company withdraws the old notice and gives a new one. For a consortium claim, every member of the consortium must also consent, whichever way the relief is flowing. A consent, like a claim, is given company by company and period by period, so in a group of forty companies the paperwork is real, and a missing consent can sink an otherwise perfect claim.

A claim must be for a stated amount, and it can be for less than the maximum. That flexibility is useful. A group can leave relief in a company where it is worth more, or hold back while an enquiry runs. And a claim can be withdrawn and replaced while the time limit lasts.

The time limit is generous. A claim can be made or withdrawn up to the latest of four dates. These are the first anniversary of the claimant's filing date, and thirty days after the end of an enquiry, after an amendment following an enquiry, or after the final determination of an appeal against that amendment. Large groups often use a simplified arrangement under regulations, in which one authorised company makes the claims for the group without sending copies of every notice.

Then there is the money. A loss surrendered by one subsidiary saves tax for another, and a group with minority shareholders, or a careful board, wants the claimant to pay for it. Section one hundred and eighty three of the twenty ten Act deals with this. Where the two companies agree, and the claimant pays the surrendering company an amount not exceeding the amount surrendered, the payment is ignored in computing both companies' profits and is not a distribution. The limit is the amount of loss, not the tax saved. In practice most groups pay the tax value, twenty five pence for each pound, but a full pound for each pound would still be within the rule.

Contrast the expenditure credit payment. When Tarnmoor Engineering paid Brackenwell six hundred thousand pounds for the credit it surrendered, a different rule applied, a new one in the Finance Act twenty twenty six, as H M R C's guidance describes it. Chapter ten covered it.

Losses brought forward, and the group.

Before April twenty seventeen, group relief was for current-year amounts only. Since then, Part five A of the twenty ten Act has let companies surrender losses carried forward from periods beginning on or after the first of April, twenty seventeen. That includes carried-forward trading losses, deficits, management expenses and property losses.

Part five A is stricter than current-year relief, in four ways. First, the surrendering company generally must use carried-forward losses against its own profits before surrendering them. H M R C's manual says a company cannot surrender carried-forward losses under Part five A if it could have deducted them from its own total profits. Second, the claimant must first use its own carried-forward losses. Third, the claimant's relief falls inside the loss restriction of chapter fourteen, with the group's single five million pound allowance and fifty per cent above it. Fourth, pre-acquisition losses of a company that joins the group cannot be surrendered for five years. Chapter fourteen covered that bar for Brackenwell, whose losses died anyway.

Think of current-year relief as the main door between the tanks. Part five A is a second door, and it opens only after each company has first used its own carried-forward losses. That design keeps the twenty seventeen bargain intact. Losses became more flexible, but the loss restriction still caps how much old loss can shelter new profit, wherever in the group it lands. For the exam, Part five A matters most as a contrast. Current-year relief is unlimited and immediate. Carried-forward relief is conditional, capped and slow.

Payments for carried-forward relief follow the same rule as current-year payments. H M R C's manual confirms that a payment up to the amount of the loss is disregarded.

The first of those rules is why Tarnmoor plc's eight hundred and twenty five thousand pounds a year is stranded. The parent always has recharge income that the carried-forward expenses could cover, so it cannot route them to Tarnmoor Engineering as Part five A relief. Using them itself only pushes out current-year expenses, which the threshold then traps again. That is the book's reading of how the rules fit together, and it is not tested in any decided case.

Part five A also reaches consortia. Carried-forward losses can pass between a consortium company and its members, with the same ownership limits as current-year relief. The exam has so far tested current-year relief far more heavily, so that is where the rest of this chapter goes.

Non-residents, branches and dual residents.

Three groups of rules decide whether a company with a foot abroad can take part.

First, the permanent establishment. A non-resident company trading here through a permanent establishment is U K related, so it can surrender and claim. But, in outline, it can surrender only losses of its U K permanent establishment activities that cannot be relieved against profits taxed abroad. The United Kingdom will not give relief for a loss that another country is also relieving.

Second, the European rules. The repeal described at the start of this chapter took effect on the twenty seventh of October, twenty twenty one. Before then, a U K parent could, in narrow cases where every possibility of relief abroad was exhausted, claim the losses of a subsidiary resident in the European Economic Area. That door is now closed for later losses.

Third, the dual resident investing company. Section one hundred and nine of the twenty ten Act bars a company that is U K resident but also within another country's tax charge, by residence, incorporation or management, from surrendering if, in outline, it is not a trading company, or its trade is mainly holding shares or financing. The fear is double dipping, a single interest cost relieved in two countries. The same company cannot receive assets at no gain, no loss, as chapter seventeen will show. The examiners reported after November twenty twenty four that many candidates missed this bar.

In an exam the point usually hides in the facts. A company incorporated abroad whose board meets in London, or a U K company whose directors meet abroad. Ask three questions in turn. Is it U K resident? Is it also within another country's charge? Does it carry on a real trade, other than holding shares or lending? Only yes, yes and no leads to the bar.

Tarnmoor Ireland is the near miss. Before migrating, it was U K resident with an Irish incorporation, and the two tax authorities had agreed it was resident here for treaty purposes. Even if Ireland had also treated it as resident, the bar would not have bitten, because Tarnmoor Ireland trades. After the thirtieth of June in Group Year Four it is resident in Ireland, and since it has no U K permanent establishment, it is no longer U K related. From then on it neither surrenders nor claims.

Consortia, and the four tape measures.

Joint ventures between groups are everywhere in infrastructure, energy and property. Two or three companies each put in capital, and none controls the venture. None of them owns seventy five per cent, so group relief cannot work. Consortium relief fills that gap.

Section one hundred and fifty three of the twenty ten Act defines the consortium. A company is owned by a consortium if it is not a seventy five per cent subsidiary of any company, and at least seventy five per cent of its ordinary share capital is beneficially owned by companies each owning at least five per cent. Those companies are the members. A trading company that is a ninety per cent subsidiary of a consortium-owned holding company also counts.

Relief can flow in both directions. Under section one hundred and thirty two, consortium condition one, the consortium company surrenders to a member, or a member surrenders to the consortium company. The consortium company must be a trading company or a holding company, both companies must be U K related, and there is an exclusion where a profit on selling the member's shares would be a trading receipt.

Two refinements matter in practice. A consortium company may itself head a group of its own. Then, in outline, relief that its own group could give must be taken into account first, before consortium relief flows to the members. And a member that is itself in a group can pass relief through its group by the link company route, which we meet in the next section.

Now meet Helmside Energy, our invented hydrogen joint venture, formed on the first of January in Group Year One. Tarnmoor plc owns forty five per cent. Greyfell Utilities, an invented listed utility, owns forty per cent. Northlight Infrastructure Fund, an invented limited partnership, owns fifteen per cent. Northlight is not a company. So companies own eighty five per cent, which is at least seventy five, and each owns at least five. Helmside is consortium-owned, with two members, Tarnmoor and Greyfell. It trades from the start, as a maker of hydrogen plant, which matters because a consortium company must be trading or a holding company.

How much can each member take? Sections one hundred and forty three and one hundred and forty four limit relief to the member's ownership proportion. That is the lowest of four measures. Its share of the ordinary share capital, its share of the profits available for distribution, its share of the assets on a winding up, and its share of the voting power. If any of them changes during the period, the proportion is averaged.

Think of the consortium as a shared allotment. Each member can take produce only in proportion to its plot. But the plot is measured with four tape measures, and the smallest reading wins.

So here is this chapter's second misconception. A consortium member does not take losses in proportion to its shares. It takes them in proportion to the lowest of four ratios, one of which is voting power, and only against its own available profits. In our invented case, Helmside's articles give every share the same rights, so all four measures match the shareholdings, forty five per cent for Tarnmoor and forty per cent for Greyfell.

Relief up, and relief down.

Start with losses flowing up. Helmside lost four million pounds in Group Year One, three million in Group Year Two and one million in Group Year Three. It had no other income, so there was nothing it could set the losses against itself. That matters, because when a consortium company surrenders, its loss is first reduced by what it could relieve under its own current-year claim. H M R C's manual gives an example in which a consortium company's loss is partly covered by its own gains, leaving less to share.

Why should Northlight's share be lost? Because consortium relief is relief between companies within the charge to corporation tax. Northlight's own investors may be individuals, pension funds or overseas institutions, and the relief has no home in their hands. So the law gives each corporate member its fair slice, and leaves the rest where it fell.

Tarnmoor's forty five per cent of four million pounds is one point eight million pounds. Greyfell's forty per cent is one point six million. The fifteen per cent that matches Northlight's stake, six hundred thousand pounds, cannot go anywhere. It stays in Helmside and is carried forward.

But Tarnmoor plc, the member, had no taxable profits. Its own management expenses and deficits already covered its income. The relief was useful only to Tarnmoor Engineering. That is what the link company rule is for.

Under consortium condition two, in section one hundred and thirty three, a claimant that is not itself a member can claim if it is in the same group as a member, the link company. Tarnmoor plc is the link company. Tarnmoor Engineering claims. The limit is measured by the link company's ownership proportion, and the total claimed by the link company and its whole group cannot exceed what the link company itself could have claimed. So Tarnmoor Engineering took one point eight million pounds in Group Year One, one point three five million in Group Year Two, and four hundred and fifty thousand in Group Year Three. Greyfell took one point six million, one point two million and four hundred thousand. Helmside's own carried-forward losses reached one point two million pounds.

Helmside's shareholders' agreement, in our invented case, requires each claimant to pay twenty five pence for every pound of loss it takes. So Tarnmoor Engineering paid Helmside four hundred and fifty thousand pounds for Group Year One, within the rule that ignores such payments.

Now losses flowing down. In Group Year Four Helmside made its first profit, two point five million pounds. It used its own one point two million of carried-forward losses first, leaving available total profits of one point three million pounds. Tarnmoor plc, a member, had surplus current-year management expenses, and the same shareholders' agreement asks members to offer surplus relief to Helmside at full value.

When a member surrenders down, the limit is the member's ownership proportion of the consortium company's available total profits. Not the member's loss. This is the trap the examiners named after November twenty twenty four. Candidates applied the consortium percentage to the member's loss, instead of to the consortium company's profit. On a full year, forty five per cent of one point three million pounds is five hundred and eighty five thousand pounds.

One anti-avoidance rule deserves a mention here. Sections one hundred and forty six A and one hundred and forty six B, for accounting periods beginning on or after the twelfth of July, twenty ten, can halve consortium relief where arrangements stop a member from controlling the consortium company and a main purpose is a tax advantage. The rule targets structures that keep a member just short of control so that relief can flow as consortium relief. The Upper Tribunal applied it in H M R C versus South Eastern Power Networks, decided in twenty nineteen.

When the consortium ends.

Group Year Four did not end as planned. On the first of December, Tarnmoor plc and Greyfell signed heads of terms. Tarnmoor would buy Greyfell's forty per cent in exchange for new Tarnmoor shares. The deal completed on the first of March in Group Year Five, and chapter eighteen tells its share exchange.

Section one hundred and fifty five of the twenty ten Act applies the arrangements idea to consortia. A trading company is treated as not owned by a consortium if a member or the company has surrenderable amounts and arrangements exist under which, among other effects, the company could become a seventy five per cent subsidiary of a third company. A third company means one not already in the same group as the consortium company. Tarnmoor plc held forty five per cent. It was not in Helmside's group. So, as a matter of the statute's words, Tarnmoor plc was a third company, even though it was also a member.

Tom Hesketh, Tarnmoor's group head of tax, had to decide when the arrangements began. On H M R C's view, heads of terms agreed in principle, even subject to contract, are normally enough. He took the first of December as the start date. In our invented case, he was careful to show the board why. The heads of terms named a price, a structure and a timetable, and both boards had approved them.

The result is that the consortium relationship broke on the first of December. The overlapping period for Group Year Four shrank to eleven months, from the first of January to the thirtieth of November. Helmside's available total profits for that period were eleven twelfths of one point three million pounds. Forty five per cent of that is five hundred and thirty six thousand, two hundred and fifty pounds. That was the most Tarnmoor plc could surrender down, forty eight thousand, seven hundred and fifty pounds less than a full year would have allowed. The full working is in the reading edition.

Did it cost the group anything? Very little. The forty eight thousand, seven hundred and fifty pounds of management expenses not used by Helmside could go to Tarnmoor Engineering instead, within the parent's threshold, worth twenty five pence a pound either way. The arrangements rule bites hardest where relief has nowhere else to go. Here it simply changed which company used it.

Helmside paid corporation tax on seven hundred and sixty three thousand, seven hundred and fifty pounds. It paid Tarnmoor plc for the relief at the agreed rate.

From the first of March in Group Year Five, Tarnmoor plc owned eighty five per cent of Helmside, and Helmside joined the seventy five per cent group. From that date group relief, not consortium relief, is the pipe. For the months before, the arrangements still existed, so neither relief was available. Helmside also joined the group's loss restriction allowance on that day, and countersigned the standing nomination described in chapter fourteen.

Choosing a joint venture structure.

When groups go into business together, the tax answer depends on the shape they choose. The examiners asked candidates in May twenty twenty three to compare a seventy five per cent subsidiary with a sixty five per cent one. Some missed that the second was a consortium.

At seventy five per cent or more, the venture is in the investor's group. Losses move by group relief, without proportion limits. Assets move between them at no gain, no loss, as chapter seventeen explains. But the minority partner is locked out of the losses, and arrangements rules watch every exit clause.

Below seventy five per cent, with corporate partners holding at least seventy five per cent between them, the venture is a consortium company. Each member takes losses in proportion, and relief can flow down. But there is no gains group, so assets cannot move in tax-free.

A labelled illustration makes the difference concrete. Suppose a new venture loses one million pounds in its first year. If our investor owns seventy five per cent, it is in the investor's group, and the investor's group can take the whole million, while the twenty five per cent partner gets nothing. If the investor owns sixty five per cent and a company partner owns thirty five, the venture is a consortium company. The investor can take six hundred and fifty thousand pounds and the partner three hundred and fifty thousand. Which structure is better depends on who has profits, and on what the partners agreed about sharing relief.

A third shape is a partnership or limited liability partnership. Then there is no separate taxpayer at all. Each corporate partner is taxed on its own share, as the next section explains.

Three more rules complete the joint venture picture. First, dividends. A large company needs every dividend it receives to fit an exempt class. One class covers a joint venture where the recipient is one of two persons who together control the payer, the recipient holds at least forty per cent, and the other holds between forty and fifty five per cent. Tarnmoor and Greyfell, at forty five and forty, fit that test. If they had not, Helmside's dividends would still usually be exempt as dividends on ordinary shares.

Second, the substantial shareholding exemption has special look-through rules for holdings in joint venture companies, so that a venture's trade can count towards the investor's trading status. Chapter eighteen covers them.

Third, employee shares. The corporation tax deduction for shares given to employees extends to shares in a consortium member that owns the employer. So Helmside's staff could receive Tarnmoor shares, and Helmside could claim the deduction.

Companies as partners.

In Group Year Four, Tarnmoor Estates and an unconnected developer formed Moorgate Logistics, a limited liability partnership, owning half each. It built and let a logistics park. Moorgate is invented, like the rest of our case.

A limited liability partnership carrying on business with a view to profit is transparent for tax. Its business is treated as carried on in partnership by its members. So Moorgate pays no corporation tax. Its partners do.

For a corporate partner, section twelve fifty nine of the two thousand and nine Act says the firm's profits are computed as if a U K resident company carried on the business. That brings in corporation tax rules, not income tax rules, including the loan relationship rules and capital allowances. The profit is then shared according to the profit-sharing arrangements for the period, and each partner's share is apportioned to its own accounting periods. Qualifying charitable donations made by the firm are shared the same way.

In our invented case, Moorgate's accounts for Group Year Four showed a profit of one point eight million pounds. But nine hundred thousand pounds of that was a fair value gain on the park, which is not income. A gain will only be taxed when the park is sold. On corporation tax rules, the property business profit was one point one million pounds, after capital allowances on the plant in the buildings. The bank interest, three hundred thousand pounds, is a loan relationship debit. Tarnmoor Estates' half was a property profit of five hundred and fifty thousand pounds and a non-trading loan relationship debit of a hundred and fifty thousand pounds. Its accounts showed nine hundred thousand pounds as its share. Its taxable share was a net four hundred thousand pounds.

Losses work the same way. If the firm makes a trading loss, in outline the corporate partner's share is treated as its own loss, computed on corporation tax rules, and it can use it like any other loss of the period, including by group relief within its own group. That is one reason groups often hold their partnership interests through a company with plenty of group companies around it.

One more rule applies where individuals and companies are partners together. The mixed membership rules, in section eight fifty C of the Income Tax, Trading and Other Income, Act two thousand and five, can reallocate profit from a corporate partner to an individual partner. That happens where the company's share includes an individual's deferred profit, or where the company's share is more than a commercial return and the individual can enjoy it. Section twelve sixty four A of the two thousand and nine Act then adjusts the corporate partner's share. The rules stop individuals sheltering profit at corporation tax rates inside a corporate partner. Moorgate has no individual members, so they do not apply.

Planning, and the tax function's year.

For a group like Tarnmoor, group relief is not a calculation done once at the year end. It is a cycle.

At the start of the year, the tax function forecasts each company's profits and losses, and decides where relief will land. For very large companies, that forecast drives the instalments, so it is a cash decision made in month three.

During the year, it watches the deal pipeline. Any agreement in principle to sell a subsidiary, or to change a joint venture's ownership, may start arrangements. Signing heads of terms on the first of a month, rather than the last, can cost a month of relief.

It chooses the order of surrenders. Current-year relief is unrestricted and certain, so the group uses it first. Within that, it fills the most valuable claimants first, and keeps an eye on companies whose own carried-forward losses would otherwise sit behind the loss restriction. A pound of loss is worth twenty five pence in any Tarnmoor company, because each of them pays the main rate. In a smaller group, where one company sits in the marginal relief band, the same pound can be worth more in one place than another.

It manages thresholds. For the parent, it tests the profit-related threshold, remembering the controlled foreign company apportionment. For consortium claims, it checks the four ownership measures, not just the share register.

At the return stage, it collects consents, from every member for a consortium claim, and diaries the claim deadlines. It agrees payments for relief in writing, within the statutory limit. And it considers the effects elsewhere. Group relief changes which companies have taxable profits, and so affects deferred tax, chapter six, and the corporate interest restriction, chapter twenty eight, where disallowed interest is allocated between companies.

Finally, it chooses the joint venture structure with the exit in mind. The cleanest structure for losses on day one can be the costliest when the partners part.

The examiner tests this ground often. Group and consortium relief appeared in a twenty-mark question in November twenty twenty four, covering two years of claims, joining by conditional contract and arrangements, a holding company in liquidation, a dual resident investing company, preference shares and the link company. The examiners described the answers as poor. May twenty twenty three asked for the seventy five against sixty five per cent comparison. May twenty twenty six put group relief inside a two-company computation, and the best answers used it to keep a profit in the lower band. The traps the examiners listed are worth learning by heart. Wrong joining and leaving dates. Missing the dual resident investing company bar. Missing the effect of a holding company's liquidation. Applying the consortium percentage to the member's loss instead of the consortium company's profit. And forgetting the trading requirement and the link company. Lay the claims out by overlapping period, show both limits, and state the time limits and consents. In this paper the answer is technical prose and computation, never a letter, and each correct point earns about half a mark to a mark.

What to take away.

The United Kingdom taxes each company separately. Group relief is a pipe between companies, not a merger of them. It is a claim, needing consent, measured for the overlapping period, and limited to the lower of what the surrendering company can give and what the claimant can take.

A group for this purpose needs seventy five per cent of the ordinary share capital, held beneficially, plus seventy five per cent of the profits and assets available to equity holders. It can be traced through non-residents, but relief passes only between U K related companies.

Trading losses, excess capital allowances and loan relationship deficits can be surrendered in full. Management expenses, property losses, charitable donations and intangibles losses can be surrendered only above the profit-related threshold, which includes any apportioned controlled foreign company profits. For Tarnmoor plc, that meant five point oh seven five million pounds of management expenses in Group Year One, not five point nine, with eight hundred and twenty five thousand pounds a year stranded.

The claimant takes relief last, against available total profits after its own reliefs. Tarnmoor Engineering's group and consortium relief in Group Year Two was seventeen point oh nine five million pounds, leaving tax of two million, two hundred and twenty six thousand, two hundred and fifty pounds.

Groups begin on beneficial ownership, usually completion. They end early when arrangements exist, which on H M R C's view usually means agreement in principle. Farnborough showed that even a receiver's appointment can break a group.

Claims can be made up to the latest of the first anniversary of the claimant's filing date and the enquiry dates. Payments for relief up to the amount surrendered are ignored for tax.

Carried-forward losses can be surrendered under Part five A, but only after own use, inside the loss restriction, and never for a joiner's pre-acquisition losses within five years.

A consortium company is at least seventy five per cent owned by companies holding at least five per cent each, and not a seventy five per cent subsidiary. Relief is limited by the lowest of four ratios. Going up, it is that proportion of the consortium company's loss. Going down, it is that proportion of the consortium company's profits. A link company lets a member's group companies claim. Helmside's losses gave Tarnmoor Engineering one point eight million, one point three five million and four hundred and fifty thousand pounds. In Group Year Four the heads of terms cut Tarnmoor plc's surrender down to five hundred and thirty six thousand, two hundred and fifty pounds.

A corporate partner is taxed on its share of the firm's profits computed on corporation tax rules.

So the chapter's question has its answer. The law makes losses travel company by company because the group is not a taxpayer. It lets them travel at all because the group is a business. Every limit in this chapter sits on the line between those two truths.

Losses are not the only thing a group moves. Chapter sixteen turns to company gains on property and leases, and chapter seventeen shows how assets, like losses, move inside the group.

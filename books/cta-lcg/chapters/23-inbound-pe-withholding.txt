Chapter twenty three: Inbound, non-resident companies, permanent establishments and withholding.

On the eighteenth of March, twenty twenty six, Finance Act twenty twenty six received Royal Assent. Buried in its Schedule seven was a new sentence for the twenty ten Act. It concerned the person who works in the United Kingdom for a foreign company. Under the old wording, that person created a taxable presence only if they had, and habitually exercised, authority to do business for the company. Under the new wording, it is enough that they habitually conclude contracts, or habitually play the principal role leading to the conclusion of contracts that the company routinely concludes without material modification.

Read that again slowly, because it is the heart of this chapter. A person who never signs anything can now give a foreign company a permanent establishment in the United Kingdom. The person who closes the deal matters more than the person who holds the pen.

This chapter is about companies that are not resident here but do business here. What does the United Kingdom tax them on? When does their activity here become a permanent establishment? How are profits attributed to it? Who answers for the tax? And, from the other side of the payment, when must a United Kingdom company deduct income tax from interest or royalties it pays to someone abroad? The chargeable profits of non-resident companies, the permanent establishment concept, non-resident companies' gains and the deduction of income tax are all core topics on the paper. The chapter builds on The Living Law, chapter twenty six, which introduced residence, permanent establishments and the branch.

Some names for the journey. The Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine is the two thousand and nine Act. The Corporation Tax Act twenty ten is the twenty ten Act, and it holds the permanent establishment definition. The Taxation of Chargeable Gains Act nineteen ninety two is the Gains Act. The Taxation, International and Other Provisions, Act twenty ten is the International Act. And the withholding rules sit in Part fifteen of the Income Tax Act two thousand and seven, which I will call the Income Tax Act. We apply the law for the financial year twenty twenty six, and twenty twenty six to twenty seven for the income tax points.

What the United Kingdom taxes on a foreign company.

A company resident in the United Kingdom pays corporation tax on its profits wherever they arise. A company resident elsewhere pays only where it has a link to this country that the law recognises. Think of it as a footprint. The United Kingdom taxes a foreign company only where that company has left a footprint here, and since twenty twenty six the law also counts footprints made by others walking on its behalf.

Section five of the two thousand and nine Act lists the footprints. A non-resident company is within the charge to corporation tax if it carries on a trade of dealing in or developing United Kingdom land. It is within the charge if it carries on a trade in the United Kingdom through a permanent establishment here. And since the sixth of April, twenty twenty, it is within the charge on a United Kingdom property business and on other United Kingdom property income. Loan relationship and derivative profits of that property business come into the charge with it.

Notice what is missing. A foreign company that sells to United Kingdom customers from abroad, with no permanent establishment here and no land, is not within corporation tax on its trading profits at all. Trading with the United Kingdom is not the same as trading in it through a permanent establishment.

For the permanent establishment head, section nineteen says what is charged. It is the trading income arising directly or indirectly through or from the establishment, plus income from property or rights used by or held by it, attributed under the rules we meet later. Land dealing and property income are charged under their own heads, not as permanent establishment profits.

Gains follow a parallel map in section two B of the Gains Act. A non-resident company is chargeable on gains on United Kingdom assets used by its United Kingdom permanent establishment. Since the sixth of April, twenty nineteen, it is also chargeable on gains on interests in United Kingdom land, wherever it is, and on indirect disposals. An indirect disposal is a disposal of an asset that derives at least seventy five per cent of its value from United Kingdom land, where the company has a substantial indirect interest. H M R C's manual explains that this means holding at least twenty five per cent, at some time in the two years before the disposal. Finance Act twenty twenty six added a detail for protected cell companies, treating each cell as a separate company for the seventy five per cent test.

Our invented group, Tarnmoor, gives us two inbound cases. Tarnmoor plc is a listed engineering group, and every company, person and number in it is invented for teaching. Its Vallarian subsidiary, Tarnmoor Vallaria, is resident in the invented country of Vallaria. And its Irish subsidiary, Tarnmoor Ireland, stops being United Kingdom resident on the thirtieth of June, Group Year Four, as chapter twenty two explained. After that date it still owns a warehouse in England. Both companies will test the footprint rules before this chapter ends.

A fixed place of business.

Section eleven forty one of the twenty ten Act gives two ways for a company to have a permanent establishment in a territory. The first is the fixed place limb. A company has a permanent establishment if it has a fixed place of business there through which its business is wholly or partly carried on. The second is the agent limb, which comes next.

The statute gives a list of things that count as fixed places. A place of management. A branch. An office. A factory. A workshop. An installation for exploring natural resources. A mine, a well or a quarry. And a building site or a construction or installation project.

On the usual reading, three ideas sit inside the words fixed place of business. There must be a place, which can be as modest as an area of floor in someone else's premises. It must be fixed, meaning a link to a particular location with some permanence. And the business of the company must be carried on through it, which usually means the place is at the company's disposal and its business is done there.

The domestic list sets no time limit for a building site. That matters because treaties usually do. Under the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development Model, which I will call the O E C D Model, a building site or construction project is a permanent establishment only if it lasts more than twelve months. The exam supplies treaty extracts, and in the May twenty twenty six paper a construction question turned on the twelve month test. Under the domestic list, a short site can still be a fixed place, which is a question of degree. Where a treaty applies, its time limit protects the foreign company.

One misconception needs clearing at once. A United Kingdom subsidiary is not a permanent establishment of its foreign parent simply because the parent controls it. H M R C's international manual says so plainly, and it follows the O E C D Model. A subsidiary is a separate company with its own profits and its own corporation tax bill. But a subsidiary can still be the parent's agent on the facts, if it works on the parent's contracts in the way the agent limb describes. Control alone proves nothing. Conduct can prove a great deal.

The examiners have a related complaint. In November twenty twenty five, some candidates treated a permanent establishment as if it were a separate legal entity. It is not. A permanent establishment is part of the foreign company. It has no shares, no directors and no separate legal personality. The law sometimes treats it as if it were a separate enterprise, for attributing profits, but that is a hypothesis for measurement, not a description of the law of persons.

The agent who never signs.

The second limb is the agent limb, and it is the one Finance Act twenty twenty six rewrote. Why did it need rewriting?

For decades the test asked whether an agent had, and habitually exercised, authority to do business on the company's behalf. In practice that pointed at the power to conclude contracts. Multinational groups learned to live just outside it. A local company, often a group company, would find the customer, negotiate the price, agree the specification and then send the contract abroad for signature. The foreign company signed. The local company was paid a modest fee. The profit on the sale stayed abroad, and so did the taxing right.

The international response came through the base erosion and profit shifting project, which I will call B E P S. Its report on Action seven, published in October twenty fifteen, aimed to stop the artificial avoidance of permanent establishment status, including the so-called commissionaire arrangements and their cousins. It proposed the principal role wording, and that wording entered the O E C D Model. The United Kingdom's first answer was unilateral. The diverted profits tax of twenty fifteen included a limb aimed at companies that avoided a United Kingdom taxable presence. Chapter thirty tells that story and its ending. The second answer is the new statutory wording, which brings the dependent agent test inside corporation tax itself.

Here is the new test, from section eleven forty one, for chargeable periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six. A company has a permanent establishment in a territory if a person acting on its behalf there habitually concludes contracts, or habitually plays the principal role leading to the conclusion of contracts that are routinely concluded without material modification by the company. The contracts must be of one of two kinds. Contracts for the transfer of ownership of, or the right to use, property that the company owns or has the right to use. Or contracts for the provision of services by the company.

Take the phrases one at a time. Acting on behalf of the company means doing the company's business, not the agent's own. Habitually means repeatedly, as a pattern, not once. The principal role is the role that actually closes the deal, such as persuading the customer, settling the terms and agreeing the price. Routinely concluded without material modification means the foreign company's signature is a formality. If head office regularly rewrites the deals, the agent is not playing the principal role. If head office signs what arrives, it is.

The commencement date needs care, and the examiner will test it. The new wording applies for chargeable periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six. Here is a real calendar example. A foreign company with a calendar year end is on the new test from its period beginning on the first of January, twenty twenty six. Now take a foreign company whose year ends on the thirtieth of June. Its period from the first of July, twenty twenty five, to the thirtieth of June, twenty twenty six, stays on the old wording. It moves to the new test from the first of July, twenty twenty six. For the Tarnmoor story, every period is simply treated as falling under the new law.

There is also a new purpose clause. Section eleven forty A of the twenty ten Act says the permanent establishment chapter is to be read consistently with Article five of the O E C D Model and its Commentary. The version named is the one approved by the O E C D Council on the eighteenth of November, twenty twenty five. So when you meet a phrase such as principal role, the Commentary on that phrase is now part of how the United Kingdom statute is read. Chapter two explained this new habit of statute pointing at Paris.

Independent agents and the closely related rule.

The agent limb has always had an exception. Under section eleven forty two, a company does not have a permanent establishment merely because it carries on business through an agent of independent status acting in the ordinary course of that agent's business. A broker, a general commission agent or a freight forwarder serving many clients is the classic independent agent. The foreign company is one customer among many, and the agent bears its own business risk.

Finance Act twenty twenty six closed a gap in that exception. A person is not of independent status if it is closely related to the company and acts exclusively or almost exclusively on behalf of the company, or of it and other companies to which it is closely related. A group sales company that works only for its sister companies cannot hide behind the independent agent exception, however separate its board and its books.

For agents outside the group, the old questions still matter. The factors usually weighed are whether the agent is legally and economically independent of the principal, whether it bears its own business risk, whether it works for several principals, and whether it is subject to detailed instructions and control. A commission agent with fifty clients, who sets its own hours and loses money when sales fall, is independent. A one-client agent whose diary is set by the principal looks dependent, whether or not it is in the same group.

What does closely related mean? The answer is in the new subsection two C A of section eleven forty three. Looking at all the relevant facts and circumstances, two persons are closely related if one has control of the other, or both are under the control of the same person or persons. There is also a fifty per cent investment condition that points to the hybrid rules in the International Act. In a wholly owned group, every company is closely related to every other.

Put the two rules together and you have the common exam fact pattern. A United Kingdom group company works almost entirely for a foreign group company. It cannot be independent. So the only question is whether it habitually concludes contracts, or plays the principal role leading to contracts the foreign company routinely concludes without material modification. If so, and if no treaty prevents it, the foreign company has a United Kingdom permanent establishment.

Preparatory, auxiliary and the fragmentation trap.

Some activity in the United Kingdom is too slight to justify taxing a foreign company here, even when it happens at a fixed place. Section eleven forty three of the twenty ten Act carves it out. There is no permanent establishment if the activities, viewed in relation to the business as a whole, are only of a preparatory or auxiliary character.

The statute gives examples. Using facilities for storing, displaying or delivering the company's goods. Keeping a stock of goods for storage, display or delivery, or for processing by someone else. Purchasing goods or collecting information for the company. The idea is simple. A warehouse that holds spare parts for a business run elsewhere supports the business. It is not the business.

Two words in the test carry the weight. Only, and as a whole. An activity that forms an essential and significant part of the company's business is not auxiliary, even if it appears on the list. A warehouse company whose whole business is warehousing does not escape just because storage is a listed example. You always ask what the activity is, compared with what the company does overall.

Then comes the trap. Since twenty nineteen, section eleven forty three has contained anti-fragmentation rules. Without them, a group could split a single business into pieces, each one auxiliary on its own, and spread the pieces across group companies or across sites. The rule stops that. The exception does not apply where the activities carried on by the company, or by a closely related person, at the same place or at another place in the same territory, are complementary functions forming part of a cohesive business operation. The exception is then lost if either the combined activity is more than preparatory or auxiliary, or one of the places is already a permanent establishment of the company or of the closely related person.

Here is a labelled hypothetical, not part of our story. Suppose Tarnmoor Vallaria rented a small unit near Leeds to hold spare valve parts for its United Kingdom customers. On its own, storage and delivery of the company's goods looks preparatory or auxiliary. But suppose Tarnmoor Engineering, a closely related company, serviced and repaired the same valves for the same customers. If the parts store and that servicing were complementary functions in one cohesive operation, and the combined activity were more than preparatory or auxiliary, the anti-fragmentation rule would remove the exception. The unit would then be a fixed place permanent establishment of Tarnmoor Vallaria.

The examiners have flagged this rule. In November twenty twenty five, a fifteen mark question asked candidates to analyse four contracts for permanent establishment risk. One involved an independent agent, one a preparatory or auxiliary activity, one a short construction contract, and one turned on fragmentation. Few candidates cited the anti-fragmentation rule. If your scenario has a group company in the same country doing related work, test it every time.

Domestic law meets the treaty.

So far we have been reading United Kingdom domestic law. But most foreign companies trading here are resident in a country with which the United Kingdom has a double tax treaty. What happens when the two disagree?

The treaty wins. Section six of the International Act gives treaty arrangements effect, generally in priority to domestic law. Under Article seven of a treaty in O E C D form, the business profits of an enterprise of one state are taxable only in that state. The exception is where the enterprise carries on business in the other state through a permanent establishment situated there. And the treaty's own Article five decides what a permanent establishment is. H M R C's manual makes the same point. Where a treaty applies, its permanent establishment article governs whether the United Kingdom may tax business profits.

This matters because the new domestic agent test can be wider than the treaty test. A treaty only has the principal role wording if the two countries put it there, either in a new treaty or through the Multilateral Instrument, the convention that let countries update many treaties at once. Article twelve of the Multilateral Instrument carries the new agent wording. The United Kingdom did not take it up. The synthesised text of the United Kingdom and Ireland treaty confirms that Article twelve does not apply there, and commentators report that the United Kingdom reserved on the whole article. So many United Kingdom treaties still carry the older agent wording, under which an agent creates a permanent establishment only if it has, and habitually exercises, an authority to conclude contracts in the name of the enterprise.

The result is a three-step routine for the exam. First, apply the domestic test. Is there a fixed place, or a dependent agent, and does any exception apply? Second, if the foreign company is resident in a treaty country, apply the treaty's Article five, using the extract the examiner supplies, not your memory of the O E C D Model. Third, the United Kingdom taxes the business profits only if both answers are yes. The examiners have repeatedly said the same thing in their own way. Where a question supplies a treaty extract, use it. In November twenty twenty three, the examiners noted that some candidates ignored the treaty article supplied and used the O E C D Model text instead.

Where there is no treaty, the domestic test is the whole answer. Marrovia, our other invented country, has no treaty with the United Kingdom. A Marrovian company whose United Kingdom sister company habitually played the principal role in its sales would have a United Kingdom permanent establishment, full stop.

Tarnmoor Vallaria's United Kingdom customers.

Back to our invented group. In Group Year Four, Tarnmoor Vallaria starts selling Ashlar valves directly to large United Kingdom water and energy utilities. The valves are made in Vallaria under the patent licence from Calder that chapter eleven described. Tarnmoor Vallaria has no office, warehouse or staff in the United Kingdom.

It does have help. Tarnmoor Engineering's key-account engineers, based in Leeds, know the utilities well. In our invented case they visit the customers, scope each job, propose the specification and negotiate the price. When the customer agrees, the engineers send a completed contract to Tarnmoor Vallaria's office in Vallaria. Someone there signs it. In the first six months nobody in Vallaria changes a single commercial term. Tarnmoor Vallaria pays Tarnmoor Engineering a service fee for the engineers' time, priced under the transfer pricing rules that chapter twenty seven explains.

Tom Hesketh, Tarnmoor's invented group head of tax, writes a memo for the board. It follows the three-step routine.

Step one, domestic law. Tarnmoor Vallaria has no fixed place in the United Kingdom. The Leeds offices belong to Tarnmoor Engineering and are not at Tarnmoor Vallaria's disposal. So the fixed place limb fails. The agent limb is different. The engineers act on Tarnmoor Vallaria's behalf in these sales. They do it again and again, so habitually. They settle the terms, so they play the principal role. The contracts are routinely concluded without material modification. And they are contracts for the transfer of ownership of goods Tarnmoor Vallaria owns. Can Tarnmoor Engineering be an independent agent? No. It is closely related to Tarnmoor Vallaria, and in this market it acts almost exclusively for the group. Tom's conclusion is that, under the twenty ten Act alone, Tarnmoor Vallaria has a United Kingdom permanent establishment.

Step two, the treaty. Tarnmoor Vallaria is resident in Vallaria, and the invented United Kingdom and Vallaria treaty follows the O E C D Model in most respects. But its Article five keeps the older agent wording. It asks whether a person has, and habitually exercises, an authority to conclude contracts in the name of the enterprise. The engineers have no such authority. The contracts are signed in Vallaria. So there is no treaty permanent establishment, and under the treaty's Article seven, the United Kingdom cannot tax Tarnmoor Vallaria's business profits.

Step three, the answer. No United Kingdom corporation tax for Tarnmoor Vallaria, because the treaty prevails.

Tom does not stop there, and neither should you. A conclusion that rests on a treaty is only as safe as the treaty. Treaties are renegotiated. The facts can drift, for example if an engineer is given authority to sign. And the board must also ask how the arrangement looks from Vallaria and under the group's published tax strategy. The board therefore decides to change the model. From the first of January, Group Year Five, Tarnmoor Engineering becomes a buy-sell distributor. It buys Ashlar valves from Tarnmoor Vallaria and sells them to the utilities in its own name, taking its own profit. That replaces a permanent establishment question with a transfer pricing question about the distributor's margin, which is chapter twenty seven's.

This is the chapter's misconception, and you now see why it is wrong. People believe that a foreign company selling into the United Kingdom through a group company's sales team cannot have a United Kingdom permanent establishment, because the sales team works for a different company and nobody here signs anything. Before twenty twenty six, that belief was close to the truth. Since then, the principal role test makes signing irrelevant, and the closely related rule makes the separate company irrelevant. Only a treaty with the older wording now stands between the foreign company and a United Kingdom charge.

Attributing profit to a permanent establishment.

Suppose the treaty had not protected Tarnmoor Vallaria. What profit would the United Kingdom tax?

The answer comes from sections twenty and twenty one of the two thousand and nine Act, both rewritten by Finance Act twenty twenty six for chargeable periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six. Section twenty one asks what profits the permanent establishment might be expected to make if it were a separate and independent enterprise, engaged in the same or similar activities, under the same or similar conditions. That takes into account the functions performed, the assets used and the risks assumed, through the establishment and through the rest of the company. The establishment is assumed to have the same credit rating as the company, and the equity and loan capital it could reasonably be expected to have.

Section twenty then tells us how to read section twenty one. It is to be read consistently with a family of O E C D texts. They are Article seven, paragraph two, of the Model and its Commentary, the twenty ten Report on the attribution of profits to permanent establishments, the Transfer Pricing Guidelines and the twenty eighteen additional guidance. The references are to those texts as amended from time to time, subject to United Kingdom reservations. The twenty ten Report sets out what is called the authorised O E C D approach.

H M R C's manuals describe that approach in two steps. Step one is a functional and factual analysis. You hypothesise the establishment as a separate enterprise and decide which functions, assets and risks belong to it, looking at where the people who manage them actually work. Step two prices the dealings between the establishment and the rest of the company at arm's length, using transfer pricing methods by analogy.

Here is the trap. Before twenty twenty six, sections twenty two, twenty three and twenty five to thirty two of the two thousand and nine Act contained specific rules. Section twenty nine dealt with allowable deductions, and section thirty two restricted interest by reference to attributed capital. Finance Act twenty twenty six omitted them all for current periods. An answer that cites section thirty two for a twenty twenty seven exam is quoting repealed law.

For a dependent agent permanent establishment, there is a live debate. One view is that once the agent is paid an arm's length fee, nothing more is left for the establishment. The other view, which commentators say the O E C D's later work favours, looks at what the agent's people actually do. Where they perform the functions that manage the enterprise's risks, such as inventory or credit risk, profit from those risks is attributed to the establishment, and the fee does not always exhaust it. The outcome can range from nothing to a large share of the sales profit.

A labelled illustration, not story. Suppose the analysis attributed eight hundred thousand pounds of profit to the establishment after the service fee. Corporation tax at twenty five per cent would be two hundred thousand pounds a year, and Tarnmoor Vallaria would then look to Vallaria for relief from double tax.

Who answers for the foreign company.

Taxing a company that lives abroad raises a practical problem. Its directors, its bank accounts and most of its assets are out of reach. Part twenty two of the twenty ten Act contains three short chapters that answer the problem, and all three are core.

Chapter six makes the permanent establishment the company's United Kingdom representative. The establishment is treated as a separate person for this purpose, and the company's obligations on assessment, collection and recovery of corporation tax are also the representative's. It remains the representative even after the establishment ceases, for tax relating to the period when it existed. Section nine seventy one sets out some limited exceptions.

Chapter seven lets H M R C look to other companies in the group. If corporation tax assessed on a non-resident company is still unpaid six months after it became payable, H M R C can serve a notice on a related company. That means a company in the same group as the non-resident, or a member of a consortium owning it, during the relevant period. The related company must pay within thirty days, and the notice has effect as an assessment. In a consortium case only a proportion is recoverable. This is why a United Kingdom parent cannot treat a foreign subsidiary's United Kingdom tax as somebody else's problem.

Chapter five deals with income tax deducted at source. Income tax deducted from payments a company receives is set against its corporation tax for the period, and any excess is repaid. Section nine sixty seven covers United Kingdom resident companies and section nine sixty eight covers non-resident companies, so a foreign landlord company whose tenants deduct tax from its rent can set that tax against its corporation tax bill. We will see that mechanism at work with Tarnmoor Ireland at the end of the chapter.

Do not confuse chapter seven with the recovery rules on migration. When a company leaves the United Kingdom, the Taxes Management Act nineteen seventy lets H M R C recover unpaid exit tax from group companies and controlling directors, as chapter twenty two explained. That is a different power, aimed at the moment of leaving. Part twenty two, chapter seven, covers a company that is non-resident and stays chargeable here.

A non-resident company that comes within the charge, through a permanent establishment or a United Kingdom property business, is otherwise treated like any other company for corporation tax. It notifies chargeability, files returns, pays on the normal dates and, if it is large, pays by instalments. And it is inside the wider regimes that apply to United Kingdom profits, such as the corporate interest restriction.

Why the payer collects the tax.

Now turn the telescope around. So far the foreign company has been earning profits here. Now a United Kingdom company is paying money to someone abroad. When must it deduct income tax before paying?

The logic is collection, not policy. A lender in another country is hard to tax by assessment. The company paying the interest is easy to find. So the law makes the payer the collector, deducting tax at source and paying it to H M R C. The recipient then reclaims or credits it, or a treaty cuts it.

The core rule is in section eight hundred and seventy four of the Income Tax Act. A company paying yearly interest arising in the United Kingdom must deduct income tax. So must anyone paying yearly interest to a person whose usual place of abode is outside the United Kingdom. For twenty twenty six to twenty seven, the deduction is at the basic rate, twenty per cent. Coming next, and already enacted, Finance Act twenty twenty six switches the rate to the savings basic rate from twenty twenty seven to twenty eight, and sets that rate at twenty two per cent.

What is yearly interest? No statute defines it. H M R C's manual notes that the law has distinguished yearly interest from short interest since eighteen oh six, and that the line rests on case law. Interest on a loan intended to last a year or more, or capable of doing so, is yearly. Interest on a loan intended to last less than a year is short, and no deduction is needed. The parties' intention when the loan is made matters most, and H M R C looks for clear evidence where short loans keep being rolled over.

The modern landmark is the Lehman Brothers case. Lehman Brothers International Europe went into administration in twenty oh eight and, unusually, its administrators ended up with a surplus of several billion pounds after paying all proved debts. Creditors were owed statutory interest for the years they had waited. Was that interest yearly interest, so that tax had to be deducted before payment? The Supreme Court, in H M R C versus the Joint Administrators of Lehman Brothers International Europe, decided in March twenty nineteen, held unanimously that it was. Interest that compensates a creditor for being kept out of money over more than a year can be yearly interest, even though it was never payable from year to year.

The administration is simple to describe. A United Kingdom company accounts for income tax it deducts on a quarterly return, form C T sixty one. The quarters end on the thirty first of March, the thirtieth of June, the thirtieth of September and the thirty first of December, with a short return at the end of its accounting period. The return and the tax are due within fourteen days after the end of the return period.

The exits from interest withholding.

If the basic rule were the whole story, every large group would be drowning in deductions. In practice most interest paid by a large company leaves gross, through one of a handful of exits. Knowing them is most of the exam skill.

The first exit is the excepted payment. Under sections nine thirty three to nine thirty seven, no deduction is needed where the person beneficially entitled to the interest is a United Kingdom resident company. The same applies to a non-resident company that trades here through a permanent establishment and brings the interest into its chargeable profits. Certain other gross recipients, such as local authorities, charities and pension scheme administrators, are also covered. And under section nine thirty, the payer need not be certain. It is enough that it reasonably believes the payment is excepted, although H M R C can direct otherwise.

In our invented group, this exit carries a great deal of money. Tarnmoor Finance, the group treasury company, lends four hundred and thirty million pounds to four United Kingdom group companies at six per cent. That is twenty five point eight million pounds of interest a year, and every pound of it is paid gross, because the lender is a United Kingdom company.

The second exit is for banks. Interest paid by a bank in the ordinary course of its business is outside the rule, and so is interest on an advance from a bank where the person beneficially entitled is within the charge to corporation tax on it.

The third exit is the quoted Eurobond. Under section eight eight two, no deduction is needed from interest on a quoted Eurobond. Section nine eight seven defines one as a security issued by a company that carries a right to interest and is listed on a recognised stock exchange, or admitted to trading on a multilateral trading facility operated by a regulated recognised stock exchange. Tarnmoor Finance's five hundred and fifty million pounds of five and a half per cent notes are listed, so their thirty point two five million pounds of annual interest is paid gross. Without the exemption, twenty per cent withholding would take six point oh five million pounds a year off the payments to bondholders, and every one of them would need a treaty claim or a refund. That is why listing is the standard design for group bonds.

The fourth exit is the qualifying private placement under section eight eight eight A, in force since the first of January, twenty sixteen. It covers unlisted debt securities issued by companies that meet conditions in regulations, so private debt deals can also pay interest gross.

The fifth exit is the treaty. Many treaties cut withholding on interest, often to zero or a low rate. But for interest, unlike royalties, the payer cannot simply apply the treaty rate on its own judgement. The overseas lender must claim. One route is the D T Company form, on which the lender applies to H M R C, with certification of its residence, for relief at source or a repayment. The other is the Treaty Passport scheme. A treaty resident lender applies for a passport on form D T T P one, which normally lasts five calendar years. Each time it lends, the United Kingdom borrower notifies H M R C on form D T T P two. Until H M R C issues a direction to pay at the treaty rate, the borrower must keep deducting at the domestic rate.

One exit has closed. Until twenty twenty one, the European Union's Interest and Royalties Directive let United Kingdom companies pay interest and royalties gross to associated companies in other member states. Finance Act twenty twenty one repealed those rules for payments on or after the first of June, twenty twenty one. After Brexit, European Union lenders need a treaty, like everyone else.

And where no exit applies, the full rate bites. Chapter twenty nine tells the story of Project Undertow, a proposal that Tarnmoor Finance issue eighty million pounds of perpetual notes to Tarnmoor Capital in Marrovia at seven per cent. Marrovia has no treaty with the United Kingdom. The five point six million pound coupon would have suffered withholding of one point one two million pounds at twenty per cent. At twenty two per cent, from twenty twenty seven to twenty eight, it would be one point two three two million pounds. Withholding is rarely the main reason a structure fails, but it is often the cost nobody costed. The May twenty twenty three paper made the same point, with interest paid to Bermuda tucked inside a transfer pricing and interest restriction question.

Royalties, and paying at the treaty rate.

Royalties have their own rules. Under section nine hundred and three, a company paying a United Kingdom patent royalty must deduct income tax at the basic rate. Under section nine hundred and six, a company paying a royalty for the use of intellectual property to an owner whose usual place of abode is outside the United Kingdom must also deduct at the basic rate. Finance Act twenty twenty six did not change those rates. The twenty two per cent savings basic rate is for interest only.

Here royalties differ from interest in one generous way. Under section nine hundred and eleven, a company paying a royalty may deduct at the treaty rate straight away, without any clearance from H M R C, if it reasonably believes that the payee is entitled to that treaty relief. The price of self-help is risk. If the belief turns out to be wrong, the company is liable as if section nine hundred and eleven had never applied.

In our invented case, Brackenwell Sensors pays an unconnected Vallarian university fifty thousand pounds a year for a licence of a sensor patent. Under section nine hundred and six it would deduct twenty per cent, ten thousand pounds. The invented United Kingdom and Vallaria treaty cuts royalty withholding to five per cent. Brackenwell holds the university's evidence of Vallarian residence and reasonably believes it is entitled, so it deducts two thousand five hundred pounds and pays forty seven thousand five hundred. If the belief proved wrong, Brackenwell would owe the other seven thousand five hundred pounds itself. The royalty is paid each thirty first of December, so the tax goes on the C T sixty one for the quarter to that date, due by the fourteenth of January.

There is an anti-avoidance rule, and it has teeth. Section nine one seven A of the Income Tax Act was added by Finance Act twenty sixteen. It applies where the payee is connected with the payer and the royalty is paid under avoidance arrangements. These are arrangements one of whose main purposes was to obtain a treaty advantage contrary to the treaty's object and purpose. The royalty then bears the full domestic rate whatever the treaty says, and the tax deducted cannot be set off against the recipient's corporation tax. H M R C's manual notes that the rule overrides the usual priority of treaties. The Brackenwell licence is with an unconnected university, so the rule does not reach it.

Two quick points finish the map. There is no withholding on dividends paid by United Kingdom companies. And Finance Act twenty twenty six abolished, from twenty twenty six to twenty seven, the notional tax credit that non-resident shareholders could treat as attached to United Kingdom dividends. That is an income tax point, and only for awareness.

When the foreign company is a landlord.

Our last inbound case is Tarnmoor Ireland. Until the thirtieth of June, Group Year Four, its board met in Leeds, so it was United Kingdom resident and taxed here on everything. Chapter twenty two told the story of its migration to Dublin, with its exit charges and its payment plan. After that date it is a non-resident company. But it still owns the warehouse in England that it bought for three million pounds in Group Year One, let to an unconnected logistics company.

What does the United Kingdom now tax? Not Tarnmoor Ireland's distribution profits, which are earned in Ireland with no United Kingdom permanent establishment. But section five of the two thousand and nine Act still reaches the rent, because Tarnmoor Ireland carries on a United Kingdom property business. Since the sixth of April, twenty twenty, that charge is to corporation tax, not income tax. So Tarnmoor Ireland files corporation tax returns on its rental profits at the main rate, computes them under corporation tax rules, and is inside the corporate interest restriction like any other group company with United Kingdom profits.

The rent also brings in the non-resident landlord scheme. Under it, the letting agent, or the tenant if there is no agent, must normally deduct income tax at the basic rate from rent paid to a landlord whose usual place of abode is abroad. The exception is where H M R C has approved the landlord to receive the rent gross. Companies remain within the scheme after the move to corporation tax. Any tax deducted is credited against the company's corporation tax on that rental income, under the set-off rule for non-resident companies we met earlier.

A labelled illustration, not story, with round numbers. Suppose the rent were two hundred thousand pounds a year and Tarnmoor Ireland's rental profit one hundred and eighty thousand pounds. Without approval, the tenant would deduct forty thousand pounds. Corporation tax at twenty five per cent on the profit would be forty five thousand pounds, so after the credit Tarnmoor Ireland would pay five thousand pounds more. The full working is in the reading edition. Tom's team does the obvious thing and applies for approval to receive the rent gross, so cash is not tied up for months.

And when Tarnmoor Ireland sells the warehouse, the Gains Act charges it on the gain, because a non-resident company is chargeable on disposals of interests in United Kingdom land. The eight hundred thousand pounds of gain that was postponed when it migrated comes back into charge on that sale, as chapter twenty two explained. The footprint left by a building does not wash away when the company's mind moves abroad.

What to take away.

The United Kingdom taxes a non-resident company only where it leaves a footprint here. Section five of the two thousand and nine Act lists them. Dealing in or developing United Kingdom land, trading through a United Kingdom permanent establishment, and since April twenty twenty a United Kingdom property business. Gains follow the same map, with United Kingdom land and land-rich assets taxed since April twenty nineteen, using a seventy five per cent value test and a twenty five per cent interest test.

A permanent establishment arises through a fixed place of business, or through an agent. For chargeable periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six, the agent test asks whether a person habitually concludes contracts, or habitually plays the principal role leading to contracts routinely concluded without material modification. A closely related agent acting exclusively or almost exclusively for its group cannot be independent. Preparatory or auxiliary activity is excluded, but the anti-fragmentation rule takes the exclusion away for complementary functions in a cohesive operation. A subsidiary is not a permanent establishment by control alone, and a permanent establishment is never a separate legal person.

Where a treaty applies, its Article five decides, and many United Kingdom treaties keep the older agent wording because the United Kingdom did not adopt the Multilateral Instrument's Article twelve. Domestic test, treaty test, then the answer. That is how Tarnmoor Vallaria had a domestic permanent establishment in Group Year Four, but no treaty one, and why the group moved to a buy-sell distributor from Group Year Five.

Profits are attributed as if the establishment were a separate and independent enterprise, read with the authorised O E C D approach. The old sections twenty two to thirty two, apart from twenty four, are gone.

Part twenty two of the twenty ten Act makes the establishment the company's United Kingdom representative. It lets H M R C recover unpaid tax from related companies after six months, with thirty days to pay. And it sets income tax deducted against corporation tax.

A United Kingdom company paying yearly interest deducts twenty per cent for twenty twenty six to twenty seven, and twenty two per cent from twenty twenty seven to twenty eight. The exits are excepted payments to United Kingdom companies and permanent establishments, banks, quoted Eurobonds, qualifying private placements, and treaty clearance through the D T Company form or the Treaty Passport. Royalties to non-residents bear the basic rate, but the payer may apply the treaty rate on a reasonable belief, unless the connected-party anti-avoidance rule applies. Tax deducted goes on the quarterly C T sixty one, due within fourteen days.

So, can a foreign company that never signs a contract here be taxed here? Yes, if someone closely related habitually closes its deals, and if no treaty stops it. The footprint now includes the steps others take on its behalf.

The treaty has been the hero of this chapter. Chapter twenty four, on treaties and double tax relief, looks at it properly.

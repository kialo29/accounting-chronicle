Chapter eight: Plant and machinery for larger companies.

In our invented group, Tarnmoor, the capital expenditure committee meets early in Group Year Two with a list of purchases for Tarnmoor Engineering, the main trading company. New computer-controlled machining centres for the valve factory. Chillers and a new electrical system for the test hall. Test rigs that will be hired out to customers. A second-hand milling line bought from a competitor that is closing a site. Tom Hesketh, the group's head of tax, reads the list and asks the engineers the same question about every line. Is it new, or has somebody used it before?

That question is worth millions of pounds. On the answer turns whether a machine is written off for tax in the year it is bought, or drips into relief over a decade and more. And there is a second question Tom asks, which the engineers find stranger still. What will we do with it when we have finished with it? Because the most generous relief in the code comes with a receipt that must be handed back on the way out.

This chapter takes plant and machinery allowances to Advanced Technical depth, for the financial year twenty twenty six. Every syllabus row this chapter covers is core, except the value added tax adjustments, which are non-core. The Living Law, chapter twelve, built the basic machine. Plant as the tools of the trade rather than the setting in which it is carried on. The main and special rate pools. The annual investment allowance. Full expensing. Here we go further, into the special balancing charge, the forty per cent allowance, the hybrid rate, the group's single annual investment allowance, cars, long-life and short-life assets, software, hire purchase, part use and entertainment, and the way allowances are claimed and given effect. Buildings, fixtures, leasing and successions belong to chapter nine.

One naming note. The Capital Allowances Act two thousand and one, I will call the Capital Allowances Act. The Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine is the two thousand and nine Act.

Why the tax code ignores depreciation.

A company that buys a machine for a million pounds does not charge a million pounds against its profit that year. Its accounts spread the cost over the machine's useful life as depreciation. Depreciation is an estimate. It depends on the directors' view of useful lives, residual values and impairment, and two honest companies with identical machines can depreciate them quite differently. A tax system that followed depreciation would let every board choose its own timetable of relief.

So the tax code refuses it. Depreciation, amortisation of plant and impairment of plant are capital and are added back in the computation. In their place the Capital Allowances Act gives statutory allowances at rates Parliament sets. The swap has two effects. It makes relief uniform, so that the same machine earns the same relief in any company. And it hands the government a lever. By changing the rates, Parliament can pull investment forward or push it back.

The last few years show the lever at full stretch. In the Spring Budget of twenty twenty three, the government announced full expensing, a one hundred per cent first-year allowance for companies' new main-rate plant, to run from the first of April, twenty twenty three, to the thirty first of March, twenty twenty six. It replaced the temporary super-deduction. At the Autumn Statement on the twenty second of November, twenty twenty three, the government made full expensing permanent, and the Finance Act twenty twenty four removed the end date. Then, at the Budget on the twenty sixth of November, twenty twenty five, the government announced two linked changes, both enacted by the Finance Act twenty twenty six. The main pool writing-down allowance falls from eighteen to fourteen per cent. And a new forty per cent first-year allowance reaches assets that full expensing does not, such as plant bought for leasing and plant bought by unincorporated businesses.

Read together, the policy is clear. Generous to new investment, less generous to old pools. The government's policy paper says the rate cut bites on businesses with pools of historic main rate expenditure, and on spending that does not qualify for a first-year allowance.

For a large group, the consequence is that each asset has to be sorted. New or used. Main rate or special rate. Owned, hired or leased out. Kept or sold. Each answer sends the asset down a different road. A capital allowances computation is a sorting machine, only as good as the fixed asset data behind it.

What counts, and when.

Before any allowance there are two gates. The first is the qualifying activity. The Capital Allowances Act lists them. A trade is the obvious one. A property business is another, which is how a property company such as our invented Tarnmoor Estates claims for plant in the buildings it lets. The management of a company with investment business is a third, which is how the parent, Tarnmoor plc, claims for the furniture and computers in its head office. An employment is on the list too, though for companies it does not matter.

The second gate is qualifying expenditure. The general rule is capital expenditure on the provision of plant or machinery wholly or partly for the purposes of the qualifying activity, where the company owns the plant as a result. Capital, because revenue spending on repairs is simply deducted. Provision, which takes in the cost of installing the plant and of alterations to a building that the installation requires. Owns, which is why hire purchase and leasing need special rules, as we shall see. And plant, which still rests on the case law. The Living Law, chapter twelve, told that story. Plant is the apparatus with which the business is carried on, not the setting in which it is carried on. The statute then fences off buildings and structures with lists of what is excluded and what is saved, a subject for chapter nine.

Then comes timing, which matters far more now that a single date can decide a full year's relief. The law says capital expenditure is incurred when the obligation to pay it becomes unconditional, even if payment is due later. There are refinements. Where the company becomes owner of the asset by the end of a period, and the obligation to pay becomes unconditional within one month after the end, the expenditure is treated as incurred immediately before the period ends. Where an amount is not due until more than four months after the obligation becomes unconditional, it is incurred on the date it is due. And where the obligation is made unconditional earlier than normal commercial practice, mainly to bring relief into an earlier period, the anti-avoidance rule moves the date back to when payment is due. The examiner tested timing in May twenty twenty four, with plant built in stages.

One more trap sits at the gate. Plant a company already owns, and then brings into use for its qualifying activity, is not bought for that activity. The Act treats the company as incurring qualifying expenditure when the plant is brought into use. But no first-year allowance is given on that deemed expenditure. Assets a company acquires from a connected person carry the same bar, and the buyer's qualifying cost is capped at the seller's disposal value. Groups move plant between companies all the time, so this is not a corner case. A machine sold from one Tarnmoor company to another carries no annual investment allowance and no first-year allowance for the buyer, whatever its age.

Full expensing and the fifty per cent allowance.

Full expensing is the centrepiece of the modern regime, and its conditions are where the exam marks lie. The law, in section forty five S of the Capital Allowances Act, gives a first-year allowance on expenditure incurred on or after the first of April, twenty twenty three, by a company within the charge to corporation tax, on plant or machinery that is unused and not second-hand. The allowance is one hundred per cent for main rate expenditure. For special rate expenditure, it is fifty per cent, and the balance goes to the special rate pool. There is no cap on the amount.

Then the exclusions. The general first-year exclusions in section forty six apply. No first-year allowance in the final chargeable period of a qualifying activity. None for cars. None for plant bought for leasing, including hire, with one exception for an excluded lease of background plant in a building. None where the plant was first used for something else and later brought into the qualifying activity. And a separate anti-avoidance rule denies the allowance where the expenditure is incurred under disqualifying arrangements, broadly arrangements with a main purpose of securing the allowance.

Three conditions are tested again and again. Company, because full expensing is for corporation tax only. New and unused, because a second-hand asset, however modern, cannot get it. The May twenty twenty six paper turned on precisely this distinction, with new and second-hand plant side by side. And special rate, because a special rate asset gets fifty per cent, not one hundred. Integral features in a building, such as electrical and lighting systems, cold water systems, heating and air cooling, are special rate. So are thermal insulation, long-life assets and cars that are not main-rate cars.

The Act also stops a company having two bites. It cannot claim the annual investment allowance and a first-year allowance on the same expenditure, nor two first-year allowances. That forces a choice for each item, which we will come back to when we allocate the group's annual investment allowance. And the Act lets a company claim less than the full first-year allowance if it wants to. What is not claimed is relieved through the pool in the usual way.

In our invented case, Tarnmoor Engineering's purchases in Group Year Two sort like this. The new machining centres cost nine million pounds. They are new, main rate, used in the company's own trade, and bought by a company. Full expensing gives nine million pounds of allowances. The chillers and the new electrical system in the test hall cost one point two million pounds. They are new, but they are special rate. The fifty per cent allowance gives six hundred thousand pounds, and the other six hundred thousand pounds goes into the special rate pool, where it will draw six per cent a year.

An analogy helps here, and The Living Law used it. The main pool is a bath that drains at fourteen per cent a year. It never quite empties. Full expensing is different. It tips the whole bucket out at once. But a bucket emptied at once raises a question the bath never had to answer. What happens if you sell the bucket?

The receipt you hand back.

Here is the misconception this chapter exists to kill. Many candidates think that selling a fully expensed machine just reduces the main pool, as selling any old machine always did. It does not. A disposal of an asset that had full expensing triggers a special balancing charge.

The reason is simple. Under the pool system, a disposal value comes off the pool, and the clawback is as slow as the relief was. Full expensing gave the relief in a single year. If its sale proceeds merely went into the pool, the company would repay over many years a relief it enjoyed in one, and a company with a large old pool might never feel the proceeds at all. So Parliament built a charge that matches the speed of the relief.

The law, in section fifty nine A, says that where a company has had full expensing on expenditure on an asset and must bring a disposal value into account, it is liable to a balancing charge for that period. The charge is the relevant proportion of the disposal value. The relevant proportion is the expenditure that had full expensing, divided by the total expenditure on the asset that had any first-year allowance or was allocated to a pool. For a machine that was wholly full expensed, the proportion is one, and the whole disposal value is taxed at once. The company's disposal receipts for the pool are then reduced by the charge, so the same proceeds are not counted twice.

For a fifty per cent asset, section fifty nine B halves the proportion. Half the disposal value is a balancing charge at once. The other half comes off the special rate pool, which makes sense, because half the cost went into that pool in the first place.

Where an asset's cost was split, the disposal is split too. Suppose, for this example only, a company bought a new machine for five hundred thousand pounds, set two hundred thousand pounds against the annual investment allowance and claimed full expensing on the other three hundred thousand pounds. If it sells the machine for two hundred and fifty thousand pounds, the relevant proportion is three fifths. One hundred and fifty thousand pounds is a special balancing charge. One hundred thousand pounds comes off the pool. That split was the subject of a five mark part of a question in November twenty twenty four, and the examiners said it was the harder part.

Two more points complete the picture. As with any disposal value, the amount brought in cannot exceed the original cost. And there is a targeted anti-avoidance rule. Where arrangements have a main purpose of avoiding or reducing one of these special balancing charges, the charge is calculated as if the arrangements had not been made.

In our invented case the charge arrives in Group Year Three. On the first of October, Calder Valve Engineering sells the plant of its process-valve business to Tarnmoor Vallaria, the group's subsidiary in the invented country of Vallaria, for one point four million pounds. The price splits three ways. Nine hundred thousand pounds for machines that had full expensing. Two hundred thousand pounds for a heavy test bed, a long-life asset that had the fifty per cent allowance. Three hundred thousand pounds for older plant from the main pool. So Calder has a special balancing charge of nine hundred thousand pounds on the machines, and a further one hundred thousand pounds on the test bed, one million pounds in all, taxed at twenty five per cent in its nine-month period. The other one hundred thousand pounds for the test bed comes off the special rate pool, and the three hundred thousand pounds for the old plant comes off the main pool. Because the buyer is a connected company, the price must be an arm's length one, which is a transfer pricing question for chapter twenty seven.

The forty per cent allowance.

Full expensing has a hole in it, and the forty per cent allowance was designed to fill part of it. Plant bought for leasing is excluded from full expensing. So is anything bought by a business outside corporation tax. Before twenty twenty six, a company that bought new plant to lease out got only the annual investment allowance, if any was left, and then the main pool.

The law now adds section forty five U to the Capital Allowances Act, inserted by section twenty nine of the Finance Act twenty twenty six. It gives a forty per cent first-year allowance on expenditure incurred on or after the first of January, twenty twenty six, on plant or machinery that is unused and not second-hand, and that is not special rate expenditure. There is no requirement that the claimant be a company. The usual general exclusions apply, including the bar on cars, and there is a disqualifying arrangements rule in the same terms as for full expensing.

The important difference is leasing. The forty per cent allowance is available for plant provided for leasing, with conditions. The lessee must use the plant wholly or almost wholly to earn income within the charge to tax, or be resident in the United Kingdom and not use it to any significant extent to earn untaxed income from outside it. For this test, income relieved by double taxation relief counts as outside the charge. In plain terms, plant leased into the British tax net qualifies, and plant leased overseas does not.

What happens to the other sixty per cent? It goes into the main pool and draws writing-down allowances from the next period. And when the asset is sold, there is no special balancing charge. Section fifty nine A refers only to full expensing, so a forty per cent asset's proceeds simply come off the main pool, the old way.

Commencement needs care, because the allowance starts on a calendar date, not at the start of a period. Take a real-calendar example. A leasing company with a year ending on the thirty first of March, twenty twenty six, buys a new machine for leasing to a British customer. If the obligation to pay became unconditional on the twentieth of December, twenty twenty five, there is no first-year allowance at all. If it became unconditional on the fifth of January, twenty twenty six, the company has forty per cent. The rule on when expenditure is incurred decides which.

In our invented case, Tarnmoor Engineering buys new test rigs for eight hundred thousand pounds in Group Year Two and hires them out to utility customers in the United Kingdom on two-year hires. Because they are for leasing, full expensing is not available. Because they are new, main rate and leased to customers who use them in their taxed businesses, the forty per cent allowance is. That gives three hundred and twenty thousand pounds now, and four hundred and eighty thousand pounds goes into the main pool. The hires are short, so Tarnmoor Engineering, as lessor, keeps the allowances. Had the rigs been leased on long, finance-type terms, the long funding lease rules could have moved the allowances to the customer. That story belongs to chapter nine.

A useful way to remember the three first-year allowances is by who they are for. Full expensing is for companies investing in their own new main-rate plant. The fifty per cent allowance is its special rate companion. The forty per cent allowance is for everyone else's new main-rate plant, including plant leased into the United Kingdom.

Fourteen per cent, and the hybrid rate.

Section twenty eight of the Finance Act twenty twenty six cuts the main rate writing-down allowance from eighteen to fourteen per cent, for corporation tax chargeable periods beginning on or after the first of April, twenty twenty six. For income tax the date is the sixth of April. The special rate stays at six per cent.

A period that straddles the first of April, twenty twenty six, uses a hybrid rate. The formula weights the old and new rates by days. Eighteen per cent is multiplied by the days before the first of April, divided by the days in the period. Fourteen per cent is multiplied by the days from the first of April onwards, divided by the days in the period. The two are added, and the result is rounded up to two decimal places. Rounded up, not to the nearest, and days, not months.

Three real-calendar examples show how it works. A company with a year from the first of January to the thirty first of December, twenty twenty six, has ninety days at the old rate and two hundred and seventy five at the new. Its rate is fourteen point nine nine per cent. A year from the first of October, twenty twenty five, to the thirtieth of September, twenty twenty six, splits one hundred and eighty two days to one hundred and eighty three, which gives fifteen point nine nine four five per cent, rounded up to sixteen per cent exactly. A year from the first of July, twenty twenty five, to the thirtieth of June, twenty twenty six, gives seventeen point zero one per cent. On a main pool of one million pounds, the first company's allowance is one hundred and forty nine thousand, nine hundred pounds.

Short periods add another layer. The writing-down allowance is reduced in proportion for a chargeable period of less than a year. Calder's nine-month period in Group Year Three gets nine twelfths of fourteen per cent, which is ten and a half per cent. The annual investment allowance is reduced in the same proportion, so a nine-month period has a maximum of seven hundred and fifty thousand pounds.

The rest of the pool mechanics are as The Living Law taught them. A pool of one thousand pounds or less can be written off in full, and a balancing allowance on the main pool arises only when the qualifying activity ends.

In our invented group, every story period is simply run on the financial year twenty twenty six rules, so Tarnmoor's pools use fourteen per cent with no hybrid rate. Tarnmoor Engineering's main pool shows why the rate cut matters to a large group. It brings forward thirty nine point five million pounds of old expenditure, most of it bought before full expensing existed. Add the five hundred thousand pounds for the second-hand milling line and deduct three hundred thousand pounds of proceeds from old plant, and the pool stands at thirty nine point seven million pounds. Fourteen per cent is five point five five eight million pounds. At eighteen per cent it would have been more than seven million pounds. That gap is the policy paper's historic pool, in the flesh.

One annual investment allowance for the whole group.

The annual investment allowance gives one hundred per cent relief on up to one million pounds a year of qualifying expenditure. For a company, it is less dramatic than it once was, because full expensing already gives one hundred per cent on new main-rate plant with no cap. But the annual investment allowance still matters, because it covers ground full expensing does not. Second-hand plant. Special rate expenditure, where it beats the fifty per cent allowance. Fixtures bought with a second-hand building. Cars are excluded, and so is expenditure in the period the qualifying activity ends.

The group rule is the Advanced Technical point. The law, in section fifty one C, says that where a company is a parent undertaking of other companies in a financial year, the parent and its subsidiaries are entitled to only one annual investment allowance between them, for their chargeable periods ending in that financial year. Which financial year? The section does not define it, or borrow the Companies Act meaning. So, on this book's reading with the Interpretation Act nineteen seventy eight, it is the year to the thirty first of March, not the parent's accounting year. Parent undertaking has its Companies Act meaning. A company counts as a subsidiary for these purposes if the parent holds that status at the end of the subsidiary's chargeable period. Separate rules, in sections fifty one D and fifty one E, give a single allowance to companies under common control that are related to each other, a test the Act spells out. Within the group, the companies allocate the single allowance as they think fit.

Two consequences matter for a group that buys companies and changes year ends. First, a target's period that ended before the acquisition is outside the group. In our invented case, Calder's year to the thirty first of March of Group Year One ended the day before Tarnmoor bought it, so Calder kept its own full allowance for that year. Its next year, to the thirty first of March of Group Year Two, ended while Tarnmoor was its parent, so it shares that allowance year with the other companies' Group Year One periods. Second, a change of year end can move a company into a new allowance year. Calder's year to the thirty first of March of Group Year Three shares the allowance with the other companies' Group Year Two periods. Its nine-month period to the thirty first of December of Group Year Three falls in the next allowance year, with their Group Year Three periods, and is capped at seven hundred and fifty thousand pounds on its own. So Calder's two periods ending in Group Year Three draw on different allowances.

So where should the allowance go? Every Tarnmoor company pays the main rate, so the question is purely one of timing. The allowance adds most relief this year where no first-year allowance is available at all, and most of all where the alternative is the slow special rate pool. In our invented case, for the year to the thirty first of March of Group Year Three, the group gives four hundred thousand pounds to Tarnmoor Estates, for the fixtures in the office it bought in Group Year Two. They are second-hand integral features, so special rate, with no first-year allowance. It gives the other six hundred thousand pounds to Calder, for second-hand special rate plant bought in its year to the thirty first of March of Group Year Three. Tarnmoor Engineering gets none. Its new plant has full expensing or the fifty per cent allowance, and its second-hand milling line is main rate, drawing fourteen per cent a year. On the Tarnmoor Estates and Calder items, the allowance replaces only six per cent a year, so special rate goes first. Calder's earlier six hundred thousand pounds of second-hand plant, in its year to the thirty first of March of Group Year Two, used the previous year's allowance.

The allocation is a group decision that must reach each company's return, so a sensible group records it centrally each year.

Cars and long-life assets.

Cars have their own regime, because a car is the asset most likely to be used privately. A car, for these purposes, excludes motorcycles, vans and vehicles not commonly used or suitable for private use. A main-rate car has carbon dioxide emissions of no more than fifty grams per kilometre, or is electric. Every other car is special rate. Cars get no annual investment allowance, no full expensing, no fifty per cent and no forty per cent allowance. The one exception is a new zero-emission car, which has a one hundred per cent first-year allowance for expenditure up to the thirty first of March, twenty twenty seven, for corporation tax. That date is coming next, and a group planning a fleet change should watch it.

The exam uses cars to test sorting. A van is not a car, so a new van can be fully expensed. A new car at one hundred and twenty grams per kilometre goes to the special rate pool and draws six per cent. On a forty thousand pound car, that is two thousand four hundred pounds in the first year. A car on hire purchase follows the same sorting, with the hire purchase timing rule we will come to shortly.

Long-life assets are the quieter trap. A long-life asset is plant with an expected useful economic life, when new, of at least twenty five years. Long-life asset expenditure is special rate expenditure. There is a de minimis. A company's long-life asset expenditure in a chargeable period is not treated as such if the total is within a monetary limit of one hundred thousand pounds. But for a company that limit is divided by one plus the number of associated companies, reduced for a period shorter than a year, and never increased for a longer one. And the test is all or nothing. If the period's long-life spending exceeds the limit, all of it is long-life expenditure, not just the excess.

For a large group, the de minimis all but vanishes. In Group Year Two each company in our invented group has nine associated companies, so its limit is one hundred thousand pounds divided by ten, which is ten thousand pounds. Suppose, for this example only, that Tarnmoor Engineering had also bought a new heat-treatment furnace for three hundred thousand pounds, with a thirty-year life. It would be a long-life asset, so special rate. That changes the first-year allowance from one hundred per cent to fifty. One hundred and fifty thousand pounds now, and the other one hundred and fifty thousand pounds into the special rate pool at six per cent. The wrong classification overstates first-year relief by one hundred and fifty thousand pounds on one asset.

The examiner hides long-life assets in a passing sentence about useful lives. The candidate who spots one, and divides the limit correctly, collects marks others never see.

Short-life assets and software.

The short-life asset election lets a company keep an asset out of the main pool in its own single asset pool. If the asset is sold or scrapped for less than its written down value, the company gets a balancing allowance at once, instead of leaving the unrelieved cost to drain from the pool for decades. For corporation tax the election must be made within two years after the end of the chargeable period in which the expenditure was incurred, and it is irrevocable. If the asset is still held at the eighth anniversary of the end of that period, its balance is transferred to the main pool. The election is not available for cars, special rate expenditure, or plant for leasing, with limited exceptions.

In a world of full expensing, the short-life asset election matters less for companies than it used to. A new main-rate asset that is fully expensed has nothing left to relieve. The election earns its keep on assets that cannot be fully expensed. Second-hand computer equipment and other used plant beyond the annual investment allowance are the usual candidates. The cost of the election is that a sale above written down value produces a balancing charge.

Computer software has two homes in the corporation tax code, and a company must know which it is in. The Capital Allowances Act treats capital expenditure on software, or on a right to use it, as expenditure on plant. If the company later grants a sub-licence for a capital sum, that sum is a disposal value. But most company software also falls within the intangible fixed assets regime in Part eight of the two thousand and nine Act, which chapter eleven explains. The two are reconciled like this. Software that is treated in the accounts as part of the cost of the related hardware is outside the intangibles regime, so it stays with capital allowances automatically. For other capital software, the company may elect, under section eight hundred and fifteen of the two thousand and nine Act, for the expenditure to be dealt with under capital allowances instead. H M R C's guidance says the election must be in writing, identify the expenditure, and be made within two years after the end of the accounting period in which the expenditure was incurred. It cannot be revoked.

Why elect? Because capital allowances may give faster relief than amortisation in the accounts, which the intangibles regime generally follows. For a group implementing a new enterprise system, the choice is worth a line in the board paper.

Hire purchase and plant provided by lessees.

Ownership is the gateway to allowances, and hire purchase seems to fail it. Under a hire purchase contract the hirer does not own the asset until the last instalment and the option fee are paid. The Capital Allowances Act solves this by deeming. A person who incurs capital expenditure under a contract providing that it shall or may become the owner of plant on performing the contract is treated as the owner while entitled to the benefit of the contract.

The timing rule is the exam point. When the plant is brought into use, all the capital expenditure still to be incurred under the contract is treated as incurred at that time. So the whole capital element goes into the computation at once, not instalment by instalment. The interest element is not capital expenditure at all. It is a finance cost, relieved as such over the life of the contract. In May twenty twenty six, in a question with hire purchase vans, the examiners named the capital element as one of the errors candidates made.

There is one modern twist. Where a contract would be treated as a lease under generally accepted accounting practice, the hire purchase rule applies only if it is accounted for as a finance lease, or, for a lessee that recognises a right-of-use asset, would be. That keeps ordinary operating leases out, and leaves long leases of plant to the long funding lease rules in chapter nine.

So the sorting questions for a hire purchase asset are the usual ones, applied when it is brought into use. Is it new, and main rate? Then full expensing is in point for a company. Is it second-hand? Then the annual investment allowance or the pool. Is it a car? Then the car rules, and a car above fifty grams per kilometre goes straight to the special rate pool, as the hotel question in May twenty twenty four expected.

The Act deals with a mirror-image problem too. A lessee is sometimes required by its lease to provide plant, perhaps machinery installed in a leased unit that is not a fixture. Without help, the lessee would have spent the money on an asset it does not own. The law treats the lessee as the owner while it uses the plant for its qualifying activity, and brings in no disposal value when the lease ends. The lessor has to bring in a disposal value if it later disposes of the plant.

Part use and business entertainment.

Allowances are for plant used in the qualifying activity. When an asset is used partly for something else, the Act cuts the allowances back. A first-year allowance or an annual investment allowance is reduced to an amount that is just and reasonable, having regard to the extent of use for other purposes. The asset goes into its own single asset pool, so that writing-down allowances and any balancing adjustment can be reduced in the same way.

For companies, the common case of private use by employees is handled differently in practice. H M R C's view, in its Capital Allowances Manual, is that where an employer provides an asset to a director or employee as part of the remuneration package, the cost is accepted as incurred for the business's qualifying activity, and the company's claim is not restricted. The employee is taxed on the benefit instead. The manual adds a caution. Where the asset is wildly out of line with the business's commercial needs, H M R C may restrict allowances on the ground of personal choice, citing an old case, G H Chambers, Northiam Farms, Limited versus Watmough.

Business entertainment is the other side of the same coin, and it has its own section. The law treats use of plant for business entertainment as use that is not for the qualifying activity. Business entertainment includes hospitality of any kind provided in connection with the business. So a corporate hospitality boat, a box at a sports ground, or a marquee kept for customer events earns allowances only for the proportion of use that is not entertaining. Two exceptions narrow the rule. Entertainment is not restricted where providing it is the trade itself, as with a hotel or an events company. And hospitality provided only for staff is not caught. Directors count as staff for this purpose.

Suppose, for this example only, that Tarnmoor bought a launch on a lake near its headquarters and used it on sixty days a year, forty for customer days and twenty for staff summer events. The customer days are business entertainment, so the allowances would be reduced to a just and reasonable proportion, here something like a third of the full figure, in a single asset pool. It is the same principle that disallows the cost of customer entertaining in the profit computation, which chapter seven covered. The plant used to entertain is caught as well.

Read the facts. A hotel's televisions are used in a trade whose function is hospitality, so they are not restricted. The same televisions in a manufacturer's hospitality suite are.

Giving effect, claiming, and value added tax adjustments.

Allowances are worth nothing until they reach a computation. The law gives effect to them according to the qualifying activity. For a trade, the allowance is treated as an expense and a balancing charge as a receipt in calculating the trade's profits. That is why capital allowances can create or increase a trading loss, which can then be carried back, carried forward or surrendered as group relief. For a property business, the same rule applies to the property business's profits. For a company with investment business, the allowances for plant used in managing its investments are deducted from that business's income, with any excess treated as management expenses. So Tarnmoor plc's allowances on its head office equipment add to its excess management expenses, which, in our invented case, it surrenders to Tarnmoor Engineering, as chapters thirteen and fifteen explain.

A company claims capital allowances in its company tax return. That is the rule in the company return rules in Schedule eighteen to the Finance Act nineteen ninety eight. A claim can be made, amended or withdrawn only by amending the return. The general time limit is the first anniversary of the filing date, in practice two years after the end of a twelve-month period, with later dates where H M R C opens an enquiry, including thirty days after the enquiry is completed.

That enquiry limb produced a case with a twist. In H M R C versus Dundas Heritable Limited, decided by the Upper Tribunal in twenty nineteen, a pub company had filed its returns years late, with capital allowance claims inside them. H M R C opened enquiries into the returns and then disallowed the claims as out of time. The tribunal held that the opening of the enquiries had brought the late claims within the time limit, under the enquiry limb of the rule. H M R C's own enquiry had cured the lateness. The lesson for a tax function is not to rely on a lucky enquiry. It is that the claim window is set by statute and the claim lives in the return, so a group that wants to change its claims, for example to claim less in order to preserve losses, must do it within the window.

Value added tax adjustments are the one non-core row. Where a business that has bought plant later incurs an additional value added tax liability on it, for example under the capital goods scheme, the extra amount is treated as further qualifying expenditure in the period it accrues. It attracts the annual investment allowance or a first-year allowance if the original expenditure did. An additional value added tax rebate is the reverse. It is a disposal value, or added to one. The capital goods scheme thresholds changed from the twenty ninth of July, twenty twenty six, and computer equipment was removed from the scheme. The principle is the one to learn. Allowances follow the net cost the business bears.

Tarnmoor's sixteen million pounds.

Now Tarnmoor Engineering's Group Year Two, in our invented case, put together. The full working, in columns, is in the reading edition.

First, the first-year allowances. Full expensing on the new machining centres, nine million pounds. The fifty per cent allowance on the one point two million pounds of chillers and electrical systems, six hundred thousand pounds, with six hundred thousand pounds carried to the special rate pool. The forty per cent allowance on the eight hundred thousand pounds of leased test rigs, three hundred and twenty thousand pounds, with four hundred and eighty thousand pounds carried to the main pool.

Second, the main pool. Thirty nine point five million pounds brought forward. Add five hundred thousand pounds for the second-hand milling line. Deduct three hundred thousand pounds of proceeds from old plant, none of which had full expensing, so no special balancing charge arises. The pool is thirty nine point seven million pounds, and fourteen per cent is five point five five eight million pounds. Only then does the four hundred and eighty thousand pound balance from the test rigs join the pool, because the law keeps a first-year balance out of the pool until the next period. That leaves thirty four point six two two million pounds to carry forward.

Third, the special rate pool. Seven point seven two million pounds brought forward. Six per cent is four hundred and sixty three thousand, two hundred pounds. The six hundred thousand pound balance from the chillers then joins, leaving seven point eight five six eight million pounds.

Fourth, a building. The company also claims structures and buildings allowance of fifty eight thousand, eight hundred pounds on a factory extension that cost one point nine six million pounds. Chapter nine explains that allowance.

Add the columns, and the total is exactly sixteen million pounds. Chapter seven took Tarnmoor Engineering's tax-adjusted trading profit before capital allowances of forty two million pounds. After these allowances, its trading profit is twenty six million pounds. At twenty five per cent, sixteen million pounds of allowances are worth four million pounds of tax.

Notice what the computation does not contain. No annual investment allowance, because the group allocated it to special rate spending in Tarnmoor Estates and Calder. No special balancing charge. And no depreciation, which chapter seven added back. Notice also how much came from the sorting. If the machining centres had been second-hand, the nine million pounds of full expensing would have been one point two six million pounds of writing-down allowance. Tom's question about every line was worth more than seven million pounds of relief in the first year.

A year later, Calder's sale to Tarnmoor Vallaria runs the sorting in reverse. One million pounds of special balancing charges, and two hundred and fifty thousand pounds of tax, fall in the same nine-month period in which Calder bears the cost of closing the division where Dan Hartley worked. The relief came fast, and so does the clawback.

Planning, and how the examiner tests it.

The planning in this area is mostly about timing, sorting and records, and it starts before the purchase order is signed.

Timing first. Expenditure is incurred when the obligation to pay becomes unconditional, so a purchase signed just before a year end can bring a full year's relief forward, subject to the four-month rule and the anti-avoidance rule against artificially early obligations. For a very large company paying quarterly instalments, relief in the current year also reduces the instalments still to come, so the tax function must update its forecast as soon as a big purchase is committed.

Sorting second. Procurement teams should know which features of a purchase change its tax treatment. New rather than second-hand. Main rate rather than special rate. Bought outright rather than leased. A useful life of twenty five years or more. A group that buys reconditioned plant to save cash may lose full expensing worth far more than the saving.

Allocation third. The group's single annual investment allowance should be allocated deliberately each financial year, to expenditure that has no first-year allowance, special rate first. Full expensing takes care of the rest.

Fourth, the decision not to claim. Allowances are not compulsory. A company that is loss-making, or whose losses would be caught by the restriction on carried-forward losses above the five million pound deductions allowance, may do better to claim less now and keep the expenditure in the pool for later years. In a group, the answer depends on whether the allowances could instead create a loss for group relief. And because the claim lives in the return, any change must be made within the statutory window.

Fifth, interactions. Capital allowances are left out of tax E B I T D A, meaning earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, for the corporate interest restriction, so full expensing does not reduce a group's interest capacity, as chapter twenty eight explains. Full expensing creates large deferred tax liabilities, because the tax written down value falls far below the book value, as chapter six explains. And a disposal of fully expensed plant within the group, or across a border as Calder's was, needs a transfer pricing check and a special balancing charge in the same breath.

Finally, records. Every one of these answers depends on the fixed asset register. A large group's tax function should tag each addition as new or second-hand, main or special rate, owned, hired or leased out, with its useful life and claim history, so that a disposal ten years later can be sorted without archaeology.

Capital allowances appear at nearly every sitting of this paper. There have been standalone fifteen mark questions, on a hotel in May twenty twenty four and on a warehouse, new and second-hand plant and hire purchase vans in May twenty twenty six. And allowances are embedded in almost every full computation, from the balancing charge on fully expensed plant in May twenty twenty three to the disposal split in November twenty twenty four.

Each item is worth half a mark to a mark, for the right treatment and a short reason, and the examiners have asked more than once for a conventional layout, with columns for first-year allowances, the main pool, the special rate pool, any single asset pools and the allowances.

The traps are consistent. Second-hand plant cannot be fully expensed. The whole capital element of hire purchase goes in when the asset is brought into use. Cars are excluded from the annual investment allowance and the first-year allowances, except new zero-emission cars. Disposals of fully expensed plant give a balancing charge, not a pool deduction. The long-life asset limit is divided by the associated companies. And one trap belongs to the twenty twenty seven sittings in particular. The tax tables published for twenty twenty six show the eighteen per cent rate. For a paper set on the Finance Act twenty twenty six, the main rate is fourteen per cent, with a forty per cent first-year allowance alongside. Check the twenty twenty seven tables when they appear.

What to take away.

The tax code ignores depreciation and gives statutory allowances instead, and every allowance depends on sorting. New or used. Main or special rate. Owned, hired or leased. Kept or sold.

Full expensing gives a company one hundred per cent on new main-rate plant, and fifty per cent on new special rate plant, with the balance in the special rate pool. It is permanent, and barred for second-hand plant, cars, plant for leasing and the final period of a qualifying activity.

When fully expensed plant is sold, there is a special balancing charge equal to the disposal value times the share of the cost that was fully expensed. For a fifty per cent asset, half the disposal value is a charge and half comes off the special rate pool.

The forty per cent first-year allowance applies from the first of January, twenty twenty six, to new main-rate plant, for any business, including plant leased into the United Kingdom but not overseas, with the other sixty per cent in the main pool and no special balancing charge.

The main rate writing-down allowance is fourteen per cent for chargeable periods beginning on or after the first of April, twenty twenty six. Straddling periods use a day-weighted hybrid rate, rounded up to two decimal places. A calendar twenty twenty six year gives fourteen point nine nine per cent. The special rate stays at six per cent.

A group gets one annual investment allowance of one million pounds, for its companies' chargeable periods ending in the same financial year, the year to the thirty first of March, allocated as it thinks fit. Put it where no first-year allowance is available, special rate first.

Cars over fifty grams per kilometre are special rate, and cars get no annual investment allowance or first-year allowance, except new zero-emission cars until the thirty first of March, twenty twenty seven. Long-life assets are special rate, with a one hundred thousand pound limit divided by the number of associated companies. Short-life asset elections are due within two years. Software is plant, but most company software sits in the intangibles regime unless the company elects out within two years.

Hire purchase puts the whole capital element in when the asset is brought into use. Part use and business entertainment reduce allowances to a just and reasonable amount. Allowances are claimed in the return, within the statutory window.

In our invented case, Tarnmoor Engineering's allowances for Group Year Two total sixteen million pounds, nine million of it full expensing, and fifty eight thousand, eight hundred pounds of it structures and buildings allowance. Calder's sale of process-valve plant a year later produced one million pounds of special balancing charges.

The structures and buildings allowance in that total is the bridge to the next chapter. Plant is the apparatus. Chapter nine turns to the setting itself, the buildings and the fixtures inside them, and to what happens to allowances when assets are leased or a whole trade changes hands.

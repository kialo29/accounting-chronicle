Chapter six: Deferred tax and the tax charge.

After the November twenty twenty five sitting of this paper, the examiners wrote a sentence that every candidate should hear before the exam. A question had asked for the total tax charge in the profit and loss account, current and deferred. In the examiners' words, only a small number of candidates calculated the deferred tax charge. Most wrote about balances, and many forgot whole categories of difference.

So here is the question this chapter answers. Why does a company that owes H M R C seven hundred and fifty thousand pounds report a tax charge of nine hundred and fifty thousand pounds? And why is the higher figure, not the lower one, the honest number for a shareholder to read?

This is the second chapter of Part Two, on measuring profit. Chapter five showed that taxable profit starts from the accounts. This chapter runs the other way, and shows how the accounts report the tax. Deferred tax is a core topic on the Taxation of Larger Companies and Groups paper. The Living Law signposted deferred tax but never taught it, so we start from first principles and go to Advanced Technical depth.

Two numbers for one year.

Every company's profit and loss account carries one line for tax. Behind that line sit two numbers. The first is current tax. That is the corporation tax the company expects to pay H M R C for the period, computed on taxable total profits, plus any adjustment for earlier periods. The second is deferred tax. That is an accounting measure of the tax effects of items that the accounts and the tax computation recognise in different periods.

Add the two together and you have the total tax charge, the number analysts divide by profit before tax and the examiner asks you to compute.

Our running case is the invented Tarnmoor group, a listed engineering group whose companies, people and figures are all made up for teaching. In Group Year One, Tarnmoor bought Calder Valve Engineering, the invented manufacturer from The Living Law. Calder kept its thirty first of March year end. Its first year end inside the group is the thirty first of March of Group Year Two.

For that year, in our invented case, Calder's taxable total profits are three million pounds. At twenty five per cent, its corporation tax is seven hundred and fifty thousand pounds. Its research and development expenditure credit of four hundred thousand pounds is set off against that liability, so the cash it actually sends H M R C is three hundred and fifty thousand pounds. Yet Calder's accounts show a tax charge of nine hundred and fifty thousand pounds.

Here is the misconception this chapter sets out to kill. Many people think deferred tax is tax you owe H M R C, a hidden liability the taxman will one day demand. It is not. H M R C never assesses deferred tax and never refunds a deferred tax asset. Deferred tax exists only in the accounts. It measures how today's transactions will change tomorrow's tax bills.

The gap between the two figures is two hundred thousand pounds of deferred tax. By the end of this chapter you will be able to build it from the ground up.

This chapter applies the law for the financial year twenty twenty six, with a main rate of twenty five per cent.

Why the accounts need deferred tax.

Accounting rests on matching. Income and the costs of earning it belong in the same period. Tax is a cost of earning profit. So the tax charge should broadly follow the profit that produced it.

Corporation tax does not cooperate. The tax computation recognises many items in a different year from the accounts. A machine is depreciated over ten years in the accounts but written off in one year for tax under full expensing. A bonus is charged in the accounts this year but deducted for tax only when paid, if payment comes more than nine months after the year end. A pension contribution accrued this year is deducted for tax only when paid. If the tax line showed only current tax, the reported tax rate would swing wildly from year to year for reasons that have nothing to do with how profitable the company really is.

Picture deferred tax as the bill in the post. The timing difference has already happened. The profit has been earned, or the cost has been spent. The tax consequence has not yet landed, but it is on its way, and its amount is known with reasonable confidence. A prudent householder who knows the gas bill is in the post does not pretend the winter was free. The accounts record the bill when the gas was burned, not when the envelope arrives.

Now the precise rule. A deferred tax liability records tax that will become payable in future periods because of differences that exist at the balance sheet date. A deferred tax asset records tax that will be saved in future periods. The movement in those balances during the year is the deferred tax charge or credit. It sits in profit or loss when the underlying item went through profit or loss, and in other comprehensive income or equity when the underlying item went there.

That last point matters for the examiner. The deferred tax charge is a movement, not a balance. The November twenty twenty five question asked for both, the charge and the closing balance. Always compute the opening position, the closing position, and the difference between them.

There is a cash story here too. A large deferred tax liability tells a reader that the company has paid less tax so far than its profits suggest, and will pay more later. Lenders and analysts read the deferred tax note for exactly that reason.

Temporary differences and timing differences.

There are two accounting languages for deferred tax in the United Kingdom, and a group tax adviser must speak both.

The first is International Accounting Standard twelve, Income Taxes, which practitioners call I A S twelve. A listed group such as Tarnmoor prepares its consolidated accounts under international standards, so I A S twelve governs the group accounts. A subsidiary that uses the reduced disclosure framework, Financial Reporting Standard one oh one, also measures its tax under I A S twelve, because that framework takes international recognition and measurement with lighter disclosure. Chapter five explained Calder's move to it.

I A S twelve looks at the balance sheet. For each asset and liability, it compares the carrying amount in the accounts with the tax base, which is broadly the amount that will be deductible for tax in future. The gap is a temporary difference. If an asset is carried at more than its tax base, recovering it will generate taxable profit, so there is a taxable temporary difference and a deferred tax liability. If an asset is carried at less than its tax base, or a liability is carried that will be deductible when paid, there is a deductible temporary difference and potentially a deferred tax asset.

The second language is Financial Reporting Standard one oh two, Section twenty nine. Calder used it until the thirty first of March of Group Year Two. It looks at the profit and loss account instead. It recognises deferred tax on timing differences, which are gains and losses recognised in the accounts in a different period from the tax computation. It then adds some items that are not strictly timing differences, such as deferred tax on fair value adjustments in a business combination and on revaluations. The Association of Chartered Certified Accountants calls this the timing differences plus approach.

Both languages ignore permanent differences. A regulatory fine is never deductible. Depreciation on a building that qualifies for no allowance is never relieved against income. Those items change the effective tax rate permanently, and no deferred tax arises on them.

For most items in a trading company, the two languages give the same answer. Plant, unpaid bonuses and pension accruals work the same way in both. The languages part company mainly at the edges, such as share schemes and the initial recognition of some assets.

For the exam, read which standard the question names. A new company reporting under international standards, as in the May twenty twenty six deferred tax question, is an I A S twelve question. Use its vocabulary, carrying amount and tax base.

Liabilities always, assets only when probable.

The recognition rules are deliberately asymmetrical. Under I A S twelve, a deferred tax liability is recognised for all taxable temporary differences, subject to a short list of exceptions. Under Section twenty nine the same is true of timing differences. Liabilities are not optional. A company cannot decline to book one because it hopes the difference will never reverse. The examiners' report on the May twenty twenty six sitting made the same point. Liabilities are always provided.

Assets are different. A deferred tax asset is recognised only to the extent that it is probable that taxable profit will be available to absorb the deduction. Section twenty nine applies the same idea. That is a judgement, and it is where the auditors spend their time.

I A S twelve gives two sources of comfort. The first is taxable temporary differences. If the same company has deferred tax liabilities, owed to the same tax authority, that will reverse in the same periods as the deductible differences, or in periods to which a loss can be carried, those reversals will create the taxable profit the asset needs. That is the point the May twenty twenty six examiners wanted. A tax loss can be recognised as an asset to offset a liability from accelerated capital allowances.

The second source is forecast profit. Here the standard is stern. Its guidance says the existence of unused tax losses is strong evidence that future taxable profit may not be available. A company with a recent history of losses needs convincing other evidence, not hope.

Three exceptions to the liability rule matter for this paper. First, no deferred tax liability is recognised on the initial recognition of goodwill. Second, I A S twelve has an initial recognition exception for some assets and liabilities acquired outside a business combination where the transaction affects neither accounting nor taxable profit. In May twenty twenty one the International Accounting Standards Board narrowed that exception, for annual periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty twenty three, so that it no longer applies where a single transaction creates equal taxable and deductible differences. Leases are the main example. A lessee now books deferred tax on both the right-of-use asset and the lease liability. Third, the Pillar Two exception, which has its own section later.

Two measurement rules complete the picture. Deferred tax is never discounted, however far away the reversal. And deferred tax assets and liabilities may be offset in the balance sheet only where they relate to the same tax authority and there is a legal right to set them off.

Which rate, and when.

Deferred tax is measured at the rate expected to apply when the difference reverses, but only using rates and laws enacted or substantively enacted by the balance sheet date. Both standards use that test.

In the United Kingdom, a rate is substantively enacted when the bill containing it has completed its passage through the House of Commons. The logic is constitutional. Once the Commons has finished with a Finance Bill, the House of Lords can debate a tax rate but cannot change it. A resolution with statutory effect under the Provisional Collection of Taxes Act nineteen sixty eight also counts.

The best real illustration came in twenty twenty one. The government announced that the main rate would rise from nineteen per cent to twenty five per cent from the first of April, twenty twenty three. The Finance Bill completed its Commons stages on the twenty fourth of May, twenty twenty one, and received Royal Assent on the tenth of June. Groups with a balance sheet date on or after the twenty fourth of May had to remeasure their deferred tax at the new rate, to the extent the differences were expected to reverse after the first of April, twenty twenty three. A deferred tax liability built up at nineteen per cent jumped by almost a third overnight, and the increase went straight through the tax charge.

For the financial year twenty twenty six, the position is calm. The Finance Act twenty twenty six, which received Royal Assent on the eighteenth of March, twenty twenty six, sets the main rate at twenty five per cent for the financial year twenty twenty seven, with the small profits rate at nineteen per cent and the marginal relief fraction at three two hundredths. So for a large company, twenty five per cent is enacted for every reversal the examiner is likely to set you. Use it, and say why.

A company expecting to pay at the small profits rate, or within marginal relief, measures at the rate it expects to pay. Every Tarnmoor company pays the main rate, so the point never arises in our story.

The Finance Act twenty twenty six also cut the main pool writing down allowance from eighteen to fourteen per cent. That does not remeasure existing deferred tax, because the rate of tax has not changed. It only changes how quickly differences reverse.

The fixed asset difference.

In most trading companies, the largest deferred tax balance comes from capital allowances. Take the net book value of the assets that qualify for capital allowances, and deduct their tax written down value. The difference, at twenty five per cent, is the deferred tax liability.

The trap is the word qualifying. Land never attracts capital allowances, so including it in net book value creates a phantom difference. The May twenty twenty five examiners reported that some candidates did exactly that. A building that qualifies for no allowance is also outside the comparison, because its depreciation is never relieved. Buildings that attract the structures and buildings allowance need more care, and practice on them varies. The suggested answer to the November twenty twenty five question stripped out land and buildings before comparing net book value with tax written down value. Do the same.

Full expensing makes this difference large. In our invented case, Tarnmoor Engineering, the group's main trading company, claims full expensing of nine million pounds on new plant in Group Year Two. Its current tax falls by two million two hundred and fifty thousand pounds. But the plant still sits in the accounts at close to cost, while its tax base is nil. Ignoring depreciation in the year for simplicity, the deferred tax liability rises by the same two million two hundred and fifty thousand pounds. The total tax charge does not move.

That is the most important insight in this chapter for a board conversation. Full expensing is a cash benefit, not a reduction in the effective tax rate. What the company gains is time, and the value of money over that time.

The newer forty per cent first-year allowance for main rate plant, available from the first of January, twenty twenty six, works the same way on a smaller scale. On Tarnmoor Engineering's eight hundred thousand pounds of test rigs leased to customers, the allowance is three hundred and twenty thousand pounds, and four hundred and eighty thousand pounds goes into the main pool. The tax base falls faster than the book value, so a liability builds.

Differences reverse as well as arise. When a fully expensed asset is sold, the special balancing charge brings the tax back into current tax. A company that keeps investing may see its liability grow for years, which is why it is tempting, and wrong, to think it will never be paid.

Short-term differences.

The second family of differences is short-term. These are the ones the examiners say candidates forget, so learn the list.

Unpaid remuneration comes first. Under section twelve eighty eight of the Corporation Tax Act two thousand and nine, which I will call the two thousand and nine Act, remuneration charged in the accounts but unpaid nine months after the end of the period is deductible only in the period when it is paid. A bonus or a cash long-term incentive plan accrual that will be paid later creates a deductible difference, and a deferred tax asset.

Pension contributions are next. Under section one hundred and ninety six of the Finance Act two thousand and four, an employer's contributions to a registered pension scheme are deducted when paid, not when accrued. An accrual at the year end creates a deferred tax asset. Where the spreading rules apply to an unusually large contribution, the part not yet deducted is also a deductible difference.

Employee benefit contributions, such as payments into an employee benefit trust, are deductible only when the trust provides qualifying benefits, within the time limits chapter seven explains. Until then there is a deductible difference.

General provisions are a further source. A provision that tax does not allow until the expenditure is incurred, or until a loss is specifically identified, creates a deductible difference. By contrast, a specific provision that tax allows as soon as it is made creates no difference at all, because the accounts and the computation move together. Ask in each case whether the tax deduction follows the accounting entry. Where it does, there is no deferred tax. Where it lags, there is an asset.

The direction of each item is worth fixing in your mind. Accelerated allowances produce liabilities, because tax relief has come early. Accrued costs that tax relieves later produce assets, because tax relief is still to come. If you find yourself creating a liability from an unpaid bonus, stop and think again. The May twenty twenty six examiners reported candidates confusing assets with liabilities.

These assets are usually easy to recognise. A profitable company paying its bonuses and pension contributions next year will plainly have the taxable profit to absorb the deductions. Recognition becomes a live judgement mainly for losses, which come shortly.

One more source of difference connects to chapter five. Under the lease spreading rules, a lessee's transition adjustment on moving to on balance sheet lease accounting is spread over the weighted average remaining lease term. The unspread balance is a short-term difference too.

Calder's first year end in the group.

Now we can build Calder's numbers, all invented, for its year to the thirty first of March of Group Year Two. Calder still reports under Financial Reporting Standard one oh two this year. Its move to the reduced disclosure framework takes effect from the first of April of Group Year Two, so it does not touch these balances.

Start with fixed assets. The net book value of Calder's qualifying plant and machinery is fourteen million pounds. The tax written down value of its pools is eight point four million pounds. The difference is five point six million pounds. At twenty five per cent, the deferred tax liability is one point four million pounds.

Now the short-term differences. Calder has accrued six hundred thousand pounds for a long-term incentive plan that will not be paid within nine months, and two hundred thousand pounds of pension contributions not yet paid. Together that is eight hundred thousand pounds. At twenty five per cent, the deferred tax asset is two hundred thousand pounds.

The asset is plainly recoverable, because Calder is profitable. The net liability at the year end is one point two million pounds.

At the start of the year, Calder had a liability of one point one million pounds and an asset of one hundred thousand pounds.

So the movement is this. The liability has grown by three hundred thousand pounds, a charge. The asset has grown by one hundred thousand pounds, a credit. The net deferred tax charge for the year is two hundred thousand pounds.

Add the current tax of seven hundred and fifty thousand pounds and the total tax charge is nine hundred and fifty thousand pounds. That answers the opening question.

Where does the expenditure credit go? Calder's four hundred thousand pounds of research and development expenditure credit is taxable income in its computation and, in common practice, is presented above the tax line as other income or as a reduction in research costs. So it sits in profit before tax, and the tax on it sits in current tax. Setting it against the liability is a payment mechanism, not a reduction in the tax charge.

Now the elegant part. In our invented case, Calder's profit before tax is three point eight million pounds, and it has no permanent differences this year. Twenty five per cent of three point eight million pounds is nine hundred and fifty thousand pounds. The total tax charge is exactly twenty five per cent of profit, even though the current tax is only about nineteen point seven per cent of profit. Deferred tax has done its job. Timing differences have dropped out of the reported rate.

Nadia Kerr, Tarnmoor's invented chief financial officer, likes that result. Cash tax is low because of the allowances, the book rate is twenty five per cent, and there are no surprises. The full working is in the reading edition.

Losses and the asset nobody books.

Tax losses carried forward reduce future tax, so in principle they support a deferred tax asset. In practice, losses are where the recognition test bites hardest.

Take Brackenwell Sensors, the invented loss-making sensor developer that Tarnmoor bought on the first of July of Group Year Two. By then its pre-change losses totalled eight point six million pounds. At twenty five per cent, that is a potential asset of two point one five million pounds.

Tarnmoor recognises none of it. The reasons come in two layers. The first is general. A company with a history of losses needs convincing other evidence that profits will arrive, and a venture-funded developer rarely has it. The second is specific to the tax law. After a change in ownership, the law restricts the use of losses that arose before the change, and in Brackenwell's case a major change in the nature or conduct of its trade looks likely. Chapter fourteen explains why those losses may never be usable at all. The accounts carry the asset at nil, and the note discloses the unrecognised losses.

Even for a healthy company, the loss restriction shapes the judgement. Carried forward losses can be set against the first five million pounds of profits each year, the deductions allowance, plus half of profits above it. So a group recognising a loss asset must schedule its forecasts, and the longer the horizon, the less of the asset is probable.

The cleanest route to recognition is the one the May twenty twenty six question tested. Imagine a new company, reporting under international standards, in its first year. Suppose it buys plant for four million pounds, full expenses it, and depreciates it by four hundred thousand pounds in the accounts. Its taxable temporary difference is three point six million pounds, and its deferred tax liability is nine hundred thousand pounds. Suppose it also has a tax loss of two million pounds and an unpaid pension accrual of two hundred thousand pounds. The deductible items total two point two million pounds, a potential asset of five hundred and fifty thousand pounds.

Is the asset probable? Yes, because the reversing liability, in the same company and owed to the same authority, will generate the taxable profit to absorb it. So the company shows a net liability of three hundred and fifty thousand pounds. Being new, it has no opening balances, so its deferred tax charge is the whole three hundred and fifty thousand pounds.

The examiners reported three errors on that question. Candidates treated the temporary difference as accounting loss minus tax loss, asked for brought forward balances for a company that had none, and confused assets with liabilities.

Share schemes, revaluations and other awkward items.

Share schemes come first. Under international standards and Financial Reporting Standard one oh two, an employer charges the fair value of share options to profit over the vesting period. Section ten thirty eight of the two thousand and nine Act denies a deduction for that charge. Instead, Part twelve of the same Act gives a deduction when the employee acquires the shares, measured as their market value less anything the employee pays. So the accounting charge comes early and the tax deduction comes late, in a different amount.

Under I A S twelve, the company recognises a deferred tax asset based on the estimated future deduction, measured using the share price at the balance sheet date. If that estimated deduction exceeds the cumulative accounting charge, the tax effect of the excess goes to equity, not to profit or loss. A rising share price therefore increases the asset, and a falling one shrinks it.

Revaluations come next. If a company revalues land or investment property upwards, the accounts carry the asset above its tax base. Under I A S twelve, investment property carried at fair value is presumed to be recovered through sale, so the deferred tax is measured using the rules that would apply on a sale. The professional guidance on Financial Reporting Standard one oh two describes the same approach for non-depreciable revalued assets and investment property. For a United Kingdom company that means the chargeable gain on sale, after indexation allowance frozen at December twenty seventeen.

What about roll-over relief? The accounting guidance says deferred tax is still provided on a revaluation even where the company expects to roll the gain into a replacement asset, because roll-over only postpones the tax.

Finally, uncertain tax positions. Where a company takes a filing position that H M R C may not accept, the accounting interpretation on uncertainty over income tax treatments, in force for periods beginning on or after the first of January, twenty nineteen, asks whether acceptance is probable, assuming the authority examines the point with full knowledge. If not, the company measures the uncertainty using the most likely amount or an expected value. If the dispute is only about timing, the provision in current tax is matched by a deferred tax asset, and the total charge moves little. Chapter four showed that a provision of this kind can trigger notification under the uncertain tax treatment rules.

Fair values when a company is bought.

When a group buys a company, the consolidated accounts restate the target's assets at fair value at the acquisition date. The target's own tax position does not change at all. A share purchase gives no step-up in the tax base of anything the target owns. So every fair value uplift creates a temporary difference in the group accounts.

Tarnmoor's purchase of Calder shows it. In our invented case, Tarnmoor's acquisition accounting recognised Calder's customer relationships at a fair value of four million pounds. Calder had built those relationships itself, so in Calder's books and in Calder's tax computation they have no value and no tax base. In the group accounts the asset is four million pounds and its tax base is nil. So the group recognises a deferred tax liability of one million pounds at twenty five per cent.

Where does the other side of that entry go? Into goodwill. I A S twelve treats deferred tax arising on a business combination as one of the identifiable liabilities acquired, so it changes the amount of goodwill. Recognising a one million pound liability makes goodwill one million pounds larger than it would otherwise be.

And goodwill itself? No deferred tax. Goodwill is the residual, and deferred tax on it would only increase it again.

After acquisition, Tarnmoor amortises the customer relationships in the group accounts over ten years, in our invented case, four hundred thousand pounds a year. Calder gets no tax deduction for that amortisation, because the asset does not exist in Calder. Without deferred tax, that amortisation would push the group's reported tax rate up every year. With it, the liability unwinds by one hundred thousand pounds a year, a deferred tax credit that exactly offsets the tax effect of the amortisation. The reported rate stays clean.

None of this touches Calder's own tax computation. The liability lives only in Tarnmoor's consolidated accounts.

Contrast an asset purchase, which buys new tax bases under the intangible fixed assets regime, within the limits chapter eleven explains. A share purchase buys the target's history, including its tax bases.

Brackenwell's acquisition raised the mirror image. At the acquisition date the group had to decide whether Brackenwell's losses were recoverable. It concluded that they were not, so no asset was recognised, and goodwill was correspondingly higher.

Explaining the rate.

Every listed group publishes a reconciliation explaining why its tax charge differs from profit multiplied by the applicable rate. I A S twelve, in paragraph eighty one, letter c, requires it in one or both of two forms, a reconciliation of amounts or a reconciliation of rates, and requires the group to explain how it chose the applicable rate. Most United Kingdom groups use the United Kingdom rate of twenty five per cent.

The reconciliation is where deferred tax proves its worth. Timing differences, fully provided at twenty five per cent, do not appear in it at all. What remains are the genuinely different items. Permanent disallowances. Profits taxed abroad at other rates. Unrecognised losses. Rate changes. Prior year adjustments. Top-up taxes.

Here is Tarnmoor's simplified reconciliation for Group Year Two, all invented. Consolidated profit before tax is one hundred million pounds. At twenty five per cent the expected charge is twenty five million pounds.

The Vallarian subsidiary, Tarnmoor Vallaria, earns thirty million pounds taxed at Vallaria's invented rate of twenty per cent. That saves one point five million pounds against the United Kingdom rate. The Marrovian finance company, Tarnmoor Capital, earns three point three million pounds taxed at Marrovia's invented nine per cent, saving five hundred and twenty eight thousand pounds. But the United Kingdom's controlled foreign company charge on Tarnmoor plc for that company adds back one hundred and thirty two thousand pounds.

Expenses that are never deductible, such as fines, customer gifts and capital deal costs, total two million pounds in our simplified version, adding five hundred thousand pounds. The corporate interest restriction disallows two point three million pounds of interest. The disallowed interest can be carried forward and reactivated, so in principle it supports a deferred tax asset, but Tarnmoor judges future capacity too uncertain and recognises none. That adds five hundred and seventy five thousand pounds.

One more line. Tarnmoor plc's surplus management expenses that the controlled foreign company threshold traps, eight hundred and twenty five thousand pounds, carry forward with no asset recognised, as chapter thirteen explains. That adds about two hundred and six thousand pounds.

The total tax charge is about twenty four point four million pounds, an effective rate of about twenty four point three nine per cent. The precise figures are in the reading edition. Notice what is not in the list. Full expensing, unpaid bonuses, pension accruals, the customer relationship amortisation. All timing. All absorbed by deferred tax.

This simplified version leaves out top-up tax under the Pillar Two rules, about sixty six thousand pounds for Group Year Two, which chapter thirty computes. In a real Tarnmoor note, it would be another line.

Tom Hesketh, Tarnmoor's invented head of tax, reviews the reconciliation line by line, because an item nobody can explain usually means an error in the provision.

Pillar Two and the emergency exception.

On the twenty third of May, twenty twenty three, the International Accounting Standards Board did something standard-setters rarely do. It issued an amendment to I A S twelve that applied immediately, and its central feature was a mandatory exception from its own core requirement.

The problem was the global minimum tax. The rules agreed through the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development impose top-up tax where a group's effective rate in a jurisdiction falls below fifteen per cent. Those rules were being written into national law around the world, and the United Kingdom's version is the Pillar Two legislation in the Finance Number Two Act twenty twenty three. Top-up tax depends on jurisdictional effective rates computed under their own rules, with their own deferred tax mechanics. Applying I A S twelve's ordinary deferred tax requirements to it would have been extraordinarily hard, and nobody could say quickly how to do it consistently.

So the Board told companies not to try. Under the exception, an entity neither recognises nor discloses information about deferred tax assets and liabilities related to Pillar Two income taxes. It must say that it has applied the exception. It must disclose its current tax expense related to Pillar Two income taxes separately. And where the legislation is enacted or substantively enacted but not yet in effect, it must disclose known or reasonably estimable information about its exposure, which may be given as a range.

The exception had to be adopted for United Kingdom use. The United Kingdom Endorsement Board adopted the amendments on the nineteenth of July, twenty twenty three. The Financial Reporting Council made equivalent amendments to Financial Reporting Standards one oh two and one oh one in July twenty twenty three, again with a temporary exception and targeted disclosures.

The exception was described as temporary, but no end date was set for it. It is a candid admission by the standard-setters that a new kind of tax had outrun the accounting framework.

For Tarnmoor, with revenue far above seven hundred and fifty million euros, the consequence is simple. Any top-up tax, such as the multinational top-up tax on its low-taxed Marrovian profits, appears in current tax and is disclosed separately. No deferred tax is recognised for it. The rules themselves belong to chapter thirty.

The exception is fair game in a tax accounting tail. A sentence explaining it is worth more than a paragraph on how top-up tax is computed.

The tax function, the board and the examiner.

In a group like Tarnmoor, deferred tax is a quarterly discipline, not a year-end afterthought. The tax function prepares a tax provision for each reporting entity, current and deferred, reconciles it to the returns when they are filed, and books the differences as prior year adjustments. Under the senior accounting officer rules, which chapter four explained, Tarnmoor's chief financial officer must certify each year that its United Kingdom companies have appropriate tax accounting arrangements. A deferred tax balance that nobody can reconcile is a warning sign about those arrangements.

Three conversations recur. The first is the effective tax rate. Boards like a low rate. The tax function's job is to explain that full expensing and the forty per cent allowance lower cash tax, not the rate, and that the rate falls only through permanent items such as overseas rate differences or reliefs that produce no reversal.

The second is loss assets. Recognising Brackenwell's losses would have flattered Tarnmoor's balance sheet by over two million pounds. The tax function's job is to give the auditors an honest forecast, scheduled against the deductions allowance and the change of ownership rules, and to resist pressure to recognise what the law will probably take away.

The third is the rate itself. A change of rate announced in a Budget becomes a remeasurement the moment the bill clears the Commons. A group should model that effect before the vote, not after.

Now the examiner. The paper has asked about deferred tax four times in recent sittings. In May twenty twenty four, a part question on deferred tax on vehicles. In May twenty twenty five, a deferred tax movement after a computation. In November twenty twenty five, the total tax charge and closing balance after a full computation. And in May twenty twenty six, a whole ten mark question on a new company reporting under international standards.

The pattern is clear. Deferred tax usually arrives as the tail of a computation, worth three to five marks, and occasionally as a standalone question. Requirements typically ask you to calculate. Lay out fixed asset differences and short-term differences separately, compute each balance at twenty five per cent, compute the movement, and add current tax to reach the total charge. Exclude land. Include bonuses, pensions and provisions. State why an asset is recognised. And never mix pounds and thousands of pounds in one table.

What to take away.

Deferred tax is an accounting measure, not a tax. H M R C never assesses it.

The total tax charge is current tax plus deferred tax. Deferred tax is the movement in the balances. The examiner wants the charge, not just the balances.

There are two accounting languages. I A S twelve compares carrying amounts with tax bases and finds temporary differences. Financial Reporting Standard one oh two, Section twenty nine, looks for timing differences, plus fair value adjustments on business combinations and revaluations. Permanent differences create no deferred tax in either.

Liabilities are always provided, apart from goodwill and a few other exceptions. Assets are recognised only when recovery is probable, and a reversing liability in the same company is the strongest support. Nothing is discounted.

Measure at enacted or substantively enacted rates. In the United Kingdom that means the bill has cleared the Commons. The main rate of twenty five per cent is enacted for the financial year twenty twenty six, by the Finance Act twenty twenty five, and for the financial year twenty twenty seven, by the Finance Act twenty twenty six.

The fixed asset difference is qualifying net book value less tax written down value, leaving out land. Full expensing creates a liability equal to the tax it saves, so it improves cash, not the effective rate.

Short-term differences usually create assets. Bonuses unpaid nine months after the year end, pension accruals, employee benefit contributions and general provisions.

Calder's numbers, all invented. Qualifying net book value fourteen million pounds, tax written down value eight point four million pounds, liability one point four million pounds. Short-term differences of eight hundred thousand pounds, asset two hundred thousand pounds. Opening liability one point one million pounds and asset one hundred thousand pounds.
